# Chapter 10 rapid review — Airway management, respiration, ventilation, and oxygenation

## Respiratory foundations

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Airway patency, ventilation, oxygenation, and tissue perfusion can fail independently.** An open airway permits airflow. Effective ventilation brings fresh gas to alveoli and clears CO₂. Oxygenation depends on transfer into blood. Tissue delivery then depends on the oxygen content of blood and how much blood reaches the tissues.

- **Airway and lungs.** The nose warms, moistens, and filters air. Air travels through the pharynx and larynx into the trachea, then through bronchi and bronchioles to the alveoli. The epiglottis and vocal cords help protect the lower airway. Loss of upper-airway tone can let the tongue obstruct the pharynx. Bronchiolar constriction and mucosal swelling increase resistance and work of breathing. Sustained effort can lead to fatigue.
- **Mechanics.** The diaphragm and external intercostals enlarge the thorax during inspiration. Pleural coupling transmits expansion to the lungs, lowering alveolar pressure below atmospheric pressure. Relaxation and elastic recoil drive quiet expiration. Obstruction and air trapping can make expiration active. Air in the pleural space can disrupt coupling and permit lung collapse.
- **Gas exchange and metabolism.** External respiration exchanges O₂ and CO₂ across the alveolar–capillary interface. Hypoxemia refers to low arterial oxygen tension, while tissue hypoxia reflects inadequate oxygen at the cells. Hemoglobin carries most blood oxygen. Internal respiration exchanges gases between systemic capillaries and cells. Aerobic metabolism uses oxygen and glucose to produce ATP, releasing CO₂ and water. Inadequate delivery promotes anaerobic metabolism and lactate accumulation.
- **Failure mechanisms.** CNS depression, neuromuscular weakness, or chest injury can impair ventilation. Pulmonary edema, pneumonia, and ventilation–perfusion mismatch can impair gas exchange. Hemorrhage can reduce tissue delivery despite a reassuring SpO₂. Anemia and carbon monoxide exposure can reduce oxygen content or delivery despite preserved arterial oxygen tension. [^als-model][^als-co]
- **Respiratory drive.** Brainstem centers regulate rate and depth. Central chemoreceptors respond to CO₂-related pH changes, while peripheral chemoreceptors respond to low arterial oxygen. In susceptible COPD, excess oxygen can raise arterial CO₂ by worsening ventilation–perfusion matching and reducing how much CO₂ oxygenated hemoglobin carries, known as the Haldane effect. Reduced ventilatory drive can contribute. The older “hypoxic drive” explanation is incomplete. [^als-copd-physiology]
- **Age and reserve.** Children have smaller airways and greater oxygen demand relative to reserve. Their more compliant chest wall makes gentle ventilation especially important. In older adults, reduced reserve, limited neck mobility, poor mask fit, impaired cooperation, or aspiration risk can complicate airway care.

## Airway assessment and BLS airway management

A clear voice and airflow support **current patency**. Handling of secretions, cough, mental status, and recurrent obstruction help assess **airway protection**. Aspiration can occur even when gag or cough is present.

| Finding | Likely mechanism or concern |
| --- | --- |
| Snoring | Tongue or relaxed pharyngeal tissue obstructing airflow |
| Gurgling | Blood, vomit, or secretions in the airway |
| Stridor or crowing | Upper-airway narrowing or laryngeal spasm |
| Expiratory wheeze | Lower-airway narrowing, including possible bronchospasm |
| Progressive hoarseness, swelling, or repeated emesis | Threat to sustained patency or airway protection |

**Positioning.** Head-tilt/chin-lift moves soft tissue forward when spinal injury is not suspected. Jaw thrust advances the mandible while limiting cervical movement when spinal injury is suspected. Lift the bony jaw and avoid pressure on tissue beneath the chin. A lateral recovery position helps drainage when breathing is adequate and spinal injury is not suspected. Positive-pressure ventilation and spinal precautions change positioning priorities.

**Suction.** A rigid Yankauer works for visible oral contamination. A soft catheter fits narrower passages when appropriate. Measure depth, insert without suction when feasible, and suction on withdrawal. Use brief passes and the lowest effective vacuum. Deep or prolonged suction can cause tissue injury, gagging, vagal bradycardia, and interrupted oxygenation. Thick material or a solid obstruction may require another method of visible removal. Avoid blind sweeps. The outline’s prolonged suction and preoxygenation cycles are examples. Its residual-volume explanation for desaturation remains uncertain. Interruption of ventilation and oxygenation is a related concern. [^als-model][^als-airway]

- **OPA.** Holds the tongue forward in a deeply unresponsive patient without gag or cough. An intact reflex increases the risk of vomiting or laryngospasm. Incorrect size or placement can worsen obstruction.
- **NPA.** May help with clenched teeth or limited OPA tolerance. Avoid in suspected basilar skull fracture or severe facial trauma. Never force it against resistance. Reassess for bleeding and tolerance.
- **Both adjuncts** require continued positioning and reassessment. Neither protects against aspiration. [^als-model]

> [!NOTE]
> **ALS connection.** Report which position, suction technique, or mask seal restored airflow and whether the effect lasted. Recurrent emesis, swelling, or unsustainable BVM can prompt advanced airway consideration. A supraglottic airway provides a ventilation route with less aspiration protection than a cuffed tracheal tube. Placement attempts can interrupt ventilation and worsen hypoxemia or hypotension. Continue effective basic support during the transition. [^als-airway][^als-sga]

**Medication connection.** Rapid hoarseness or stridor with swelling after a plausible exposure raises concern for anaphylaxis. Epinephrine can reduce edema by vasoconstriction and relax bronchial smooth muscle. Albuterol relaxes bronchial smooth muscle through beta₂ activity and does not treat laryngeal edema or shock. Route, protocols, and credentialing govern administration. [^als-model][^als-scope]

## Breathing assessment and monitoring

Examine **rate, rhythm, effort, depth, and airflow**. Look for chest rise and symmetry, listen and feel for air movement, then compare bilateral breath sounds. Retractions, accessory muscle use, nasal flaring, wheeze, crackles, and unequal sounds inform assessment. Asymmetric or paradoxical movement can reflect chest-wall injury. Interpret rate in context and by age.

- **Distress:** Work of breathing is increased while ventilation may still be adequate. Tachypnea alone does not establish a need for assisted breaths.
- **Failure:** Inadequate rate or depth limits effective ventilation. Falling effort with shallow chest excursion, poor air entry, or declining responsiveness can signal fatigue. Cyanosis may appear late or be less evident with low hemoglobin.
- **Arrest:** In an unresponsive patient with absent normal breathing or only gasps, check for a definite pulse within 10 seconds. If none is definitely felt, start CPR and use the AED as soon as available. If a definite pulse is present with ineffective breathing, provide ventilatory support and reassess circulation. An organized monitor rhythm does not establish a pulse. [^als-adult-bls][^als-pediatric-bls]

**Ventilation math**

- **Minute ventilation** = tidal volume × respiratory rate.
- **Alveolar ventilation** ≈ (tidal volume − dead space per breath) × respiratory rate.
- At **42 breaths/min** and **200 mL/breath**, minute ventilation is **8.4 L/min**. With **150 mL** of dead space per breath, estimated alveolar ventilation is only **2.1 L/min**. Rapid shallow breathing can move plenty of gas through the conducting airways while providing little fresh gas for exchange.

**Clinical example.** Sudden severe headache followed by declining consciousness, posturing, and snoring suggests a possible intracranial emergency with an airway problem. In the source case, shallow breathing at **5–6/min** and **SpO₂ 79%** support prompt airway positioning and effective assisted breaths with oxygen. Report last known well, clinical progression, and response before and after treatment. The pattern does not establish the cause. [^als-stroke][^als-model][^als-airway]

**ALS monitoring.** SpO₂ estimates hemoglobin saturation and may improve with oxygen while CO₂ rises from ineffective breathing. Motion, cool extremities, poor perfusion, and skin pigmentation can affect oximetry. Waveform capnography displays exhaled CO₂ and reports **ETCO₂** at end expiration. Ventilation, pulmonary blood flow, and sample quality all influence it. Shock can lower ETCO₂ even when ventilation is inadequate. Compare the waveform with chest rise, pulse, BP, and any leak or disconnection. A sustained waveform supports confirmation and ongoing monitoring of tracheal tube placement. In compatible dyspnea with chest symptoms, an authorized clinician may use a 12-lead ECG to evaluate ischemia and inform destination or notification. [^als-fda][^als-model][^als-airway][^als-acs]

## Assisted ventilation and noninvasive support

**Manual ventilation.** Oxygen alone cannot move gas into alveoli when breathing is ineffective. With a BVM or pocket mask, open the airway, establish a seal, and deliver just enough volume for visible chest rise. In two-person BVM, one clinician maintains the airway and holds a two-handed seal while the other delivers breaths. If the chest does not rise, check position, seal, obstruction, and equipment before delivering more volume. Use adjuncts only when indicated and tolerated. Attach oxygen as soon as practicable while keeping ventilation effective. A pocket mask with a one-way valve provides a barrier. If mouth-to-mouth or mouth-to-nose ventilation is used, the outline calls for a barrier and reserves the nasal route for situations such as inability to open or seal the mouth. [^als-adult-bls][^als-manual]

**Pressure and perfusion.** Spontaneous inhalation lowers intrathoracic pressure. PPV pushes air in and can reduce venous return, preload, and cardiac output. Excessive rate or volume can worsen air trapping, impair perfusion during CPR, and inflate the stomach, increasing regurgitation and aspiration risk. If BP falls after PPV, compare chest rise, breath sounds, pulse, and equipment findings before assuming the cause. In suspected severe traumatic brain injury (TBI), avoid hypoxemia, hypotension, and unnecessary hyperventilation. [^als-airway][^als-tbi]

### Resuscitation rates in the cited 2025 guidance

| Clinical context | Ventilation rate |
| --- | --- |
| Adult with pulse and absent or inadequate breathing | **10 breaths/min** |
| Adult CPR with an advanced airway | **10 breaths/min** with continuous compressions |
| Infant or child with pulse and inadequate breathing, or during CPR with an advanced airway | **20–30 breaths/min** |
| Newborn resuscitation at birth | Separate algorithm, **30–60 inflations/min** when ventilation is indicated |

Before an advanced airway, coordinate breaths and compressions by age and rescuer count. The outline lists **30:2** for adults and for a lone rescuer with an infant or child, and **15:2** for two rescuers with an infant or child. Resuscitation at birth follows its separate algorithm. The outline includes older rates for comparison. Bag capacity differs from delivered volume, and the source’s overlapping newborn bag-size recommendations require age- and device-specific verification. [^als-adult-bls][^als-adult-als][^als-pediatric-bls][^als-neonatal]

### CPAP, bilevel support, high flow, and transport ventilators

- **CPAP** maintains one pressure throughout spontaneous breathing. It can recruit poorly aerated lung units, reduce work of breathing, and lower left-ventricular afterload in cardiogenic pulmonary edema.
- **Bilevel NIV** adds inspiratory pressure support above expiratory pressure. This can unload respiratory muscles and improve ventilation. The 2022 NAEMSP position includes both NIV approaches for selected patients. ERS/ATS prehospital guidance for cardiogenic pulmonary edema is conditional and low certainty. Field evidence does not establish that one mode is superior. [^als-niv][^als-ers-niv]
- **HFNC** delivers heated, humidified high-flow oxygen. It cannot substitute for ventilation in apnea or inadequate breathing. ERS guidance favors trying NIV first in adults with hypercapnic COPD and acute respiratory failure, based mainly on hospital evidence. Hypercapnia alone is an overly broad exclusion for high-flow oxygen. [^als-high-flow]
- **Transport ventilators** depend on device capability, the patient’s physiology, and the gas or power supply. High-pressure alarms can indicate obstruction, increased resistance, or reduced compliance. Low-pressure alarms can indicate leak or disconnection. Check the patient and circuit, and keep backup BVM and oxygen available. Settings and alarm handling require training and authorization. [^als-ventilator][^als-airway]

> [!CAUTION]
> **NIV depends on spontaneous breathing and airway protection.** Apnea, active vomiting, loss of airway protection, or hemodynamic instability make NIV unsuitable or warrant immediate reassessment. Facial injury or suspected pneumothorax may also preclude it. Use local criteria and device settings. Trend alertness, effort, air entry, BP, tolerance, and SpO₂. ETCO₂ has no fixed trajectory during NIV, especially with mask leak. [^als-niv][^als-model]

## Altered airways, facial trauma, and choking

- **Tracheostomy or laryngectomy.** A tracheostomy may retain continuity with the upper airway. A **total laryngectomy** disconnects the lungs from the mouth and nose. Ventilation must enter the stoma. If anatomy is uncertain, the cited guidance uses initial oxygen to face and stoma while identifying the route. Use chest rise to judge effectiveness. Absent airflow or inability to pass a suction catheter raises concern for blockage or displacement. Positive pressure through a displaced tube can inflate soft tissue. Confirm tube type and baseline needs with the caregiver or airway card. Tube exchange and rescue procedures require specific authorization. [^als-ntsp]
- **Facial trauma and dentures.** Swelling can threaten patency, bleeding may repeatedly contaminate the airway, and facial injury can impair a mask seal. Avoid an NPA in severe midface trauma. Secure full dentures can help the seal. Loose dentures or visible fragments can obstruct.

> [!WARNING]
> **Severe choking in the cited 2025 guidance.** For a responsive adult or child, alternate **five back blows** with **five abdominal thrusts**. Use chest thrusts in a responsive adult in late pregnancy or when the abdomen cannot be encircled. For a responsive infant, alternate five back blows with five heel-of-one-hand chest thrusts. If unresponsive, begin CPR with compressions and remove only a visible object when opening the airway for breaths. Avoid blind sweeps. The outline’s older thrust-only, breath-first, and two-finger infant techniques are superseded. [^als-adult-bls][^als-pediatric-bls]

An effective cough with air movement supports encouraging cough and monitoring for deterioration. After relief, reassess breathing and pulse. Persistent symptoms may reflect remaining obstruction or aspiration. Authorized ALS clinicians may consider direct visualization and forceps removal for persistent visible obstruction. [^als-adult-bls][^als-pediatric-airway][^als-model]

## Oxygen therapy

Select oxygen according to the patient’s **oxygenation, ventilation, perfusion, illness or injury, and local targets**. Treat hypoxemia promptly. Inadequate ventilation requires effective breaths even when supplemental oxygen improves saturation. Excess oxygen can contribute to oxidative injury in ischemia and reperfusion. [^als-model][^als-acs][^als-stroke]

### Condition-specific examples

- **Suspected COPD exacerbation.** The cited prehospital trial used **88–92%** and found lower mortality with titrated oxygen than routine high-flow oxygen. Correct hypoxemia promptly and titrate per protocol. A better SpO₂ with worsening drowsiness or chest rise warrants reassessment for ventilatory failure. [^als-copd-trial][^als-copd-physiology]
- **Acute coronary syndrome.** The 2025 multisociety guideline recommends oxygen for confirmed hypoxia below **90%** and advises against routine oxygen at or above **90%**. [^als-acs]
- **Acute ischemic stroke.** The cited 2026 AHA/ASA guideline gives a goal **above 94%** for hypoxic patients. Reduced consciousness or bulbar dysfunction can require airway and ventilation support. [^als-stroke]
- **Trauma and shock.** SpO₂ can appear reassuring despite poor oxygen delivery. Assess perfusion and bleeding together with oxygenation. The Brain Trauma Foundation recommends continuous oxygen supplementation and prompt correction of hypoxemia in suspected **severe TBI**. Apply local condition-specific targets for other trauma. [^als-model][^als-tbi]
- **Carbon monoxide.** A standard two-wavelength pulse oximeter can be falsely reassuring. Suspected exposure warrants oxygen per protocol. Report the exposure, other symptomatic people, syncope, confusion, and pregnancy. Blood co-oximetry and specialty consultation may help guide further care. [^als-co][^als-model]
- **Pregnancy.** ACOG’s 2025 intrapartum guidance advises against routine maternal oxygen for abnormal fetal heart rate patterns when the mother is adequately oxygenated. In prehospital maternal illness or trauma, assess breathing, oxygenation, perfusion, and exposures under the relevant EMS protocol. [^als-obstetric][^als-model]

### Delivery and supply

| Device | Distinguishing feature |
| --- | --- |
| Nasal cannula | Low flow mixes with room air. The outline gives **1–6 L/min** as a typical range. |
| Nonrebreather | Valves and an inflated reservoir support high-concentration delivery. Fill before placement and verify the reservoir stays inflated during inhalation. |
| Simple or partial rebreather mask | A simple mask has no reservoir. A partial rebreather retains some exhaled gas. Adequate flow limits unintended rebreathing. |
| Venturi mask | A selected entrainment adapter and manufacturer-specified flow provide a controlled concentration. |
| Tracheostomy mask | Delivers oxygen at the neck with less tubing traction than a T-tube and room for secretions to drain. |

**Cylinder planning.** Estimated duration in minutes = **(gauge pressure − reserve pressure) × cylinder factor ÷ flow**. Use psi for pressures, the appropriate cylinder factor, and L/min for flow. An E cylinder at **2,000 psi**, with **200 psi** reserve and factor **0.28**, lasts about **33.6 minutes at 15 L/min** in the constant-flow example. Use agency reserve policy and account for leaks, ventilator driving gas, variable demand, and transfer delays. A high-pressure regulator powers a compatible device. A therapy regulator controls patient flow. Humidification may help during prolonged delivery of dry oxygen. [^als-ventilator][^als-niv]

> [!CAUTION]
> Secure cylinders, use compatible regulators, and keep oxygen equipment clear of ignition sources, oil, and grease. The source gives different valve-opening instructions and includes briefly opening an unconnected cylinder. Verify device instructions and agency policy. During source transfer, avoid leaving a nonflowing reservoir mask on the face. Verify flow before reapplying it and reassess the patient.

## Pediatric care and mixed-level teamwork

**Pediatric airway and ventilation.** A large occiput can flex the neck when the infant is supine. Use age-appropriate alignment and shoulder padding when needed. Avoid overextension and soft-tissue pressure under the chin. Fit the mask and adjuncts to the child. Use brief suction passes because oxygen reserve is limited and vagal stimulation can cause bradycardia. The pediatric section gives **3–5 seconds per pass** as its example. Match timing and catheter size to the patient and applicable protocol. An OPA requires absent gag and is inserted in its normal orientation without rotation in infants and children. Narrow nares and enlarged adenoids can limit NPA use, and possible head injury requires caution.

**Watch the trend.** Retractions may be pronounced early. Falling effort with shallow chest movement, poor tone, reduced alertness, weak air entry, or bradycardia raises concern for decompensation. Use gentle breaths to visible chest rise when ventilation is inadequate. Known baseline and condition-specific protocols guide oxygen targets. Blow-by may be an option for a spontaneously breathing child who cannot tolerate a mask. Persistent cyanosis or altered alertness despite oxygen requires reassessing ventilation, perfusion, and reading reliability. Give ALS or the receiving facility the weight if known or estimated, lowest reliable SpO₂, device used, changes in work of breathing, and what improved chest rise. Destination decisions depend on condition, receiving capability, and protocol. [^als-pediatric-bls][^als-pediatric-airway][^als-model]

**ALS connection.** Effective BVM may remain the best option in transport, especially in children. NAEMSP finds the benefit of prehospital pediatric intubation unclear and stresses pediatric-specific competency when systems use it. If a patient found unresponsive in vomit has shallow breaths and later vomits again, keep airway clearance and assisted ventilation effective while ALS evaluates possible causes and the need for a more sustainable airway. Report what changed after positioning, suction, or ventilation and flag recurrent contamination and aspiration risk at handoff. Alcohol exposure leaves other causes open. Authorized naloxone can restore breathing in opioid toxicity, with ventilation and observation still needed. [^als-pediatric-airway][^als-adult-bls][^als-model][^als-airway]

**Scope.** The national model places positioning, suction, basic adjuncts, oxygen, BVM, and CPAP at EMT level. It adds selected medications, IV/IO access, supraglottic airways, and waveform capnography at AEMT level. Endotracheal intubation is generally paramedic-level. State law, licensure, training, credentialing, agency protocols, and medical direction govern actual authorization. Some states permit expanded EMT functions with oversight. Equipment preparation and data acquisition are distinct from advanced interpretation and performing interventions. [^als-scope][^als-model]

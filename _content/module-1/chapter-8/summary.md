# Chapter 8: Pathophysiology — Rapid Review (TL;DR)

## Cellular metabolism and perfusion

Emergency care aims to sustain or restore perfusion: delivering oxygen, glucose and other essential substances to cells while removing metabolic waste. Failure of respiration, circulation or cellular metabolism can ultimately cause organ failure.

- **Aerobic metabolism:** Glycolysis in the cytosol converts glucose into pyruvate and a small amount of ATP without requiring oxygen. With oxygen available as the terminal electron acceptor, mitochondrial pathways generate much more ATP. Heat, CO₂ and water are byproducts.
- **Anaerobic metabolism:** When oxidative metabolism is inadequate, conversion of pyruvate to lactate permits glycolysis to continue but provides far less ATP. Energy depletion and acidosis contribute to cellular dysfunction and death. Lactate can also rise despite oxygen availability; it does not independently prove hypoxia. [S6](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/24-2-carbohydrate-metabolism) [S7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26378980/)
- **Sodium–potassium pump:** ATP powers movement of three Na⁺ out and two K⁺ into the cell against their concentration gradients, maintaining electrical and cellular function. ATP depletion impairs this pump → intracellular sodium accumulates → water follows → swelling and possible cell death. [S8](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology/pages/3-1-the-cell-membrane)

### The physiological chain of perfusion

The chapter identifies eleven interdependent components:

| Component group | Required links |
| --- | --- |
| Air supply and breathing | Ambient air composition, airway patency, ventilation mechanics, ventilation regulation |
| Exchange and transport | Ventilation/perfusion ratio, blood transport of O₂ and CO₂ |
| Circulation | Blood volume, myocardial pump function, systemic vascular resistance, microcirculation, blood pressure |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Identify the failing link rather than treating an isolated vital sign. An open airway does not guarantee ventilation; normal SpO₂ does not guarantee oxygen delivery; adequate BP does not guarantee tissue perfusion.

EMTs prioritize scene safety, airway management, oxygenation and ventilation, hemorrhage control, reassessment and transport. AEMTs and paramedics may add advanced airways, monitoring, vascular access, medications and electrical therapies according to their respective authorization. Request additional resources early without delaying essential BLS care. [S1](https://nasemso.org/content.aspx?club_id=157064&module_id=701418&page_id=22) [S3](https://nasemso.org/content.aspx?module_id=701974&page_id=22)

## Respiration and ventilation

### Ambient air and airway patency

At sea level, atmospheric pressure is approximately **760 mmHg**; air contains approximately 21% oxygen (partial pressure 159.2 mmHg) and 78% nitrogen. FiO₂ describes inspired oxygen fraction (room air = 0.21); FDO₂ describes oxygen delivered by a ventilation device. Actual delivered concentrations depend on flow, fit, leaks and patient demand.

| Exposure | Mechanism of hypoxia |
| --- | --- |
| Oxygen-displacing gases | Reduce available inspired oxygen |
| Carbon monoxide | Interferes with hemoglobin oxygen transport |
| Cyanide | Prevents effective cellular oxygen utilization |

Never enter an unsafe atmosphere. Suspected CO poisoning warrants appropriate oxygen despite potentially misleading SpO₂. **ALS connection:** Blood co-oximetry can identify COHb; authorized clinicians may consider hydroxocobalamin for compatible severe cyanide exposure. [S3](https://nasemso.org/content.aspx?module_id=701974&page_id=22) [S9](https://www.cdc.gov/carbon-monoxide/hcp/clinical-guidance/index.html)

Air passes through the nasal/oral passages and pharynx to the larynx, trachea, bronchi and bronchioles. Tongue relaxation, foreign bodies, secretions, blood, vomiting and edema can obstruct the upper airway; aspirated material can impair alveolar gas exchange. Laryngospasm closes the vocal cords. The trachea divides at the carina into mainstem bronchi; distal bronchioles contain smooth muscle without cartilaginous rings.

- **Bronchitis:** Inflamed mucosa narrows the airway and increases resistance.
- **Asthma:** Inflammation and bronchoconstriction impede airflow, especially exhalation, producing wheezing, prolonged expiration and potentially hypoxia or CO₂ retention. A quiet chest with deteriorating air movement is dangerous.
- **Pediatric airway:** Infants preferentially breathe through the nose; smaller airway caliber magnifies the effect of swelling. The traditional claim that the cricoid is always the narrowest point in children under eight is oversimplified. [S11](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33179415/)

**ALS connection:** Airway patency differs from airway protection. Supraglottic devices sit above the glottis; endotracheal tubes pass through it. Advanced placement can interrupt oxygen delivery or cause apnea/hypotension; waveform capnography supports confirmation. Albuterol relaxes bronchial smooth muscle through beta-2 stimulation; ipratropium reduces cholinergic bronchoconstriction. Steroids act more slowly; epinephrine addresses anaphylactic edema, bronchospasm and distributive shock. Interventions remain protocol- and scope-dependent. [S1](https://nasemso.org/content.aspx?club_id=157064&module_id=701418&page_id=22) [S3](https://nasemso.org/content.aspx?module_id=701974&page_id=22) [S5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35001819/)

### Mechanics of breathing

**Boyle's law:** Gas pressure varies inversely with volume. During active inhalation, the diaphragm descends (approximately 60–70% of inspiratory effort) and external intercostals expand the thorax, lowering pressure. Normal exhalation is passive through muscle relaxation and elastic recoil.

Accessory inspiratory muscles (sternocleidomastoid, scalenes, pectoralis minor) expand the thorax; abdominal muscles and internal intercostals assist forced exhalation. Increased work consumes ATP and can progress to fatigue and respiratory failure.

- **Compliance:** Ability of lungs/chest wall to expand; pneumonia and pulmonary edema can impair expansion.
- **Airway resistance:** Opposition to airflow; edema, mucus and bronchoconstriction increase it.
- **Pleural integrity:** Negative intrapleural pressure and pleural fluid couple lungs to the chest wall. Air entering the pleural space disrupts this coupling, allowing lung collapse.

> [!WARNING]
> Tension pneumothorax compromises ventilation and venous return, potentially causing obstructive shock. The source's 2024 NAEMSP guidance allows consideration of a vented seal for spontaneously breathing patients with open pneumothorax but advises against seals during positive-pressure ventilation (PPV). Follow local protocols; report deterioration and suspected tension physiology promptly. Pleural decompression is an authorized advanced intervention, not indicated for every pneumothorax. [S14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39499620/)

### Minute versus alveolar ventilation

- **Minute ventilation** = tidal volume × respiratory rate.
- **Alveolar ventilation** = (tidal volume − dead space) × respiratory rate.
- Anatomical dead space is the conducting airway volume that does not participate in gas exchange (approximately 150 mL in an average adult).

| Breathing pattern | Minute ventilation | Alveolar ventilation |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 500 mL × 12/min | 6 L/min | 4.2 L/min |
| 200 mL × 28/min | 5.6 L/min | 1.4 L/min |

Rapid, shallow breathing may therefore maintain near-normal minute ventilation while severely reducing effective gas exchange. Inadequate alveolar ventilation causes CO₂ retention and may impair oxygenation. Rate and visible chest rise alone do not establish adequacy; assess depth, effort, mentation and trends. Provide appropriate assisted ventilation when inadequate; oxygen alone cannot correct hypoventilation. [S10](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35001826/)

**ALS connection — Positive pressure:** PPV increases intrathoracic pressure and can reduce venous return, particularly in hypovolemia. Excessive ventilation or incomplete exhalation may worsen air trapping and hypotension. CPAP/PEEP can improve alveolar recruitment; noninvasive ventilation requires appropriate selection and monitoring. Inadequate spontaneous breathing, inability to protect the airway, vomiting or severe hemodynamic instability may make it unsuitable. Report changing chest rise, resistance, BP and mental status. [S12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35001824/) [S13](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35001825/)

### Regulation of ventilation

| Regulator | Function |
| --- | --- |
| Central chemoreceptors (medulla) | Increased CO₂ lowers CSF pH, stimulating faster/deeper breathing |
| Peripheral chemoreceptors (carotid/aortic bodies) | Respond principally to significant arterial O₂ decline, also CO₂/pH |
| Irritant receptors | Cough, bronchoconstriction, increased rate |
| Stretch receptors | Limit excessive inflation |
| J-receptors | Increased pulmonary capillary pressure promotes rapid, shallow breathing |

Medullary ventral and dorsal respiratory groups coordinate respiratory motor activity and sensory input; pontine centers help regulate transitions between inspiration and expiration. The source's description of the VRG as the sole motor impulse source is a simplified model, not established here as exclusive.

> [!CAUTION]
> COPD does not universally switch breathing control to a purely hypoxic drive. Excess oxygen can worsen hypercapnia through V/Q changes and the Haldane effect, among other mechanisms. A common target for patients at risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure is SpO₂ 88–92%, subject to presentation and protocol. Never withhold necessary oxygen or ventilation from a critically hypoxemic or failing patient. [S15](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3682248/) [S16](https://www.bmj.com/content/341/bmj.c5462)

**ALS connection:** Opioids suppress ventilatory drive; naloxone can restore it, but ventilatory support remains the priority and depression may recur. Rapid/deep breathing in metabolic acidosis may be compensatory; suppressing it without adequate replacement ventilation can worsen acidosis. [S3](https://nasemso.org/content.aspx?module_id=701974&page_id=22) [S5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35001819/)

## Gas exchange and oxygen delivery

External respiration exchanges gases between alveoli and pulmonary capillaries; internal respiration exchanges gases between systemic capillaries and cells. Gases diffuse down partial-pressure gradients. **V/Q** is alveolar ventilation divided by pulmonary blood flow. Both are greater at the lung bases, but perfusion increases more; apices have relatively more ventilation than perfusion. Overall normal V/Q is below 1.

| Disturbance | Mechanism and implication |
| --- | --- |
| Low V/Q | Perfusion exceeds ventilation (bronchospasm, edema); extreme = shunt physiology |
| High V/Q | Ventilation exceeds perfusion (pulmonary embolism, reduced pulmonary flow); extreme = dead space |
| Hemorrhage | Reduced circulation and oxygen-carrying capacity compromise global oxygen delivery, even if pulmonary gas exchange persists |

Oxygen supports oxygenation, bronchodilators address bronchospasm, and hemorrhage control addresses blood loss; these are not interchangeable treatments.

- **O₂ transport:** 97–98.5% bound to hemoglobin, 1.5–3% dissolved in plasma. Hemoglobin has four oxygen-binding sites.
- **CO₂ transport:** Approximately 70% bicarbonate, 23% hemoglobin-bound and 7% dissolved in plasma.
- **Oxygen delivery** = cardiac output × arterial oxygen content (compatible units). Oxygen content depends largely on hemoglobin concentration and saturation.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> A normal SpO₂ can coexist with severe anemia, hemorrhage or CO poisoning. Oxygen does not replace missing hemoglobin or blood flow. Administer oxygen according to clinical indications and protocol, not solely because anemia is suspected. [S4](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK538336/) [S9](https://www.cdc.gov/carbon-monoxide/hcp/clinical-guidance/index.html)

**ALS connection — Monitoring:** Pulse oximetry estimates saturation, not ventilation or flow. Waveform capnography measures exhaled CO₂ (EtCO₂ at end-expiration); readings reflect ventilation, pulmonary flow, metabolism and sampling quality. Falling EtCO₂ during shock may reflect declining flow, not improved ventilation. Neither capnography nor ECG independently establishes tissue perfusion or diagnoses pulmonary embolism. Lactate is a supplemental severity marker, not proof of hypoxia or an automatic indication for fluids. [S5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35001819/) [S7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26378980/)

## Circulation and hemodynamics

### Blood volume and fluid movement

Adult blood volume is approximately **70 mL/kg** (4.9 L in a 70 kg adult). Plasma constitutes about 55% and formed elements 45%. Plasma contains water, albumin, antibodies and clotting factors; RBCs transport oxygen, WBCs support immunity and platelets support clotting. Veins contain approximately 64% of blood volume and function as a reservoir.

Hydrostatic pressure pushes fluid out of capillaries; albumin-generated oncotic pressure opposes filtration. Elevated pulmonary capillary hydrostatic pressure in left ventricular failure can cause pulmonary edema. Low albumin can contribute to edema without proving intravascular depletion; permeability and lymphatic drainage also influence fluid balance. [S19](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology/pages/26-1-body-fluids-and-fluid-compartments)

**ALS connection — Cause-specific resuscitation:** Crystalloid expands fluid volume transiently but cannot replace red cells or clotting factors; excessive administration can worsen dilution, hypothermia and edema. NAEMSP's 2025 statement favors blood components for life-threatening traumatic bleeding in equipped systems; isotonic crystalloids are preferred when crystalloid is used, with targets dependent on injury (including suspected TBI). Cardiogenic pulmonary edema may instead benefit from positive airway pressure or nitroglycerin when indicated, BP permits and contraindications (including recent PDE-5 inhibitor use) are excluded. [S3](https://nasemso.org/content.aspx?module_id=701974&page_id=22) [S17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40131241/) [S18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39576138/)

### Cardiac output

**CO = HR × SV**; resting adult cardiac output is approximately 5 L/min. The SA node generates rhythm; sympathetic stimulation and catecholamines increase rate and contractility, while parasympathetic activity slows rate. Excessive tachycardia can shorten filling time and decrease output; no single rate establishes this.

| Stroke volume determinant | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Preload | End-diastolic myocardial stretch influenced by venous return; Frank–Starling: increased stretch strengthens contraction within limits |
| Contractility | Force of contraction; reduced by myocardial injury, heart failure or beta-1 blockade |
| Afterload | Load opposing ventricular ejection; excessive load increases cardiac workload |

Additional fluid is not always beneficial; preload is not simply pressure, and afterload is not simply diastolic BP. [S21](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/20-2-blood-flow-blood-pressure-and-resistance)

**ALS connection:** Electrical activity does not prove mechanical output. Selected poorly perfusing bradyarrhythmias may require atropine or pacing; selected unstable tachyarrhythmias may require synchronized cardioversion. Compensatory sinus tachycardia requires treating its cause, not reflexively slowing the heart. Electrical capture does not establish mechanical capture; organized rhythm without a pulse may be PEA. CPR and appropriate AED use remain foundational in arrest. [S20](https://cpr.heart.org/en/resuscitation-science/cpr-and-ecc-guidelines/adult-advanced-life-support)

### Vascular resistance, microcirculation and blood pressure

**SVR** is resistance to systemic blood flow. Sympathetic alpha-1 vasoconstriction raises resistance and helps maintain pressure, potentially sacrificing peripheral perfusion; vasodilation lowers resistance. Most systemic vasodilation is not directly mediated by parasympathetic nerves. Diastolic BP is not a direct SVR measurement.

Microcirculation routes blood from arterioles through capillary exchange beds to venules. Precapillary sphincters regulate local flow; temperature, hypoxia, acidosis, histamine, sympathetic tone and hormones influence distribution. Sepsis may impair microcirculation despite improved systemic pressure. [S23](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/20-4-homeostatic-regulation-of-the-vascular-system)

- **Pulse pressure (PP)** = systolic BP − diastolic BP. The chapter uses PP below 25% of systolic BP as a narrow-pulse-pressure example, not a diagnostic threshold for hemorrhage.
- **MAP ≈ CO × SVR** when right atrial pressure is small and units are compatible. At ordinary heart rates, MAP ≈ DBP + PP/3; marked tachycardia or abnormal waveforms reduce accuracy.
- **Baroreceptors** in carotid sinuses and aortic arch sense arterial stretch. Falling BP reduces receptor firing → sympathetic activity increases → tachycardia, stronger contraction, vasoconstriction and increased venous return. Rising BP reverses this response. Adrenal catecholamines sustain compensation.
- **Chemoreceptor reflexes:** Significant hypoxemia, increased CO₂ and decreased pH can stimulate cardiovascular compensation, contributing to tachycardia and cool, pale skin.

**ALS connection:** Norepinephrine increases vascular tone in selected distributive shock states but cannot replace blood or hemorrhage control. Excessive vasoconstriction, arrhythmia and extravasation are risks. Reassess clinical perfusion rather than targeting a number alone. [S3](https://nasemso.org/content.aspx?module_id=701974&page_id=22) [S21](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/20-2-blood-flow-blood-pressure-and-resistance) [S22](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39693581/)

## Clinical application: hemorrhage and compensated shock

Blood loss → reduced venous return/preload → reduced SV and CO → sympathetic compensation → tachycardia and vasoconstriction → narrow pulse pressure, weak pulses and cool, pale, clammy skin. Compensation can conceal deterioration before hypotension becomes obvious; older patients or those taking beta blockers may not develop expected tachycardia.

- **Suspected internal hemorrhage:** Penetrating abdominal injury, rigidity/tenderness, HR 122/min and BP 102/88 mmHg (PP 14 mmHg) illustrate potentially severe shock despite preserved systolic BP. Cover wounds appropriately; internal bleeding requires definitive hospital control.
- **Life-threatening external hemorrhage:** Spurting extremity bleeding, poor responsiveness, absent radial pulse and BP 98/76 mmHg (PP 22 mmHg) illustrate inadequate perfusion. Prompt effective hemorrhage control, including direct pressure and an appropriate tourniquet when indicated, is foundational.

Maintain scene safety, assess airway/ventilation concurrently, support oxygenation as indicated, prevent heat loss and expedite trauma transport. ALS monitoring, access and resuscitation may supplement but never replace hemorrhage control or delay definitive care. Reassess mentation, respiratory depth/effort, skin, pulse quality and BP; communicate suspected cause, actual trends, treatment times and responses. No single reading proves adequate perfusion or shock resolution. [S3](https://nasemso.org/content.aspx?module_id=701974&page_id=22) [S17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40131241/) [S18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39576138/)

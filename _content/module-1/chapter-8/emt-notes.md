### Core Purpose of Pathophysiology in Emergency Care
* **Fundamental Goal:** Maintaining **adequate perfusion** of the body cells to ensure continuous delivery of **oxygen and glucose** and removal of **waste by-products**.
* **Cellular Need:** Oxygen and glucose are necessary for normal cell metabolism and function.
* **Clinical Application:** Many illnesses and injuries disturb the delivery of oxygen and glucose and the removal of waste by-products. Understanding these disturbances enables the EMT to:
  * Better recognize and explain why certain signs and symptoms occur.
  * Comprehend the emergency care required for the patient.

## 8.2: Cellular Metabolism

### Overview
* **Cellular Metabolism (Cellular Respiration):** The process in which body cells break down molecules of **glucose** to produce energy for the body.
* **Two Types:** **Aerobic** and **anaerobic** metabolism.
* **Determining Factor:** Which type occurs depends on whether there is an effective and continuous delivery of **oxygen** and **energy sources (fuel)**.
  * **Primary fuel:** Glucose.
  * **Primary catalyst:** Oxygen.

---

### Aerobic Metabolism
* **Definition:** The breakdown of molecules such as glucose through a series of reactions that produce energy within the cells ***in the presence of oxygen*** (*aerobic* = "with oxygen").
* **Step 1 — Glycolysis:**
  * Occurs in the fluid portion of the cell (**cytosol**) when glucose crosses the cell membrane and is broken down into **pyruvic acid** molecules.
  * Does **not** require oxygen.
  * Releases only a **small amount of adenosine triphosphate (ATP)**—an energy source required by the cell to release more energy and carry out functions such as muscle contraction.
* **Step 2 — Mitochondrial Phase (With Oxygen):**
  * When oxygen is available, the reaction continues inside the **mitochondria** of the cell.
  * Releases a **much larger amount of energy (ATP)**, which is necessary for normal cell function.
* **Figure 8-1A Breakdown (Aerobic Metabolism):**
  * Depicts Stage Two (aerobic, with oxygen): Oxygen and glucose enter the cell through the cell membrane -> glucose is processed by the mitochondria -> releases a **large amount of ATP (energy)**.
* **Other By-Products of Aerobic Metabolism (Harmless / Used or Eliminated):**
  * **Heat:** Used to maintain normal body core temperature (increased metabolism -> increased body core temperature).
  * **Carbon dioxide ($CO_2$):** Transported in the blood to the lungs and blown off during exhalation (increased metabolism -> increased respiratory rate to eliminate extra $CO_2$).
  * **Water:** Reabsorbed and used within the body or excreted.
* **Net Result:** All products are either used or eliminated without harming the cells or patient, and a high amount of energy is available for normal body functions.

---

### Anaerobic Metabolism
* **Definition:** The breakdown of molecules in the cells ***without the presence of oxygen*** (*anaerobic* = "without oxygen").
* **Process:**
  * Glucose crosses the cell membrane and normal **glycolysis** occurs, producing **pyruvic acid** and a **small amount of ATP**.
  * Without oxygen, pyruvic acid cannot enter the next phase of metabolism and is instead converted to **lactic acid**.
* **By-Products:** **Lactic acid** and only a **small amount of ATP**.
* **Figure 8-1B Breakdown (Anaerobic Metabolism):**
  * Depicts Stage One (anaerobic, without oxygen): Glucose enters the cell and is processed by the mitochondria -> releases a **small amount of ATP (energy)** and **pyruvic acid**, which converts to **lactic acid**.
* **Cellular Consequences:**
  * Accumulation of lactic acid produces an **acidic environment** that disturbs cellular function and stability.
  * High acid levels **inactivate enzyme function**, **disrupt cell membranes**, and ultimately lead to **cell death**.
  * The cell has very little energy to perform normal functions -> reduced cellular function and potential cellular damage.

---

### Sodium–Potassium Pump Failure
* **Ions Involved** *(electrically charged molecules)*:
  * **Sodium ($Na^+$):** Positively charged; **primary extracellular ion** (found primarily in fluid *outside* the cell, with some inside).
  * **Potassium ($K^+$):** Positively charged; **primary intracellular ion** (found primarily in fluid *inside* the cell, with some outside).
* **Normal Function:**
  * Most body cells require a continuous, alternating cycle of **sodium moving out** of the cell and **potassium moving into** the cell to perform specialized functions (e.g., **muscle contraction** and **nerve impulse transmission**).
  * **Concentration Gradient:** Molecules naturally move from an area of greater concentration to lesser concentration (*with* the concentration gradient).
  * Because sodium concentration is lower inside the cell than outside, sodium does not flow out passively—it must be **actively pumped out *against* the concentration gradient** using **ATP** energy.
  * Potassium then moves **with the concentration gradient** into the cell to replace the removed sodium.
* **Figure 8-2 Breakdown (The Sodium–Potassium Pump Cycle, Parts A–F):**
  * **Part A:** The pump (residing within the lipid bilayer separating extracellular and intracellular fluid) attracts **sodium ions** from inside the cell.
  * **Part B:** An intracellular phosphate from **ATP** attaches to the pump, leaving **ADP**.
  * **Part C:** The phosphate closes the pump on the intracellular side and opens it on the extracellular side, **releasing sodium ions** from the cell.
  * **Part D:** **Potassium ions** from outside the cell enter the pump, and the phosphate detaches.
  * **Part E:** Potassium ions remain in the pump as the extracellular side closes and the intracellular side opens.
  * **Part F:** **Potassium ions enter the cell** through the pump as the intracellular side opens.
* **Consequence of ATP Depletion (Pump Failure):**
  * In poor perfusion states causing anaerobic metabolism (or lack of glucose delivery), insufficient ATP causes the sodium–potassium pump to **fail**.
  * **Sodium collects inside the cell.**
  * **Water follows sodium** -> water accumulates inside the cell -> the cell **swells** and eventually **ruptures and dies**.
* **Figure 8-3 Breakdown (Cell Rupture):**
  * Shows a cell containing many sodium and potassium ions with **no ATP present** -> sodium–potassium pumps fail -> water follows sodium into the cell -> the cell swells -> the cell membrane **ruptures**.
* **Clinical Takeaway:** Sodium–potassium pump failure demonstrates how inadequate oxygen and glucose delivery leads directly to **cell death and organ failure**, reinforcing why maintaining oxygen/glucose delivery and waste elimination is a fundamental purpose of emergency care.

## 8.3: Components Necessary for Adequate Perfusion

### Overview
* **Perfusion:** Delivery of oxygen, glucose, and other substances to the cells and elimination of waste products from the cells.
* **11 Essential Components:**
  1. Composition of ambient air
  2. Patent airway
  3. Mechanics of ventilation
  4. Regulation of ventilation
  5. Ventilation/perfusion (V/Q) ratio
  6. Transport of oxygen and carbon dioxide by the blood
  7. Blood volume
  8. Pump function of the myocardium

* **Clinical Impact:** An alteration in any single component can cause poor cellular perfusion, shifting cells from **aerobic** to **anaerobic metabolism** (less ATP, lactic acid buildup, cell death, and organ failure).

### 1. Composition of Ambient Air
* **Table 8-1 Breakdown: Partial Pressure of Gases in Ambient Atmosphere at Sea Level**
  * **Oxygen:** **20.95%** (~21%) | **159.2 mmHg**
  * **Nitrogen:** **78.08%** (~78%) | **593.4 mmHg**
  * **Argon:** **0.93%** (~0.9%) | **7.1 mmHg**
  * **Carbon Dioxide:** **0.03%** | **0.2 mmHg**
  * **Total:** **99.99%** | **759.9 mmHg** (~760 mmHg) + trace gases
* **Inspired & Delivered Oxygen Fractions (expressed as decimals, not percentages):**
  * **$FiO_2$ (Fraction of Inspired Oxygen):** Concentration inhaled by a *spontaneously breathing* patient (ambient air = **0.21**; nonrebreather mask ≈ **0.95**; 100% $O_2$ = **1.0**).
  * **$FDO_2$ (Fraction of Delivered Oxygen):** Concentration delivered by a *ventilation device* (e.g., BVM with reservoir at 15 lpm ≈ **0.95–0.98**). *$FiO_2$ is typically used in place of $FDO_2$.*
* **Toxic Gases & Hypoxia Mechanisms:**
  * *Oxygen displacement:* Enclosed-space fire gases displace $O_2$ and suffocate the patient.
  * *Transport disruption:* **Carbon monoxide** binds to the oxygen-carrying portion of RBCs.
  * *Cellular utilization disruption:* **Cyanide** prevents cells from using $O_2$ even when alveolar and blood $O_2$ levels are adequate.

---

### 2. Patency of the Airway
* **Patent Airway:** Open and unobstructed by blood, secretions, vomitus, swollen tissues, bone, teeth, or other substances.
* **Figure 8-4 Breakdown (Airway Obstruction Sites, top to bottom):** Nasopharynx -> oropharynx -> posterior pharynx -> epiglottis -> larynx -> trachea -> bronchi -> bronchioles.
* **Anatomic Levels:**
  * **Nasopharynx** *(nostrils to soft palate)*: Fluid/blood can drain into the pharynx and be aspirated; serves as an alternative airway if the mandible is fractured or teeth are clenched; critical to keep clear in **infants (obligate nose breathers)**.
  * **Oropharynx** *(mouth/soft palate to epiglottis)*: Obstructed by tongue, foreign bodies, swelling, hematomas, blood, or vomitus; liquids can be aspirated into alveoli and impair gas exchange.
  * **Epiglottis:** Cartilaginous flap covering the larynx during swallowing; can swell/inflame or fall over the glottic opening when tongue muscles relax (lifted clear by jaw-thrust or chin-lift).
  * **Larynx:** Contains vocal cords.
    * *Adult:* Cylindrical airway; narrowest point = **vocal cords**.
    * *Child (<8 years):* Cone-shaped airway; narrowest point = **cricoid ring**.
    * *Obstructions:* **Laryngeal spasm (laryngospasm)** (vocal cords spasm shut from injury, airway insertion, or medications) or laryngeal fracture causing inward collapse.
  * **Trachea, Bronchi, and Bronchioles:**
    * Trachea bifurcates at the **carina** (**2nd intercostal space / angle of Louis** anteriorly; **T4** posteriorly) into right and left mainstem bronchi -> smaller bronchi -> **bronchioles** (no cartilaginous rings in distal bronchioles; lined with mucus and smooth muscle).
    * *Bronchitis:* Infection inflames bronchial mucous lining -> reduces diameter -> increases airway resistance.
    * *Asthma:* Inflammation + smooth muscle contraction (**bronchoconstriction**) -> turbulent airflow (**wheezing**), **prolonged expiratory phase** (early sign), hypoxia, and $CO_2$ retention (**hypercarbia**).

---

### 3. Mechanics of Ventilation
* **Thoracic Cavity:** Bounded by neck muscles/ribs 1–2 (superior), diaphragm (inferior), muscles/ribs/sternum (anterior), muscles/vertebrae/ribs (posterior); divided by mediastinum into right/left hemithorax.
* **Boyle's Law:** Volume of gas is inversely proportional to pressure ($\uparrow$ pressure = $\downarrow$ volume; $\downarrow$ pressure = $\uparrow$ volume).
  * **Inhalation (Active Process):** Diaphragm (60–70% of effort) moves downward + external intercostals pull ribs up/out -> thorax expands -> intrathoracic pressure drops from **760 mmHg** to **758 mmHg** -> air is pushed into lungs. Serous fluid between parietal and visceral pleura creates a **"water glass" effect** pulling the lung outward against its natural elastic recoil (maintaining constant negative intrapleural pressure).
  * **Exhalation (Passive Process):** Muscles relax + elastic lung recoil shrinks thorax -> intrathoracic pressure rises to **762 mmHg** -> air is forced out.
* **Table 8-2 Breakdown: Accessory Muscles**
  * *Accessory Muscles of Inhalation (increase thoracic size / negative pressure):*
    * **Sternocleidomastoid:** Lifts sternum upward.
    * **Scalene:** Elevates ribs 1 and 2.
    * **Pectoralis minor:** Elevates ribs 3 to 5.
  * *Accessory Muscles of Exhalation (decrease thoracic size / positive pressure; makes exhalation active, e.g., in COPD/emphysema):*
    * **Abdominal muscles:** Increase abdominal pressure, pushing diaphragm upward against lungs.
    * **Internal intercostal muscles:** Pull sternum and ribs downward.
  * *Bellows Action & Fatigue:* Using accessory muscles consumes extra energy; if $O_2$/energy is already low, respiratory muscles fatigue -> respiratory failure.
* **Compliance & Airway Resistance:**
  * **Compliance:** Ability of chest wall and lungs to stretch/expand (decreased by pneumonia, pulmonary edema, flail segment, or neuromuscular disease).
  * **Airway Resistance:** Ease of airflow through conducting airways (increased most commonly by **edema**, plus mucus and bronchoconstriction).
* **Pleural Space (Figure 8-5 Breakdown):**
  * Pressures: **760 mmHg** outside chest | **758 mmHg** inside lung | **754 mmHg** in pleural space (negative pressure).
  * A break in the parietal pleura (open chest wound) or visceral pleura (lung injury) draws air into the pleural space with each breath, collapsing the lung -> occlude open chest wounds early.
* **Minute Ventilation (Minute Volume):**
  * **Formula:** $\text{Minute Ventilation} = V_T \times f/\text{min}$
  * *Normal adult:* $500\text{ mL} \times 12/\text{min} = 6,000\text{ mL/min}$ (**6 L/min**).
  * Requires **both** adequate tidal volume ($V_T$) and adequate frequency ($f$).
* **Alveolar Ventilation:**
  * **Dead Air Space ($V_D$):** Conducting airways (trachea, bronchi, bronchioles) where no gas exchange occurs (~**150 mL** in average adult); fills **first** on every breath (explains why exhaled air still has **17–18% $O_2$**).
  * **Formula:** $\text{Alveolar Ventilation} = (V_T - V_D) \times f/\text{min}$
  * *Normal adult:* $(500 - 150) \times 12 = 4,200\text{ mL/min}$ (**4.2 L/min**).
  * *Low $V_T$ + Fast Rate Example:* $V_T = 200\text{ mL}$, $f = 28/\text{min}$ -> Minute ventilation looks near normal ($5.6\text{ L/min}$), but alveolar ventilation drops to $(200 - 150) \times 28 = 1,400\text{ mL/min}$ (**1.4 L/min**) -> severe hypoxia! Supplemental $O_2$ alone cannot fix this; requires **positive pressure ventilation**.
  * *Unsustainable Rates:* **$\ge$40/min** (adult) or **>60/min** (pediatric) reduce filling time and cause fatigue.

### 4. Regulation of Ventilation
* **Figure 8-6 Breakdown (Autonomic Control of Respiration):**
  * *Brainstem (Medulla/Pons, bottom to top):* Dorsal respiratory group (with vagus nerve), ventral respiratory group, apneustic center, central chemoreceptor, pneumotaxic center (linked to higher/voluntary centers).
  * *Peripheral & Lung Structures:* Carotid body, aortic bodies, stretch receptors, irritant receptors, J-receptors, intercostal nerves, phrenic nerve (to diaphragm).
* **Chemoreceptors:**
  * **Central Chemoreceptors (medulla):** Most sensitive to **$CO_2$ and CSF pH** ($CO_2 + H_2O \rightarrow$ carbonic acid $\rightarrow H^+$ ions). $\uparrow CO_2 \rightarrow \uparrow$ acid $\rightarrow \uparrow$ rate and depth of breathing.
  * **Peripheral Chemoreceptors (aortic arch, carotid bodies):** Most sensitive to a significant drop in **arterial $O_2$** (also somewhat sensitive to $CO_2$/pH) -> stimulates $\uparrow$ rate and depth.
  * **Hypoxic Drive:** Normally breathing is driven by $CO_2$ (**hypercapnic/hypercarbic drive**). In **COPD**, chronically high $CO_2$ desensitizes central chemoreceptors, shifting primary control to peripheral chemoreceptors triggered by **low $O_2$ (hypoxic drive)**.
* **Lung Receptors:**
  * **Irritant receptors (airways):** Detect irritating gases/aerosols/particles -> trigger cough, bronchoconstriction, $\uparrow$ rate.
  * **Stretch receptors (airway smooth muscle):** Detect high tidal volumes -> $\downarrow$ rate and volume to prevent overinflation.
  * **J-receptors (alveoli near capillaries):** Detect $\uparrow$ capillary pressure -> stimulate rapid, shallow ventilation.
* **Brainstem Respiratory Control Centers:**
  * **Ventral Respiratory Group (VRG — anterior medulla):** Solely sends motor impulses to diaphragm and external intercostals. Normal cycle: $VRG_I$ fires **2 seconds** (inhalation), then $VRG_E$ + pontine center inhibit $VRG_I$ for **3 seconds** (exhalation) -> **12 breaths/min**. In forced breathing, $VRG_I$ recruits inspiratory accessory muscles and $VRG_E$ activates expiratory accessory muscles.
  * **Dorsal Respiratory Group (DRG — posterior medulla):** Relays sensory input on rate and depth to the VRG.
  * **Pontine Respiratory Center (Pneumotaxic Center — pons):** Sends inhibitory impulses to $VRG_I$ for smooth transition from inhalation to exhalation.

### 5. Ventilation/Perfusion (V/Q) Ratio
* **Definition:** Ratio of alveolar ventilation ($V$, in L/min) to pulmonary capillary blood flow ($Q$, in L/min).
* **Figure 8-7 Breakdown:** *External respiration* (pulmonary capillaries exchange $CO_2$ to alveoli, pick up $O_2$); *Internal respiration* (systemic capillaries deliver $O_2$ to cells via interstitial fluid, pick up $CO_2$).
* **Normal Gravity Effects:**
  * *Apexes:* Larger, less compliant alveoli with higher surface tension; less blood flow -> **wasted ventilation**.
  * *Bases:* More compliant alveoli + gravity pulls blood down -> highest ventilation and perfusion, with perfusion exceeding ventilation -> **wasted perfusion**. Overall normal **V/Q < 1**.
* **Pressure Imbalances (Figure 8-8):** If alveolar pressure exceeds pulmonary capillary pressure (e.g., severe blood loss dropping BP), capillary blood flow stops -> hypoxemia and hypoxia.
* **V/Q Mismatches (Figure 8-9):**
  * *Ventilatory disturbance (perfusion without ventilation):* e.g., asthma -> airway blockage causes wasted perfusion -> treat with **$O_2$ and bronchodilators**.
  * *Perfusion disturbance (ventilation without perfusion):* e.g., arterial hemorrhage -> lack of blood/hemoglobin causes wasted ventilation -> must **stop bleeding and restore fluid/blood**.

---

### 6. Transport of $O_2$ and $CO_2$ & Gas Exchange
* **Figure 8-10 Breakdown:** Gases move from high to low concentration at both lungs and tissues.
* **Oxygen Transport (~1,000 mL/min):**
  * **97–98.5%** bound to **hemoglobin** (**4 iron binding sites**: 1 = 25%, 2 = 50%, 3 = 75%, 4 = 100% saturation; binding 1 site increases affinity for the rest). Bound = **oxyhemoglobin**; unbound = **deoxyhemoglobin**.
  * **1.5–3%** dissolved in plasma (insufficient for survival alone).
  * *Anemia Pearl:* Low hemoglobin can still read **normal $SpO_2$** on a pulse oximeter while cells are hypoxic—**give $O_2$** to anemic patients complaining of dyspnea.
* **Carbon Dioxide Transport:** **70%** as **bicarbonate** (formed in RBC, carried in plasma, reversed in lungs), **23%** on hemoglobin, **7%** dissolved in plasma.
* **Alveolar/Capillary Exchange:** $O_2$ moves alveolus -> capillary (to left heart -> body); $CO_2$ moves capillary -> alveolus (exhaled).
* **Cell/Capillary Exchange:** $O_2$ moves capillary -> cell; $CO_2$ moves cell -> capillary (to right heart -> lungs).

---

### 7. Blood Volume & Fluid Pressures
* **Volume:** ~**70 mL/kg** (70 kg / 154 lb adult ≈ **4,900 mL / 4.9 L**).
* **Composition:**
  * **Plasma (55%):** **91% water** + plasma proteins (**albumin** for oncotic pressure, **antibodies** for immunity, **clotting factors** led by **fibrinogen**).
  * **Formed Elements (45%):** **RBCs** (erythrocytes; ~48% in men, ~42% in women), **WBCs** (leukocytes), **platelets** (thrombocytes—cell fragments for clotting).
* **Table 8-3 Breakdown: Distribution of Blood**
  * **Venous (capacitance/reservoir system):** **64%**
  * **Arterial:** **13%**
  * **Pulmonary vessels:** **9%**
  * **Capillaries:** **7%**
  * **Heart:** **7%**
* **Fluid Balance (Figure 8-11 Breakdown):**
  * **Hydrostatic Pressure:** "Push" force from cardiac contraction/BP pushing fluid *out* of capillaries -> high hydrostatic pressure causes **edema** (e.g., left ventricular failure backing up into pulmonary capillaries -> **pulmonary edema**).
  * **Plasma (Colloid) Oncotic Pressure:** "Pull" force generated by **albumin** keeping/drawing fluid *into* vessels -> high oncotic pressure causes volume overload; low oncotic pressure causes volume loss and **edema**.

---

### 8. Pump Function of the Myocardium
* **Cardiac Output (CO):** Blood ejected by the left ventricle in 1 minute (normal adult at rest = **5 L/min**; can increase up to **6x**).
  * **Formula:** $CO = \text{Heart Rate (HR)} \times \text{Stroke Volume (SV)}$
* **Heart Rate:** Controlled by SA node (automaticity) and brainstem **cardiovascular control center** (**cardioexcitatory** = $\uparrow$ SNS / $\downarrow$ PNS; **cardioinhibitory** = $\uparrow$ PNS / $\downarrow$ SNS), plus sustained **epinephrine/norepinephrine ($\beta_1$)** from adrenal medulla.
  * *Rate Limit:* Excessively fast HR (**>160 bpm** in adults) shortens ventricular filling time -> $\downarrow$ preload -> **$\downarrow$ CO**.
* **Stroke Volume Determinants (Figure 8-12 Breakdown):**
  * **Preload (Fig. 8-12a):** Left ventricular end-diastolic pressure/volume (driven by venous return). **Frank-Starling Law:** Greater fiber stretch produces stronger contraction (up to a limit—severely dilated ventricles fail).
  * **Myocardial Contractility (Fig. 8-12b):** Force of ventricular contraction (reduced in CHF, MI necrosis, or $\beta_1$ blockers).
  * **Afterload:** Aortic resistance (diastolic BP) the left ventricle must overcome; chronically high afterload causes **left ventricular failure**.

---

### 9. Systemic Vascular Resistance (SVR) & Pulse Pressure
* **SVR (Figure 8-13 Breakdown):** Resistance to blood flow in vessels (**diastolic BP** is its basic measure).
  * **Vasoconstriction** ($\downarrow$ diameter via SNS / $\alpha_1$) -> $\uparrow$ SVR -> $\uparrow$ diastolic BP.
  * **Vasodilation** ($\uparrow$ diameter via PNS) -> $\downarrow$ SVR -> $\downarrow$ diastolic BP.
  * *Compensation Trade-Off:* During blood loss, vasoconstriction maintains BP at the expense of cellular perfusion -> cool skin and quiet/sleepy appearance from anaerobic energy loss (distinguish from hypoxic agitation).
* **Pulse Pressure (PP):** $\text{Systolic BP} - \text{Diastolic BP}$.
  * **Narrow Pulse Pressure:** **<25% of systolic BP** (e.g., BP 108/88 -> PP = 20 mmHg, which is <27 mmHg).
  * Signals $\downarrow$ systolic BP (falling CO/preload) + $\uparrow$ diastolic BP (compensatory $\uparrow$ SVR) -> produces a **"thready" pulse**.

---

### 10. Microcirculation
* **Figure 8-14 Breakdown:** Flow through **arterioles** (smooth muscle) -> **metarterioles** (thoroughfares) -> **true capillaries** (exchange sites) -> **venules**. **Precapillary sphincters** relax to open capillaries or contract to shunt blood away.
* **Three Regulatory Influences:**
  1. **Local factors (dominant at rest):** Temperature, hypoxia, acidosis, histamine.
  2. **Neural factors (adaptation):** SNS (constrict/close) vs. PNS (dilate/open).
  3. **Hormonal factors (sustained effect):** Epinephrine ($\alpha_1$ constricts/closes).

---

### 11. Blood Pressure & Reflex Regulation
* **Formula:** $BP = CO \times SVR$ (higher BP generally increases cellular perfusion; lower BP decreases it).
* **Baroreceptors (Figure 8-15 Breakdown):** Stretch-sensitive receptors in the **aortic arch and carotid sinuses** signaling the medulla's cardioregulatory and vasomotor centers:
  * **Response to $\uparrow$ BP:** $\uparrow$ stretch -> $\uparrow$ impulses to medulla -> $\uparrow$ PNS / $\downarrow$ SNS -> $\downarrow$ HR, $\downarrow$ contractility, vasodilation ($\downarrow$ SVR & $\downarrow$ venous return/preload) -> **$\downarrow$ BP**.
  * **Response to $\downarrow$ BP:** $\downarrow$ stretch -> $\downarrow$ impulses to medulla -> $\uparrow$ SNS / $\downarrow$ PNS -> $\uparrow$ HR, $\uparrow$ contractility, vasoconstriction ($\uparrow$ SVR & $\uparrow$ venous return/preload) -> **$\uparrow$ BP**.
  * *Significant BP Drop:* Medulla triggers adrenal medulla to release **epinephrine** ($\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \beta_1$) and **norepinephrine** ($\alpha_1, \alpha_2$).
* **Chemoreceptor Reflexes (Figure 8-16 Breakdown):** Significant drop in arterial $O_2$ (with $\uparrow CO_2$ and $\downarrow$ pH) triggers medulla's cardioexcitatory and vasomotor centers -> $\uparrow$ HR, $\uparrow$ contractility, and vasoconstriction (explaining why hypoxia presents with **pale, cool skin and tachycardia**).

### Master Checklist for Maintaining Aerobic Metabolism
* Adequate $O_2$ content in ambient air
* Patent airway
* Adequate **minute ventilation** (ventilatory rate & tidal volume)
* Adequate **alveolar ventilation** (ventilatory rate & tidal volume minus dead air space)
* Adequate **pulmonary capillary perfusion** (venous volume & right ventricular pump function)
* Adequate **alveolar–capillary gas exchange**
* Adequate **blood content** (RBCs, hemoglobin, plasma)
* Adequate **cardiac output** (HR, preload, stroke volume, myocardial contractility, afterload)
* Adequate **systemic vascular resistance** (SNS & PNS balance)
* Adequate **capillary–cell gas exchange**

## Chapter 8 Review

### Summary
* **Fundamental Purpose of Emergency Care:** Restore or sustain **perfusion** of cells with **oxygen and glucose**.
* **Aerobic Metabolism:** With adequate delivery of oxygen and glucose, cells maintain aerobic metabolism -> produces **water, carbon dioxide, heat, and ATP** (energy). With adequate perfusion and ventilation, $CO_2$ is effectively eliminated through exhalation.
* **Anaerobic Metabolism:** Occurs when oxygen is not available to cells during metabolism -> produces **lactic acid** as the primary by-product. Acid has a detrimental effect on cell structure and function and leads to **cell death**.
* **Essential Components for Cellular Delivery & Waste Removal:**
  * Establishing and maintaining an adequate and effective:
    * Airway
    * Ventilation
    * Oxygenation
    * Gas exchange at both the **alveoli** and **cells**
    * Transportation of gases in the blood
    * Blood volume
    * Cardiac function
    * Blood pressure
  * An alteration in **any one** of these components can result in **poor perfusion** and **cellular hypoxia**.

### Case Study: Follow-Up (Paul — Abdominal Stab Wound)
* **Scene Size-Up:**
  * Dispatched for a **50-year-old man** stabbed by his wife; wife is in police custody and scene is safe to enter.
  * Daughter leads you to an enclosed back porch where the patient is lying on his side in a pool of blood.
  * *Scene Hazard:* A dog (**Winston**) rushes from the house barking ferociously; instruct the daughter to take Winston inside, place him in a room, and close the door to secure him.
* **Patient Assessment & Pathophysiology Application:**
  * *Mental Status & Airway:* Patient is moaning in pain and clutching his abdomen. Responds with his name (**Paul**) and explains his wife stabbed him while he was trying to watch football -> alert, oriented to name, open airway, breathing adequately.
  * *Ventilation:* **RR 24/min**, slightly shallow -> **minute ventilation** appears slightly increased due to increased rate, and chest rise/fall is adequate, indicating enough **tidal volume** to sustain **alveolar ventilation**.
  * *Perfusion & Skin:* Radial pulse is **barely palpable**; skin is **pale, cool, and clammy** (caused by **vasoconstriction** from **sympathetic nervous system** stimulation and circulating **epinephrine and norepinephrine** to maintain blood pressure).
  * *Pulse & Heart Rate:* Weak pulse likely due to **volume loss, decreased preload, decreased cardiac output, and intense peripheral vasoconstriction**. **HR 122 bpm** (elevated to raise cardiac output and increase/maintain blood pressure).
  * *Oxygenation Intervention:* Partner places patient on a **nonrebreather mask at 15 lpm** to fill the alveoli with high-concentration $O_2$ and maximize oxygen binding to available **hemoglobin**.
  * *Physical Exam & Vitals:* Expose and perform quick head-to-toe exam; patient complains of severe belly pain; **abdomen is rigid and tender** on palpation (suspected blood loss in abdominal cavity).
    * **Vitals:** **BP 102/88 mmHg**, **HR 122 bpm**, **RR 24/min**, skin pale, cool, and clammy.
  * *Pulse Pressure Analysis:*
    * **Calculation:** $102 - 88 = \mathbf{14\text{ mmHg}}$ (25% of ~100 is 25 mmHg -> **narrow pulse pressure**).
    * **Mechanism:** Blood volume loss -> $\downarrow$ **preload** -> $\downarrow$ **stroke volume** and **cardiac output** -> $\downarrow$ **systolic BP**.
    * **Baroreceptor Reflex:** Baroreceptors in the **aortic arch and carotid sinuses** sense the pressure drop and signal the brainstem to trigger the **sympathetic nervous system** -> $\uparrow$ HR (to raise CO) + vasoconstriction (to raise SVR, causing pale/cool/clammy skin), based on $BP = CO \times SVR$.
    * **Result:** Decreased CO + increased SVR = **narrow pulse pressure**.
* **Treatment & Transport:**
  * Cover the abdominal stab wounds and prepare for transport.
  * Reassess en route, contact the receiving medical facility, and give a radio report.
  * Deliver an oral report to the physician at the facility, transfer care, clean up the ambulance, and prepare for the next call.

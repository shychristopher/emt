## 1. Core Purpose & Cellular Metabolism

### Fundamental Purpose of Emergency Care
- Maintaining adequate **perfusion** of body cells ensures:
  - Continuous delivery of **oxygen (O2)** and **glucose** (essential molecules for normal cell metabolism and function).
  - Continuous removal of **waste by-products**.
- Understanding how illnesses and injuries disrupt these processes enables the EMT to recognize why specific signs and symptoms occur and why specific emergency interventions are required.

### Aerobic vs. Anaerobic Metabolism
- **Cellular Metabolism (Cellular Respiration):** The process in which body cells break down **glucose** (primary fuel) using **oxygen** (primary catalyst) to produce cellular energy (**adenosine triphosphate [ATP]**).

| Feature | Aerobic Metabolism ("With Oxygen") | Anaerobic Metabolism ("Without Oxygen") |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Step 1: Glycolysis** | Glucose crosses cell membrane into **cytosol** and breaks down into **pyruvic acid** + a **small amount of ATP** (does not require O2) | Same initial **glycolysis** produces **pyruvic acid** + a **small amount of ATP** |
| **Step 2: Fate of Pyruvic Acid** | Enters **mitochondria** in the presence of O2, releasing a **large amount of ATP** for normal cell function | Cannot enter mitochondria without O2; converts into **lactic acid** |
| **By-Products** | **Heat** (maintains core temp), **CO2** (exhaled by lungs), and **water** (reabsorbed or excreted) | **Lactic acid** + minimal **ATP** |
| **Cellular Outcome** | High energy output with no harmful by-products left to damage cells | Acid buildup inactivates enzymes, disrupts cell membranes, causes low cellular energy, and leads to **cell death** |

### Sodium–Potassium Pump & Pump Failure
- **Key Ions & Gradients:**
  - **Sodium (Na+):** Positively charged **primary extracellular ion** (mostly outside the cell).
  - **Potassium (K+):** Positively charged **primary intracellular ion** (mostly inside the cell).
  - Specialized functions (**muscle contraction**, **nerve impulse transmission**) require continuous alternating movement of **Na+ out** and **K+ in**. Because Na+ concentration is lower inside than outside, it must be **actively pumped out against the concentration gradient** using **ATP**, allowing K+ to move *with* its gradient into the cell.
- **Six-Step Pump Cycle:**
  1. The pump (embedded in the lipid bilayer) attracts Na+ ions from inside the cell.
  2. An intracellular phosphate from **ATP** attaches to the pump, leaving **ADP**.
  3. Phosphate attachment closes the intracellular side and opens the extracellular side, releasing Na+ out of the cell.
  4. K+ ions from outside the cell enter the pump, and the phosphate detaches.
  5. K+ ions remain in the pump as the extracellular side closes and the intracellular side opens.
  6. K+ ions enter the cell through the pump as the intracellular side opens.
- **Mechanism of Pump Failure & Cell Death:**
  - In poor perfusion states (anaerobic metabolism or lack of glucose), insufficient **ATP** causes the sodium–potassium pump to **fail**.
  - **Sodium accumulates inside the cell** → **water follows sodium** into the cell → the cell **swells** → the **cell membrane ruptures and the cell dies**, progressing to **organ failure**.

6. Transport of oxygen and carbon dioxide by the blood

### Component 1: Composition of Ambient Air

| Gas | Sea-Level Percentage | Partial Pressure |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Nitrogen** | **78.08%** | `593.4 mmHg` |
| **Oxygen** | **20.95%** | `159.2 mmHg` |
| **Argon** | **0.93%** | `7.1 mmHg` |
| **Carbon Dioxide** | **0.03%** | `0.2 mmHg` |
| **Total** *(+ trace gases)* | **99.99%** | `759.9 mmHg` |

- **Low-Oxygen Environments:** Enclosed spaces with toxic gases or high altitude lower oxygen concentration and cause cellular hypoxia; ensure the patient breathes at least **21%** oxygen.
- **FiO2 vs. FDO2:**
  - **FiO2 (Fraction of Inspired Oxygen):** Expressed as a decimal/fraction for a spontaneously breathing patient (room air = `0.21`; nonrebreather mask ≈ `0.95`; 100% O2 = `1.0`).
  - **FDO2 (Fraction of Delivered Oxygen):** Delivered by a ventilation device to a patient unable to breathe adequately on their own (BVM with reservoir at `15 L/min` ≈ `0.95–0.98`); FiO2 is commonly used in place of FDO2.
- **Toxic Gases:**
  - *Oxygen displacement:* Displaces ambient oxygen and suffocates the patient (e.g., enclosed-space fire).
  - *Impaired blood transport:* **Carbon monoxide (CO)** binds to the oxygen-carrying portion of red blood cells.
  - *Impaired cellular utilization:* **Cyanide** prevents cells from using oxygen effectively even when alveolar and blood oxygen levels are adequate.

### Component 2: Patency of the Airway
- **Patent Airway:** Open and unobstructed by blood, secretions, vomitus, swollen tissues, bone, teeth, or foreign objects.
- **Anatomical Obstruction Points:**
  - **Nasopharynx** *(nostrils to soft palate)*: Blood/vomitus can drain into pharynx causing aspiration or occlusion; serves as alternative airway when oropharynx is inaccessible (fractured mandible, clenched teeth) and must be kept clear in **infants** (obligate nose breathers).
  - **Oropharynx** *(mouth/soft palate to epiglottis)*: Easily obstructed by the tongue, foreign bodies, swelling, hematomas, blood, or vomitus; aspirated liquids damage lung tissue or fill alveoli.
  - **Epiglottis:** Cartilaginous flap covering laryngeal opening during swallowing; swelling/inflammation can occlude the larynx, and flaccid tongue muscles allow it to fall over the glottic opening (lifted clear by jaw-thrust or chin-lift maneuvers; swelling may require advanced airway management).
  - **Larynx:** Contains vocal cords and mucous membrane; narrowest upper-airway point in **adults** (cylindrical airway) is at the **vocal cords**, whereas in **children younger than 8 years** (cone-shaped airway) it is at the **cricoid ring**.
    - *Laryngeal spasm (laryngospasm):* Vocal cords spasm shut (from injury, mechanical airways, or medications), blocking airflow into the trachea.
    - *Laryngeal fracture:* Loss of structural stability allows tissues to be drawn inward, causing obstruction.
  - **Trachea, Bronchi & Bronchioles:** Trachea bifurcates at the **carina** (2nd intercostal space / angle of Louis anteriorly; `T4` posteriorly) into right/left mainstem **bronchi** → smaller bronchi → smooth-muscle **bronchioles** (no cartilaginous rings distally) → **alveoli**.
    - *Bronchitis:* Infection inflames the bronchial mucous lining.
    - *Asthma:* Inflammation + **bronchoconstriction** reduces airway diameter, increases airway resistance, causes hypoxia and **hypercarbia** (CO2 retention), creates turbulent airflow (**wheezing**), and produces a **prolonged expiratory phase** (early sign).

### Component 3: Mechanics of Ventilation
- **Thoracic Cavity & Pleura:** Divided by the mediastinum into right and left hemithoraxes; lungs covered by **visceral pleura** (inner) and **parietal pleura** (outer, adhering to chest wall). Serous fluid between pleurae creates a **"water glass" effect** pulling the visceral pleura and lung outward against natural elastic recoil, maintaining negative intrapleural pressure (`754 mmHg` vs. `758 mmHg` in lung and `760 mmHg` outside).
  - *Pleural Space Breach:* A break in parietal or visceral pleura draws air into the pleural space on each inhalation, collapsing the lung and halting gas exchange—requiring early occlusion of open chest wounds.
- **Boyle’s Law Applied to Ventilation:** Gas volume is inversely proportional to pressure (`↑ pressure → ↓ volume`; `↓ pressure → ↑ volume`):
  - *Inhalation (Active):* Diaphragm (**60–70%** of effort) and external intercostals contract → thorax expands → intrathoracic pressure drops from `760 mmHg` to `758 mmHg` (negative pressure) → air flows into lungs.
  - *Exhalation (Passive):* Muscles relax → chest wall moves inward/downward + lung elastic recoil → intrathoracic pressure rises to `762 mmHg` → air is forced out.
- **Accessory Muscles of Breathing:**

| Phase | Accessory Muscles | Mechanical Action & Clinical Risk |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Inhalation** *(Increase negative pressure)* | **Sternocleidomastoid** (lifts sternum), **Scalene** (elevates ribs 1–2), **Pectoralis minor** (elevates ribs 3–5) | Consumes extra energy; inadequate O2 delivery leads to muscle fatigue and respiratory failure |
| **Exhalation** *(Active exhalation, e.g., COPD/emphysema)* | **Abdominal muscles** (push diaphragm up), **Internal intercostals** (pull sternum/ribs down) | Disruption of chest "bellows action" (compression, multiple rib fractures, chest trauma) reduces negative pressure and air intake |

- **Compliance & Airway Resistance:**
  - *Compliance:* Ability of chest wall and lungs to stretch/expand; decreased (stiffened) by pneumonia, pulmonary edema, flail chest, or neuromuscular disease—making spontaneous and artificial ventilation harder.
  - *Airway resistance:* Ease of airflow down conducting airways; increased primarily by **edema**, as well as mucus and bronchiolar constriction.
- **Minute Ventilation vs. Alveolar Ventilation & Dead Air Space (VD):**
  - **Dead Air Space (VD):** Conducting airways (trachea, bronchi, bronchioles) where no gas exchange occurs (**~150 mL** in an average adult). Explains why exhaled air still contains **17–18%** oxygen (allowing mouth-to-mouth ventilation).
  - **Minute Ventilation (Minute Volume):** `VT × f/min` → Normal adult: `500 mL × 12/min = 6,000 mL/min (6 L/min)`.
  - **Alveolar Ventilation:** `(VT − VD) × f/min` → Normal adult: `(500 mL − 150 mL) × 12/min = 4,200 mL/min (4.2 L/min)`.
  - *Low VT Clinical Trap:* If `VT = 200 mL` and `f = 28/min`, minute ventilation looks near normal (`5.6 L/min`), but alveolar ventilation plunges to `(200 − 150) × 28 = 1.4 L/min` because dead space fills first. Supplemental O2 alone will not fix this—**positive-pressure ventilation** is required.
- **Four Causes of Inadequate Ventilation:** (1) Low tidal volume, (2) rate too slow, (3) rate too fast (**>=40/min** in adults or **>60/min** in pediatrics—insufficient filling time + unsustainable workload), or (4) increased dead air space.

### Component 4: Regulation of Ventilation
- Primarily involuntary via the autonomic nervous system and brainstem respiratory centers, with limited voluntary override:

| Regulatory Structure | Location | Primary Stimulus & Physiological Effect |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Central Chemoreceptors** | Medulla | Most sensitive to **CO2** and CSF **pH** (`CO2 + H2O → carbonic acid → H+`); ↑ CO2 → ↑ rate & depth; ↓ CO2 → ↓ rate & depth (*hypercapnic drive*) |
| **Peripheral Chemoreceptors** | Aortic arch & carotid bodies | Most sensitive to significant drops in **arterial O2** (somewhat to CO2/pH); primary breathing stimulus in **hypoxic drive** (chronic CO2-retaining **COPD** patients whose central receptors desensitize to CO2) |
| **Irritant Receptors** | Airways | Sensitive to gases, aerosols, particles → trigger cough, bronchoconstriction, and ↑ ventilatory rate |
| **Stretch Receptors** | Airway smooth muscle | Measure lung size/volume → decrease rate and volume at high tidal volumes to prevent overinflation |
| **J-Receptors** | Alveoli near capillaries | Sensitive to ↑ capillary pressure → stimulate rapid, shallow ventilation |
| **Ventral Respiratory Group (VRG)** | Anterior medulla | Contains inspiratory (`VRG-I`) and expiratory (`VRG-E`) neurons; sends motor impulses to diaphragm & external intercostals. Normal cycle: **2 sec** on (inhalation) / **3 sec** off (passive exhalation) = **~12/min**; also drives forced inhalation/exhalation |
| **Dorsal Respiratory Group (DRG)** | Posterior medulla | Relays sensory input on rate and depth to the VRG |
| **Pontine Respiratory Center** *(Pneumotaxic Center)* | Pons | Sends inhibitory impulses to `VRG-I` to halt inspiration and smooth transition to exhalation |

### Component 5: Ventilation/Perfusion (V/Q) Ratio
- **Definition:** Relationship between alveolar ventilation (`V`, L/min) and pulmonary capillary blood flow (`Q`, L/min), determining gas exchange quality.
- **Normal Regional Differences:** Gravity compresses lower lobes and pulls blood to the lung bases (more compliant, better ventilated, better perfused), while apexes have larger, less compliant alveoli:
  - *Apexes:* Ventilation > perfusion (*wasted ventilation*).
  - *Bases:* Perfusion > ventilation (*wasted perfusion*).
  - *Overall normal V/Q:* **Less than 1** (perfusion exceeds ventilation).
- **V/Q Mismatches:**
  - *Pressure Imbalances:* If alveolar pressure exceeds pulmonary capillary pressure (most likely at apexes or during hemorrhagic hypotension), capillary blood flow stops → hypoxemia and cellular hypoxia.
  - *Ventilatory Disturbances (Wasted Perfusion):* Less oxygenated air reaches alveoli despite normal blood flow (e.g., asthma) → manage by improving ventilation/oxygenation (O2 + bronchodilators).
  - *Perfusion Disturbances (Wasted Ventilation):* Adequate alveolar ventilation but insufficient blood/hemoglobin in capillaries (e.g., severe arterial bleeding) → O2 helps partially, but definitive care requires stopping bleeding and restoring fluid/blood volume.

### Component 6: Transport of Oxygen and Carbon Dioxide by the Blood
- **Gas Movement Principle:** Both O2 and CO2 diffuse from areas of **higher concentration** to **lower concentration**.
- **Oxygen Transport (~1,000 mL/min delivered):**
  - **97–98.5%** bound to **hemoglobin** (**4 iron binding sites** per molecule: 1 site = 25%, 2 = 50%, 3 = 75%, 4 = 100% saturation; binding one O2 increases affinity for the rest). Bound = **oxyhemoglobin**; unbound = **deoxyhemoglobin**.
  - **1.5–3%** dissolved in plasma (insufficient to sustain life alone).
  - *Anemia & SpO2 Pearl:* Anemic patients have less total hemoglobin, so remaining hemoglobin may read **normal/high SpO2** while cells are hypoxic—always administer oxygen to a dyspneic anemic patient even with a normal pulse oximeter reading.
- **Carbon Dioxide Transport:**
  - **70%** as **bicarbonate** (formed in RBCs from `CO2 + H2O → carbonic acid → H+ + bicarbonate`, transported in plasma, converted back to `H2O + CO2` in pulmonary circulation).
  - **23%** attached to **hemoglobin**.
  - **7%** dissolved in **plasma**.
- **Gas Exchange Sites:**
  - *Alveolar/Capillary:* High alveolar O2 diffuses into capillary blood; high capillary CO2 diffuses into alveoli for exhalation.
  - *Cell/Capillary:* High arterial/capillary O2 detaches from hemoglobin and enters cells; cellular waste CO2 diffuses into capillaries to return via venules/veins to the right heart and lungs.

### Component 7: Blood Volume & Fluid Pressures
- **Normal Adult Volume:** **~70 mL/kg** (`154 lb / 70 kg` ≈ `4,900 mL` or `4.9 L`); correlates with body mass (1 L loss is far more severe in a 100-lb patient than a 200-lb patient).
- **Blood Composition:**
  - **Plasma (55%):** **91% water** + plasma proteins (**albumin** for oncotic pressure, **antibodies** for immune defense, **clotting factors** such as *fibrinogen* for coagulation).
  - **Formed Elements (45%):** **Red blood cells / erythrocytes** (~48% cell volume in men, 42% in women; carry O2 via hemoglobin), **white blood cells / leukocytes** (infection/debris defense), and **platelets / thrombocytes** (cell fragments for clotting).
- **Blood Distribution Across Vascular Compartments:**

| Compartment | Percentage of Blood Volume | Clinical Significance |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Venous System** | **64%** | Capacitance/reservoir system—volume drops here first regardless of bleeding source, directly reducing right-heart filling |
| **Arterial System** | **13%** | High-pressure distribution vessels |
| **Pulmonary Vessels** | **9%** | Pulmonary gas-exchange circulation |
| **Capillaries** | **7%** | Microcirculation exchange sites |
| **Heart** | **7%** | Pumping chambers |

- **Hydrostatic Pressure vs. Plasma Oncotic Pressure:**
  - *Hydrostatic pressure ("push"):* Generated by heart contraction and BP; pushes fluid out of capillaries into interstitial space. High hydrostatic pressure promotes **edema** (e.g., left ventricular failure backs blood into pulmonary capillaries → fluid leaks into interstitial space and alveoli → **pulmonary edema**).
  - *Plasma / colloid oncotic pressure ("pull"):* Generated by large plasma proteins (especially **albumin**) that pull/retain fluid inside vessels. High oncotic pressure → blood volume overload; low oncotic pressure → vascular volume loss and edema.

### Component 8: Pump Function of the Myocardium
- **Cardiac Output (CO):** Volume of blood ejected by the left ventricle in 1 minute (normal resting adult = **5 L/min**; can increase up to **6×**):
  - Formula: `Cardiac Output (CO) = Heart Rate (HR) × Stroke Volume (SV)`
- **Heart Rate (HR):** Controlled by intrinsic **automaticity** (SA node) and modulated by the brainstem cardiovascular control center (**cardioexcitatory** = ↑ SNS / ↓ PNS → ↑ HR; **cardioinhibitory** = ↑ PNS / ↓ SNS → ↓ HR) plus adrenal **epinephrine/norepinephrine** (β1 receptors → sustained ↑ HR and contractility).
  - *Excessive HR Limit:* Rates **>160 bpm** in adults shorten ventricular filling time, reducing preload and **decreasing** cardiac output.
- **Stroke Volume (SV) — Three Determinants:**
  1. **Preload:** End-diastolic pressure/volume in the left ventricle (driven by venous return). Governed by the **Frank-Starling law of the heart**—greater muscle fiber stretch produces stronger contraction force (up to an overstretched/dilated limit).
  2. **Myocardial Contractility:** Force of ventricular contraction; reduced in congestive heart failure or myocardial infarction (necrotic muscle), and decreased by β1-blocker medications.
  3. **Afterload:** Aortic resistance the left ventricle must overcome to eject blood; chronically high diastolic BP increases myocardial workload and can lead to left ventricular failure.

### Component 9: Systemic Vascular Resistance (SVR) & Pulse Pressure
- **Vessel Size & Resistance:**
  - **Vasoconstriction** (`↓ diameter`) → `↑ SVR` → `↑ BP` (driven by SNS and α1 effects of epinephrine/norepinephrine).
  - **Vasodilation** (`↑ diameter`) → `↓ SVR` → `↓ BP` (driven by PNS).
- **Diastolic BP & Compensation:** Diastolic BP reflects resting SVR; chronically elevated diastolic BP overworks the left ventricle into failure. During blood loss, vasoconstriction maintains BP at the expense of tissue perfusion—producing **cool skin** and anaerobic metabolism (often making a hypoperfused patient quiet/sleepy, though brain hypoxia can also cause agitation).
- **Pulse Pressure:** `Systolic BP − Diastolic BP` (systolic reflects CO; diastolic reflects SVR).
  - **Narrow Pulse Pressure:** **<25%** of systolic BP (e.g., `108/88 mmHg` → pulse pressure `20 mmHg`, which is `<27 mmHg`).
  - *Clinical Significance:* Early warning sign of dropping CO (`↓ systolic`) with compensatory `↑ SVR` (`↑ diastolic`) during blood/fluid loss—producing a **"thready" pulse** even when BP appears normal.

---

### Component 10: Microcirculation
- **Structure:** Blood flow through **arterioles** (almost all smooth muscle), **metarterioles** (thoroughfare channels), **true capillaries** (gas/nutrient/waste exchange sites), and **venules**.
- **Precapillary Sphincters:** Relax to allow blood through true capillaries; contract to shunt blood away and preserve arterial pressure.
- **Three Regulatory Factors:**
  1. *Local factors (predominant at rest):* Temperature (cold constricts/closes sphincters; heat dilates/opens), hypoxia, acidosis, histamine.
  2. *Neural factors (adaptation):* SNS constricts/closes; PNS dilates/opens.
  3. *Hormonal factors (sustained effect):* e.g., epinephrine α1 stimulation constricts arterioles and closes sphincters.

---

### Component 11: Blood Pressure & Reflex Regulation
- **Formula:** `Blood Pressure (BP) = Cardiac Output (CO) × Systemic Vascular Resistance (SVR)`
- **General Perfusion Rule:** `↑ BP → ↑ cellular perfusion`; `↓ BP → ↓ cellular perfusion` (until compensatory mechanisms are overwhelmed).
- **Baroreceptors (Aortic Arch & Carotid Sinuses):** Stretch-sensitive pressure sensors signaling the medullary cardioregulatory and vasomotor centers:
  - *Response to ↑ BP:* `↑ baroreceptor stretch` → `↑ impulses to medulla` → cardioinhibitory center (`↑ PNS, ↓ SNS`) → `↓ HR & ↓ contractility (↓ CO)`; vasomotor center (`↓ SNS`) → vasodilation (`↓ SVR & ↓ venous return/preload`) → **↓ BP**.
  - *Response to ↓ BP:* `↓ baroreceptor stretch` → `↓ impulses to medulla` → cardioexcitatory center (`↑ SNS, ↓ PNS`) → `↑ HR & ↑ contractility (↑ CO)`; vasomotor center (`↑ SNS`) → vasoconstriction (`↑ SVR & ↑ venous return/preload`) → **↑ BP**.
  - *Severe BP Drop:* Medulla stimulates the **adrenal medulla** to release **epinephrine** (α1/α2 vasoconstriction + β1 ↑ HR/contractility; β2 has no BP-raising effect) and **norepinephrine** (predominantly α1/α2 vasoconstriction).
- **Chemoreceptor Cardiovascular Reflexes:** Triggered cardiovascularly only by significant drops in **O2** (stronger CV effect than CO2/pH) → activates cardioexcitatory and vasomotor centers (`↑ HR, ↑ contractility, vasoconstriction`) and endocrine hormone cascade to boost brain O2 delivery—explaining **pale, cool skin** (vasoconstriction) and **tachycardia** (SNS SA-node stimulation) in hypoxia.

## 3. Master Summary: 10 Requirements for Aerobic Metabolism
1. Adequate oxygen content in ambient air
2. Patent airway
3. Adequate **minute ventilation** (ventilatory rate + tidal volume)
4. Adequate **alveolar ventilation** (ventilatory rate + tidal volume minus dead air space)
5. Adequate **pulmonary capillary perfusion** (venous volume + right ventricular pump function)
6. Adequate **alveolar/capillary gas exchange**
7. Adequate **blood content** (RBCs, hemoglobin, plasma)
8. Adequate **cardiac output** (HR, preload, stroke volume, myocardial contractility, afterload)
9. Adequate **systemic vascular resistance** (SNS + PNS balance)
10. Adequate **capillary/cell gas exchange**

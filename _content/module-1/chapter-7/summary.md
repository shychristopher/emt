# Chapter 7: Anatomy, Physiology, and Medical Terminology — Rapid Review

## Foundations and Medical Terminology

**Anatomy** explains body structure and relationships; **physiology** explains function. Together they allow EMTs to recognize abnormal findings, anticipate deterioration, understand interventions and communicate precisely. Body systems are interdependent, and developmental changes alter normal findings and responses to illness.

Medical words are built from a **root** (core meaning), a combining vowel (usually `o`), a **prefix** (modifies meaning before the root) and/or a **suffix** (modifies meaning at the end). A combining form consists of a root and combining vowel.

| Word part | Meaning and example |
| --- | --- |
| `cardi/o`, `pne/o`, `glyc/o` | Heart, breathing, glucose |
| `hypo-`, `hyper-` | Below/above normal: hypovolemia, hyperglycemia |
| `tachy-`, `brady-`, `a-` | Fast, slow, absent: tachycardia, bradypnea, apnea |
| `endo-`, `peri-` | Within, around: endotracheal, pericardium |
| `-itis`, `-emia`, `-al` | Inflammation, blood condition, pertaining to |
| `-centesis`, `-ectomy`, `-ostomy` | Puncture, removal, creation of an opening |
| `-scope`, `-scopy`, `-lysis` | Examination instrument, examination process, breakdown |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Communicate objective findings, anatomical location, onset, baseline, interventions and response rather than unsupported diagnoses. EMTs provide foundational assessment and BLS care; AEMTs and paramedics may provide progressively broader authorized interventions. Knowledge, equipment preparation or an ALS partner's presence does not expand scope. Laws, credentialing, local protocols and medical direction govern practice. [Scope][scope] · [Model guidelines][model]

## Anatomical Orientation

**Anatomical position:** upright, facing forward, arms at sides, palms forward. Right and left always refer to the patient.

| Planes and directions | Relationship |
| --- | --- |
| Sagittal / midsagittal | Right and left portions / equal halves |
| Frontal (coronal) | Anterior and posterior portions |
| Transverse (axial) | Superior and inferior portions |
| Anterior / posterior; ventral / dorsal | Front / back |
| Superior / inferior | Toward head / feet |
| Medial / lateral | Toward / away from midline |
| Proximal / distal | Nearer / farther from reference point |
| Unilateral / bilateral | One side / both sides |
| Ipsilateral / contralateral | Same side / opposite side |
| Palmar / plantar | Palm / sole |

### Patient positioning

- **Supine:** face up; **prone:** face down; **lateral recumbent:** on one side, potentially supporting airway drainage when appropriate.
- **Fowler's:** upper body elevated 45–60°; **semi-Fowler's:** less than 45°. Upright positioning may reduce work of breathing; supine may be better tolerated in hypoperfusion.
- **Trendelenburg:** head-down tilt; not recommended for shock because it may impede ventilation and increase intracranial pressure. Historical shock position elevates legs about 12 inches.

> [!CAUTION]
> The source's categorical rejection of leg elevation is qualified by 2024 AHA/Red Cross guidance: temporary elevation may be considered in selected nontraumatic shock, with limited and transient benefit. Avoid routine elevation in trauma or suspected spine injury. The source's 30-minute recovery-position side-switch rule is not universal; monitor airway, pressure areas and perfusion rather than rolling a suspected spine injury on a timer. Inadequate breathing requires appropriate ventilation/resuscitation. [First aid][firstaid] · [SMR][smr]

### Regions, landmarks and cavities

- **Head/neck:** cranial, occipital and cervical regions; orbits, maxilla, mandible, zygomatic bones and mastoid process.
- **Thorax:** sternum (manubrium, body, xiphoid), Angle of Louis, suprasternal notch, clavicle, intercostal spaces and costal margin; midsternal, midclavicular, anterior/mid/posterior axillary, midscapular and midspinal reference lines.
- **Trunk:** umbilical, inguinal, lumbar, flank, iliac crest and sacral regions. **Extremities:** cubital (elbow), femoral (thigh), popliteal (behind knee), malleoli (ankle) and calcaneus (heel).
- **Cavities:** cranial (brain), spinal (cord), thoracic (heart/lungs), abdominal and pelvic (abdominopelvic together). The diaphragm separates thorax and abdomen.

Abdominal quadrants meet at the umbilicus:

| Quadrant | Principal structures |
| --- | --- |
| RUQ | Most liver, gallbladder, right kidney, portions of pancreas/intestines |
| LUQ | Spleen, stomach, left kidney, most pancreas, portions of liver/intestines |
| RLQ | Appendix, right ureter, bowel, right ovary/tube |
| LLQ | Left ureter, bowel, left ovary/tube |
| Midline pelvis | Bladder, uterus or prostate |

Solid-organ or vascular injuries can produce concealed hemorrhage; hollow-organ injury can also be serious. Quadrant tenderness does not establish the injured organ. Report mechanism, guarding, distention and perfusion trends. **ALS connection:** standardized chest landmarks support accurate ECG electrode placement; acquisition is distinct from interpretation or authorization for invasive procedures. [Field triage][triage] · [ACS guideline][acs] · [Scope][scope]

## Musculoskeletal System

Bones support the body, protect organs, permit movement, store minerals and produce blood cells. **Ligaments connect bone to bone; tendons connect muscle to bone.** Bones are long, short, flat or irregular.

### Skeleton

- **Skull:** rigid cranium protects brain; facial bones include fixed maxillae and movable mandible. Intracranial bleeding/swelling cannot readily expand outward, so pressure rises and brain perfusion/function may deteriorate.
- **Spine:** 33 vertebrae — 7 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 fused sacral, 4 fused coccygeal. Intervertebral disks absorb shock; the vertebral column protects the spinal cord.
- **Thorax:** sternum, thoracic vertebrae, 12 rib pairs. True ribs 1–7 attach directly via cartilage; false ribs 8–10 indirectly; floating ribs 11–12 have no anterior attachment.
- **Pelvis:** ilium, ischium, pubis, sacrum and coccyx support abdominal organs and protect pelvic structures.
- **Upper extremity:** clavicle/scapula → humerus → radius (thumb side), ulna (little-finger side) → carpals, metacarpals, phalanges. The olecranon forms the elbow prominence.
- **Lower extremity:** acetabulum and femoral head form hip; femur, patella and tibia form knee region; medial weight-bearing tibia and lateral fibula form ankle prominences. Tarsals (including calcaneus), metatarsals and phalanges form foot.

### Joints and muscle

Joints may be immovable, slightly movable or freely movable (synovial). Synovial cartilage, membrane and ligaments support movement.

| Joint | Principal movement / example |
| --- | --- |
| Ball-and-socket | Multidirectional/rotation; shoulder, hip |
| Hinge | Flexion/extension; elbow, knee, ankle |
| Pivot | Rotation; C1–C2, radioulnar joints |
| Gliding | Sliding; small hand/foot bones |
| Saddle | Movement in perpendicular planes; thumb base |
| Condyloid | Two-plane motion without full rotation; wrist |

Flexion decreases joint angle; extension increases it. Abduction moves away from midline, adduction toward it. Circumduction combines movements; pronation/supination turn the forearm palm backward/forward. The source corrects the textbook's ankle-as-saddle and wrist-as-pivot examples. [Joint anatomy][joints]

| Muscle type | Function |
| --- | --- |
| Skeletal | Voluntary movement; nerve-stimulated fibers pull through tendons |
| Smooth | Involuntary control of hollow organs, airways and vessels |
| Cardiac | Involuntary striated myocardium; automaticity generates impulses; coronary circulation supplies oxygen |

Vascular smooth muscle contraction increases resistance through vasoconstriction; relaxation decreases resistance through vasodilation, influencing BP.

> [!WARNING]
> Fractures can injure vessels and nerves. Femoral fractures may conceal 1,000–2,000 mL of blood; pelvic fractures can potentially involve an entire blood volume (~5 L or 70–80 mL/kg). These are severity illustrations, not patient-specific predictions. Vertebral injury may occur without pain or initial neurologic deficits. High cervical injury can compromise breathing and sympathetic control. Use protocol-directed SMR, avoid forced alignment, assess distal circulation/sensation/movement and reassess. Prevent hypoxia and hypotension in head injury; expedite definitive care. [SMR][smr] · [Field triage][triage] · [Trauma airway][traumaair]

**ALS connection:** analgesia may improve comfort but opioids can depress breathing and BP; resuscitation does not repair internal hemorrhage. Report changes rather than delaying transport for nonessential procedures. [Model guidelines][model]

## Respiratory System

### Function and airway anatomy

**Ventilation** moves air; **respiration** exchanges gases; **oxygenation** supplies oxygen. CO₂ removal also helps regulate pH alongside kidneys and bicarbonate buffering. Hypoventilation retains CO₂ and tends to lower pH; deep, rapid breathing may compensate for metabolic acidosis.

- **Upper airway:** nose (filters, warms and moistens air), mouth, nasopharynx, oropharynx, laryngopharynx and larynx to cricoid cartilage. The thyroid cartilage forms the Adam's apple; cricoid is the complete cartilaginous ring. True vocal cords surround the glottis; epiglottis and glottic closure protect the airway during swallowing. Altered consciousness can compromise these reflexes and permit aspiration.
- **Lower airway:** trachea (C-shaped cartilage, esophagus posterior) → main bronchi → bronchioles → alveoli. Bronchoconstriction increases airflow resistance; bronchodilation decreases it.
- **Alveoli:** thin respiratory membranes and surrounding capillaries permit diffusion. Surfactant reduces surface tension and collapse; premature infants may have deficiency, and drowning may disrupt it.
- **Pleura:** visceral covers lung, parietal lines chest wall; negative pressure in the pleural space supports lung expansion.

### Ventilation and gas exchange

Inspiration: diaphragm and intercostals contract → thorax expands → pressure falls → air enters. Expiration: muscles relax → thoracic volume decreases → air exits. The diaphragm accounts for approximately 60–70% of breathing effort and is supplied by the phrenic nerve (C3–C5); high cervical injury may impair or eliminate spontaneous breathing.

- **Minute ventilation = respiratory rate × tidal volume.** Alveolar ventilation excludes dead-space air; fast, shallow breathing may be ineffective.
- In lungs, O₂ diffuses from alveoli to blood and CO₂ from blood to alveoli. At systemic capillaries, O₂ moves to tissues and CO₂ enters blood for return to lungs.

### Assessing adequacy

Adequate breathing requires appropriate rate, depth, regular rhythm, chest expansion, air movement and effort. Chapter reference rates (not treatment thresholds): adults 8–24/min (average 12–20), elderly 20–22, children 18–37, infants 30–60.

Inadequacy may manifest as shallow/unequal rise, diminished breath sounds, abnormal rate, retractions, nasal flaring, cyanosis, cool clammy skin, grunting, infant seesaw breathing or head bobbing. Declining effort and alertness can indicate fatigue, not improvement. Agonal gasps are not normal breathing; assess for arrest and initiate indicated BLS resuscitation. [Adult BLS][bls] · [PALS][pals]

> [!IMPORTANT]
> A patent airway is not necessarily protected against aspiration. Positioning, suction and effective BVM ventilation remain central. Positive-pressure ventilation can reduce venous return and BP when excessive, especially in shock. Assess chest rise, mask seal, breath sounds and perfusion. [Airway physiology][airway] · [Trauma airway][traumaair]

**ALS connection:** SpO₂ estimates hemoglobin saturation, not ventilation or oxygen content; motion, poor perfusion, temperature and pigmentation affect accuracy. EtCO₂ reflects exhaled CO₂ and waveform, influenced by ventilation, metabolism and pulmonary flow; it does not measure pH. Waveform loss requires patient/airway/circuit evaluation. Supraglottic devices sit above the glottis; endotracheal tubes enter the trachea but do not guarantee aspiration protection. CPAP requires appropriate patient selection and spontaneous breathing, not apnea. [FDA][fda] · [Airway physiology][airway] · [Model guidelines][model]

## Circulatory System

### Heart, vessels and blood

The heart pumps blood through a closed vascular system to deliver oxygen/nutrients, remove wastes, support immunity, clotting, temperature and acid–base balance. It sits within the pericardium; the myocardium contracts in response to electrical impulses.

**Blood flow:** Body → venae cavae → right atrium → tricuspid valve → right ventricle → pulmonary valve → pulmonary arteries → lungs → pulmonary veins → left atrium → mitral valve → left ventricle → aortic valve → aorta → body.

**Conduction:** SA node → AV node → bundle of His → bundle branches → Purkinje fibers. Electrical activity does not establish effective mechanical pumping; organized ECG activity without a pulse is PEA. Assess actual perfusion; provide CPR/AED when indicated. [Adult ALS][als]

Arteries carry blood away from the heart, veins return it; pulmonary arteries and veins reverse the usual oxygenation pattern. Arterioles feed capillaries, where exchange occurs, and venules drain into veins. Venous valves help prevent backflow.

Central pulse sites: carotid and femoral. Peripheral sites: radial, brachial (also infant pulse/BP), dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial. An apical heartbeat does not guarantee adequate output.

- RBC hemoglobin carries oxygen; WBCs provide immune defense; platelets/clotting factors form clots. Plasma transports cells and dissolved materials; albumin helps retain intravascular water.
- Excess hydrostatic pressure pushes fluid into tissues (edema); pulmonary edema interferes with gas exchange.

### Perfusion and shock

**Cardiac output = heart rate × stroke volume.** Blood pressure reflects flow and vascular resistance. Systolic pressure occurs during ventricular contraction; diastolic during relaxation. Neither is an isolated measure of one physiological variable. Normal BP does not rule out hypoperfusion.

| Shock mechanism | Physiological problem |
| --- | --- |
| Hypovolemic | Reduced circulating volume, e.g., hemorrhage |
| Distributive | Abnormal vascular tone, e.g., anaphylaxis/sepsis |
| Cardiogenic | Pump failure; excess fluid may worsen congestion |
| Obstructive | Mechanical impediment to circulation, e.g., tension pneumothorax |

Perfusion supplies cells with oxygen and glucose and removes wastes; shock compromises this exchange. Aerobic metabolism generates substantial energy, water, heat and CO₂; anaerobic metabolism produces little energy and contributes to acid buildup.

Approximate gas transport figures from the chapter: O₂ 97% hemoglobin-bound / 3% dissolved; CO₂ 70% bicarbonate / 23% hemoglobin-bound / 7% dissolved. These vary with physiology. Oxygen delivery depends on oxygen content **and blood flow**. Anemia, hemorrhage and CO poisoning can make ordinary pulse oximetry falsely reassuring; lactate alone does not establish shock. [Gas transport][transport] · [CDC CO][co]

> [!WARNING]
> A screaming trauma patient can still be in profound shock. Assess immediate threats objectively: bleeding, airway, ventilation, pulse quality, skin, mentation and serial BP. Control accessible life-threatening hemorrhage, prevent heat loss and expedite appropriate trauma care. [Field triage][triage]

**ALS connection:** IV/IO access is a route, not treatment itself. Fluids, blood products and vasopressors are cause-specific and not interchangeable; excessive crystalloid may harm hemorrhagic or cardiogenic patients. ECG supports rhythm/ischemia assessment, but does not prove perfusion. Manual defibrillation, synchronized cardioversion and pacing apply to distinct clinical indications and require authorization. [Model guidelines][model] · [Adult ALS][als] · [ACS guideline][acs]

## Nervous System

The nervous system regulates consciousness, sensation, movement and involuntary functions.

- **CNS:** brain and spinal cord, protected by meninges and cerebrospinal fluid. The brain requires a continuous supply of oxygen and glucose; the blood–brain barrier regulates entry of substances.
- **Cerebrum:** thought, speech, memory, sensation and conscious movement. **Cerebellum:** coordination and balance. **Brainstem:** midbrain, pons and medulla; medullary centers regulate respiration, cardiac activity and vascular tone.
- **PNS:** afferent nerves carry sensory information toward CNS; efferent nerves carry motor signals away. Somatic control governs skeletal muscle; autonomic control regulates involuntary activity.

| Autonomic division | Principal effects |
| --- | --- |
| Sympathetic | Stress response: increased heart rate, vascular tone, bronchodilation, pupil dilation, sweating; reduced digestive activity |
| Parasympathetic | Recovery: slowed heart rate, increased digestive secretions, pupillary and bronchial constriction |

The initial sympathetic neural response is followed by sustained catecholamine release. Consciousness requires an intact reticular activating system (RAS) and at least one functioning cerebral hemisphere; dysfunction of the RAS or both hemispheres can produce unconsciousness.

**ALS connection:** Hypoxia, hypoglycemia, shock, drugs, seizures and stroke can impair consciousness. Report baseline and serial AVPU/GCS, focal deficits, last known well, witness history and glucose if authorized. Stroke scales cannot establish ischemic versus hemorrhagic stroke; protect the airway, avoid unsafe oral intake and follow stroke routing. [Stroke 2026][stroke] · [Model guidelines][model]

## Endocrine System

Ductless glands release hormones into blood, regulating metabolism, development, fluid balance and reproduction.

| Gland | Major role |
| --- | --- |
| Pineal | Melatonin; circadian rhythm |
| Pituitary | Coordinates multiple endocrine functions, growth and water balance |
| Thyroid / parathyroids | Metabolism and growth / calcium and phosphorus balance |
| Thymus | Immune maturation |
| Adrenals | Stress response, salt/water balance and metabolism |
| Pancreatic islets | Insulin promotes glucose uptake; glucagon raises blood glucose |
| Gonads | Reproduction and sex hormones |

### Adrenergic receptors

| Receptor | Principal effect |
| --- | --- |
| Alpha-1 | Vasoconstriction, contributing to pale/cool skin |
| Alpha-2 | Commonly inhibits presynaptic norepinephrine release |
| Beta-1 | Increased heart rate, contractility and conduction |
| Beta-2 | Smooth muscle relaxation, notably bronchodilation |

Sweating is primarily sympathetic cholinergic, not a direct alpha-1 effect. Epinephrine activates alpha and beta receptors; norepinephrine has strong alpha and meaningful beta-1 activity with relatively little beta-2 effect. These are the source's clinical corrections to simplified textbook descriptions. [Autonomic physiology][autonomic]

**Medication connection:** Epinephrine can counter vasodilation and bronchoconstriction in anaphylaxis; resuscitation uses distinct protocols. Albuterol predominantly activates beta-2 receptors, relieving bronchospasm but not upper-airway obstruction. Norepinephrine supports vascular tone in selected shock states under ALS protocols; it cannot replace hemorrhage control. Medication indications, routes and authorization differ by provider level. [Scope][scope] · [Model guidelines][model] · [Adult ALS][als]

## Integumentary System

Skin provides protection, sensation, thermoregulation and water/electrolyte regulation. The **epidermis** is the outer barrier; **dermis** provides strength and contains vessels, sensory nerves, follicles and glands; **subcutaneous tissue** contains fat, vessels and nerves. Hair, nails, sweat and oil glands are accessory structures.

Burns disrupt the barrier, fluid balance and temperature regulation. Deep circumferential burns may threaten distal circulation as edema develops. Protect injured tissue, prevent hypothermia and reassess distal neurovascular status.

> [!WARNING]
> Facial burns with oral swelling can progress to airway compromise despite normal initial speech, breathing or oxygenation. Singed hair alone is less specific. Reassess voice, stridor, effort and alertness; prepare suction/BVM and notify receiving clinicians early. Deep burns or suspected inhalation injury warrant burn-center consultation or transfer consideration. Ordinary SpO₂ cannot exclude CO poisoning. ALS may evaluate early airway management, analgesia and fluids according to clinical findings. [Burn referral][burn] · [CDC CO][co] · [Trauma airway][traumaair]

## Digestive System

Mechanical digestion involves chewing, swallowing and peristalsis; chemical digestion breaks carbohydrates into glucose, fats into fatty acids and proteins into amino acids.

- Mouth/saliva begin digestion; esophagus carries food to stomach, where gastric secretions aid breakdown.
- **Small intestine:** duodenum, jejunum, ileum; completes digestion and absorbs nutrients. **Large intestine:** absorbs water and forms stool for elimination.
- **Liver:** produces bile, stores sugars, produces clotting/plasma components and detoxifies substances. **Gallbladder:** stores/releases bile. **Pancreas:** digestive secretions plus endocrine glucose regulation.
- **Spleen:** LUQ blood filtration and immune function; not a digestive organ.

Vomiting and diarrhea may cause volume/electrolyte loss; GI hemorrhage may cause concealed shock. Ask about hematemesis or blood in stool, intake, anticoagulants and liver disease. **ALS connection:** vascular access and antiemetics may be appropriate; ondansetron has potential QT effects, and nausea relief does not exclude serious disease. [Model guidelines][model]

## Urinary (Renal) System

Kidneys filter blood, excrete wastes, regulate fluid/electrolytes, BP and acid–base balance, and support RBC production. Urine flows from kidneys → ureters → bladder → urethra.

**ALS connection:** Renal failure or missed dialysis can cause volume overload and hyperkalemia, potentially disturbing cardiac conduction. ECG cannot reliably exclude hyperkalemia. Report dialysis schedule, access, medications and respiratory/perfusion findings. Avoid routine BP measurement or vascular access using a dialysis fistula when possible; follow policy. [Model guidelines][model] · [Adult ALS][als]

## Reproductive Systems

Fertilization joins sperm and ovum; development progresses from embryo to fetus to newborn.

- **Male:** testes produce sperm/testosterone; epididymis stores sperm; vas deferens transports it; accessory glands contribute seminal fluid; penis delivers semen.
- **Female:** ovaries produce ova, estrogen and progesterone; fallopian tubes transport ova; uterus supports fetal development; vagina functions as birth canal; vulva protects external openings; breasts produce milk.

**ALS connection:** Later pregnancy may involve compression of major vessels when supine; appropriate positioning or uterine displacement may help, subject to trauma/resuscitation needs. Pain and bleeding may signal ectopic pregnancy; postpartum hemorrhage may reflect inadequate uterine contraction. ALS may consider authorized resuscitation, uterotonics or magnesium sulfate for eclamptic seizures. Document gestational age, bleeding, contractions, delivery times and maternal/newborn condition. [First aid][firstaid] · [Model guidelines][model]

## Pediatric Anatomy and Physiology

Children have smaller airways and circulating volume, higher baseline heart/respiratory rates and metabolic requirements, and lower BP than adults. Caregiver knowledge is especially valuable for children with special health care needs.

### Airway, head and breathing

- Large tongue, narrow airway (newborn trachea approximately 4–5 mm versus adult 20 mm), pliable cartilage and soft tissue make obstruction from swelling, secretions or poor alignment more likely.
- Large occiput may flex the neck when supine; individualized shoulder padding may help neutral alignment. Avoid hyperextension, hyperflexion and pressure on soft tissue beneath the chin. Support an infant's head.
- Infants prefer nasal breathing but are not invariably unable to breathe orally. The traditional cricoid-as-narrowest rule is debated; it should not determine advanced airway size. [Pediatric airway review][pedair]
- Anterior fontanelle typically closes at 12–18 months; posterior by 3 months. Do not press on them or rely on fontanelle appearance alone to identify dehydration/ICP; assess behavior, mental status, perfusion, tears and urine output.
- Pliable, horizontal ribs limit chest expansion and protection from trauma; significant internal injury may occur without fractures. Infants depend heavily on the diaphragm; abdominal movement during breathing can be normal.
- Retractions, grunting and tachypnea reflect increased effort; grunting helps maintain lung expansion but is tiring. Seesaw motion, falling effort, poor air movement, apnea, bradycardia or altered interaction may indicate deterioration.

> [!CAUTION]
> A respiratory rate of 60/min is not a universal failure threshold. Age, depth, appearance, work and trajectory determine adequacy. Exhausted children may breathe more slowly as failure progresses. Age-appropriate BVM ventilation remains central; avoid excessive pressure/volume and reassess chest rise and perfusion. [PALS][pals] · [Airway physiology][airway]

### Circulation and other developmental differences

| Difference | Clinical significance |
| --- | --- |
| Limited ability to increase contractility | Compensation depends heavily on tachycardia and vasoconstriction; cool/mottled skin may occur, although cold exposure can also cause mottling |
| Small blood volume | Modest absolute blood loss can be severe; hypotension may occur abruptly and late (chapter approximation: >30% volume loss, not a treatment threshold) |
| High oxygen/glucose use, limited glucose stores | Hypoxia and hypoglycemia can progress rapidly; seizures, limpness, apnea or abnormal behavior require assessment |
| Less abdominal protection | Liver and spleen are more vulnerable to blunt injury |
| Flexible bones, developing coordination | Greenstick fractures and fall injuries |
| Large surface area relative to mass, thin skin | Rapid heat loss and impaired temperature regulation |

Bradycardia is particularly concerning with hypoxia (the chapter emphasizes late bradycardia in children and early bradycardia in newborns). Do not wait for hypotension before recognizing possible shock. Reassess pulse quality, capillary refill, behavior, breathing and vital trends.

**ALS connection:** Obtain measured or approved estimated weight in kilograms, communicate its source and prepare appropriately sized equipment. IV/IO access, fluids and glucose treatment depend on cause and authorization. Oral glucose requires safe swallowing; IV dextrose supplies glucose directly and glucagon depends on hepatic stores. Reassess breathing, glucose where authorized and clinical response. [PALS][pals] · [Model guidelines][model]

## Geriatric Anatomy and Physiology

Normal aging reduces **physiologic reserve**; disease is not an inevitable consequence of aging. Comorbidities, polypharmacy and altered compensatory responses may mask classic signs. Establish baseline cognition, mobility, breathing, medications (especially anticoagulants) and the onset of change. Infection may occur without fever; acute confusion is not automatically dementia.

| System | Aging changes and clinical consequences |
| --- | --- |
| Cardiovascular | Valve stenosis restricts forward flow; regurgitation permits backflow (mitral regurgitation may cause pulmonary congestion). Reduced stroke volume and chronotropic reserve limit compensation. Conduction degeneration predisposes to dysrhythmias. Arterial stiffness increases systolic pressure; impaired baroreflexes predispose to orthostatic hypotension. |
| Respiratory | Weaker muscles, stiff chest wall, reduced alveolar surface, diminished chemoreceptor response and air trapping reduce respiratory reserve. Weaker cough/cilia and immune defenses increase infection risk. |
| Neurologic | Slower reflexes, reduced sensation, balance and regulatory responses increase falls, dehydration and atypical presentation. Neuropathy may mask pain, including myocardial ischemia. |
| Gastrointestinal | Impaired swallowing, dentition, taste, absorption and motility increase aspiration, malnutrition and constipation risks. Reduced hepatic blood flow/metabolism affects medication clearance. |
| Endocrine | Reduced insulin effectiveness, altered fluid-regulating hormones and reduced beta responsiveness contribute to glucose intolerance, fluid imbalance and impaired cardiovascular compensation. Thyroid hormone changes affect metabolism and temperature. |
| Musculoskeletal | Osteoporosis, reduced muscle mass, cartilage/ligament deterioration and kyphosis increase falls, fracture risk, slow healing and positioning difficulty. |
| Renal | Fewer nephrons and reduced renal blood flow impair filtration, fluid/electrolyte regulation and drug clearance; acute stress may precipitate renal failure. |
| Integumentary | Thin skin and diminished subcutaneous tissue increase tears, pressure injuries and slow healing; reduced sweating and thermoregulation increase heat/cold vulnerability. |

Vision changes may include cataracts (clouded lens), glaucoma (optic nerve damage associated with pressure), macular degeneration (central vision loss) and retinal detachment. Presbycusis preferentially affects higher frequencies; check hearing aids, reduce background noise and avoid shouting.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> New confusion, weakness, dyspnea or syncope may reflect acute disease despite absent pain, fever or marked tachycardia. Do not provoke standing for orthostatic vitals in unstable/fall-prone patients. Respect baseline spinal curvature, use appropriate padding and protocol-directed SMR rather than forcing a flat position. Protect fragile skin and maintain temperature. [Field triage][triage] · [ACS guideline][acs] · [SMR][smr]

**ALS connection:** Reduced respiratory, cardiac, hepatic and renal reserve increases vulnerability to medication effects and inappropriate fluids. Report baseline medications, recent doses, new sedation, hypotension, dyspnea, access-site swelling and monitoring alarms. Oxygen alone cannot correct hypoventilation; noninvasive ventilation and other advanced care require appropriate selection and reassessment. [Airway physiology][airway] · [Model guidelines][model]

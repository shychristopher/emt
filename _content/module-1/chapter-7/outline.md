## 1. Anatomical Terms, Positions, Planes & Quadrants

### Core Concepts & Reference Standard
- **Anatomy:** The structure of the body and the relationship of its parts to each other (how the body is made).
- **Physiology:** The function of the living body and its parts (how the body works).
- **Clinical Importance:** Using accurate terms for position, direction, and location minimizes confusion and clearly communicates assessment findings—especially when requesting online medical direction.
- **Anatomical Position:** The default reference posture for all directional and location terms:
  - Patient standing erect
  - Facing forward
  - Arms down at the sides
  - Palms facing forward

### Patient Positions

| Position | Description & Clinical Considerations |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Supine** | Lying face up on the back. |
| **Prone** | Lying face down on the stomach. |
| **Lateral Recumbent / Recovery Position** | Lying on the left or right side. Position so the airway can be easily monitored and avoid excessive chest pressure that could impair breathing. If kept in the recovery position for **greater than 30 minutes**, turn the patient to the opposite side to avoid injury from impaired blood flow to the lower arm. |
| **Fowler’s Position** | Lying on the back with the upper body elevated at a **45° to 60°** angle. |
| **Semi-Fowler’s Position** | Lying on the back with the upper body elevated at an angle **less than 45°**. |
| **Trendelenburg Position** | Lying on the back on an inclined plane with legs elevated higher than the head and body (head down, legs up). **No longer recommended** for shock management: gravity can push abdominal organs against the diaphragm (making breathing harder) and may increase intracranial pressure in head-injured patients. |
| **Shock Position** | Alternative to Trendelenburg where only the feet and legs are elevated approximately **12 inches**. **No longer recommended** for treating shock (no evidence of effectiveness) and **contraindicated** with suspected spine injury; may still be considered for a simple faint (syncopal episode) or vasodilation-related hypotension. |

### Anatomical Planes & Reference Lines

| Plane / Reference Line | Definition & Anatomical Division |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Anatomical Planes** | Imaginary divisions of the body (paired with reference lines) that clarify internal organ structure and relationships. |
| **Sagittal Plane / Median Plane** | Vertical lengthwise plane dividing the body into right and left segments (not necessarily equal). |
| **Midsagittal Plane** | Vertical plane dividing the body into **equal** right and left halves. |
| **Frontal Plane / Coronal Plane** | Vertical side-to-side plane dividing the body into front (**anterior plane**) and back (**posterior plane**) halves. |
| **Transverse Plane / Horizontal Plane** *(Axial Plane)* | Plane parallel to the ground dividing the body into upper (**superior plane**) and lower (**inferior plane**) halves. |
| **Midline** | Imaginary vertical line from the top of the head through the nose and navel down between the legs; aligns with the midsagittal plane by dividing the body into equal halves. |
| **Midaxillary Line** | Imaginary vertical line from the middle of the armpit (axilla) down to the ankle; connecting both midaxillary lines forms the frontal plane. |
| **Transverse Line** | Imaginary horizontal line through the waist; forms the transverse plane dividing the superior plane (above waist) from the inferior plane (below waist). |

### Directional & Regional Terms

| Term / Pair | Definition & Clinical Reference |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Anterior vs. Posterior** | Toward the front (*anterior*) vs. toward the back (*posterior*). |
| **Superior vs. Inferior** | Toward the head or above a reference point (*superior*) vs. toward the feet or below a reference point (*inferior*). |
| **Dorsal vs. Ventral** | Toward the back/backbone/spine, including dorsum of hand/foot (*dorsal*) vs. toward the front/belly/abdomen (*ventral*). |
| **Medial vs. Lateral** | Toward the midline/center (*medial*) vs. toward the left/right or away from the midline (*lateral*). |
| **Laterality Terms** | **Bilateral** = both left and right sides; **unilateral** = one side; **ipsilateral** = same side; **contralateral** = opposite side. |
| **Proximal vs. Distal** | Near the point of reference (*proximal*) vs. distant/far from the point of reference (*distal*). |
| **Right vs. Left** | Always refers to the **patient's** right and left (opposite the viewer's left/right when facing the patient). |
| **Midclavicular vs. Midaxillary** | **Midclavicular:** Center of each collarbone (clavicle); the **midclavicular line** runs down the anterior thorax from the center of either clavicle. **Midaxillary:** Center of the armpit (axilla), extending from mid-armpit to ankle. |
| **Plantar vs. Palmar** | Sole of the foot (*plantar*) vs. palm of the hand (*palmar*). |

## 2. Body Systems: Structure and Function

### I. The Musculoskeletal System
- **Structural Overview:** Composed of a bony framework (skeleton) held together by **ligaments** (bone to bone), muscle layers, **tendons** (muscle to bone), and connective tissues.
- **Four Core Skeletal Functions:**
  1. Giving the body shape
  2. Protecting vital internal organs
  3. Allowing movement
  4. Storing minerals and producing blood cells
- **Bone Classifications:** Adult bones are classified by size and shape as **long**, **short**, **flat**, or **irregular**.

#### A. Six Basic Skeletal Components
1. **Skull:** Rests atop the spinal column to house and protect the brain; divided into **cranium** and **face**:
   - *Cranium:* Forms the top, back, sides, and forehead via interlocking flat bones (**occipital**, two **parietal**, two **temporal**, and **frontal**). A thick, tough outer layer and thinner, brittle inner layer provide strength and elasticity, yet fractures and anterior/inferior bony ridges can lacerate, bruise, bleed, or swell brain tissue within a non-expanding space (risking increased intracranial pressure, unresponsiveness, or death).
   - *Face:* Located between brow and chin with **14 bones**:
     - **13 immovable/interlocking bones:** **Orbits** (eye sockets), **nasal bones**, **maxillae** (fixed upper jaw), and **zygomatic bones** (cheekbones)
     - **1 freely movable bone:** Horseshoe-shaped lower jaw (**mandible**) attached on hinge joints
2. **Spinal Column (Vertebral Column):** Principal body support consisting of **33** irregularly shaped **vertebrae** bound by strong ligaments and separated by fluid-filled elastic cartilage *intervertebral disks* (shock absorbers/movers vulnerable to twisting, grinding, or improper lifting); houses and protects the spinal cord.
   - *Assessment Pearl:* Spinal cord damage typically causes sensation or motor loss *distal* to the injury, whereas vertebral column injury usually causes pain alone.

| Spinal Division | Vertebrae | Anatomical Region & Key Clinical Notes |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Cervical Spine** | `C1–C7` (7) | Neck; most prone to injury |
| **Thoracic Spine** | `T1–T12` (12) | Upper back; 12 pairs of thoracic ribs attach posteriorly |
| **Lumbar Spine** | `L1–L5` (5) | Lower back; least mobile (most lower-back injuries involve muscles rather than vertebrae) |
| **Sacral Spine** *(Sacrum)* | `S1–S5` (5 fused) | Fused together to form the rigid posterior wall of the pelvis |
| **Coccyx** | 4 fused | Most distal end of spine (tailbone); lacks typical vertebral protrusions |

3. **Thorax:** Chest formed by **24 ribs** (12 pairs attached posteriorly by ligaments to `T1–T12`), **sternum**, and thoracic spine:
   - *Ribs:*
     - Pairs **1–7** (*true ribs*): Attach directly to the sternum by cartilage.
     - Pairs **8–10** (*false ribs*): Attach via cartilage to the ribs above.
     - Pairs **11–12** (*floating ribs* / also classified under *false ribs*): Do not attach anteriorly to the sternum.
   - *Sternum (Breastbone):* Flat, narrow anterior chest bone divided into three parts:
     - Superior **manubrium** (articulates with the clavicle and first ribs)
     - Middle **body** (attaches ribs)
     - Inferior **xiphoid process**
4. **Pelvis:** Doughnut-shaped structure forming the floor of the abdominal cavity, supporting the intestines, and housing the bladder, rectum, and internal reproductive organs:
   - Includes the sacrum and coccyx posteriorly
   - **Iliac crests** ("wings") on each lateral side
   - **Pubis** in the anterior and inferior portion
   - **Ischium** in the posterior and inferior portion
5. **Lower Extremities (Hip to Toes):**
   - *Hip joint:* Pelvic **acetabulum** socket + rounded head of **femur** (thighbone).
   - *Knee joint:* Flat distal femur + proximal **tibia** form a hinged joint protected anteriorly by the triangular **patella** (kneecap, prone to bruising/fracture from blows or falls).
   - *Lower leg:* Weight-bearing anterior/medial **tibia** (shinbone; distal end forms **medial malleolus**) and lateral, smaller **fibula** (distal end forms **lateral malleolus** with tibia to create the ankle socket).
   - *Foot:* Proximal **tarsals** including **calcaneus** (heel bone), **5 metatarsals** (arch of foot), and **14 toe phalanges** per foot (**2** in the big toe, **3** in each other toe).
6. **Upper Extremities (Shoulder to Fingers):**
   - *Shoulder girdle:* **Clavicle** (collarbone) + **scapula** (shoulder blade) with lateral triangular tip **acromion**; ball-and-socket shoulder joint (humeral head in shallow scapular cup) offers the widest mobility and easiest dislocation.
   - *Arm & forearm:* **Humerus** (largest upper-extremity bone); hinged elbow joint formed by distal humerus + proximal lateral/thumb-side **radius** (upper two-thirds muscle-sheathed, distal third palpable) and medial/pinky-side **ulna** (with posterior **olecranon** bony prominence).
   - *Wrist & hand:* **8 carpals** (wrist), **metacarpals** (hand), and **phalanges** (**3** per finger, **2** per thumb).

#### B. Joints, Movements, Bone Hemorrhage & Muscle Types
- **Joint Mobility:** Immovable (skull), slightly movable (spine), or freely movable (synovial cavity, synovial membrane, articular cartilage, wrapped in ligaments).
- **Joint Movements:**
  - **Flexion:** Bending toward the body or decreasing the angle between bones/parts.
  - **Extension:** Straightening away from the body or increasing the angle between bones/parts.
  - **Abduction:** Movement away from the midline.
  - **Adduction:** Movement toward the midline.
  - **Circumduction:** Conical movement through 360° (combination of flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, as at the shoulder).
  - **Pronation:** Turning the forearm so the palm faces toward the back.
  - **Supination:** Turning the forearm so the palm faces toward the front.

| Joint Type | Permitted Motion | Anatomical Examples |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Ball-and-Socket Joint** | Widest range of motion (flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, rotation) | Shoulder, hip |
| **Hinged Joint** | Flexion and extension in one plane | Elbow (forward), knee (backward), finger |
| **Pivot Joint** | Turning / rotational motion | `C1–C2` (head/neck), wrist |
| **Gliding Joint** | Flat sliding of one bone across another | Small bones in hands and feet |
| **Saddle Joint** | Limited combinations of movement along perpendicular planes | Ankle (foot turns inward slightly while moving up/down) |
| **Condyloid Joint** | Modified ball-and-socket permitting limited two-direction motion without full rotation | Wrist (up/down and side-to-side) |

- **Bone Injury & Hemorrhage Risk:** Fractures (loss of bone continuity) can injure adjacent nerves, vessels, and soft tissues and rupture rich intraosseous blood vessels in compact bone, marrow cavity, and spongy bone:
  - *Femur fracture:* **1,000 to 2,000 mL** blood loss
  - *Pelvic fracture:* Can lose up to the entire **~5 L** (`70–80 mL/kg`) blood volume, causing life-threatening shock

| Muscle Type | Control & Structure | Location & Key Physiological Role |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Skeletal Muscle** *(Voluntary)* | Consciously controlled; mostly tendon-attached to bone (broad/flat in trunk, spindle-like in limbs) | Locomotion, chewing, swallowing, talking, eye movement |
| **Smooth Muscle** *(Involuntary)* | Automatic rhythmic wavelike contractions without conscious control | Hollow organs, ducts, respiratory tract, intestines, urinary tract, blood vessels (*vasoconstriction* vs. *vasodilation*) |
| **Cardiac Muscle** | Involuntary striated meshwork with **automaticity** (generates impulses independent of CNS) | Found exclusively in the walls of the heart; cannot tolerate even brief interruption of coronary blood supply |

### II. The Respiratory System
- **Four Basic Functions:**
  1. **Respiration:** Gas exchange of oxygen (O2) and carbon dioxide (CO2) across alveolar, capillary, and cellular membranes.
  2. **Ventilation:** Mechanical movement of air into and out of the lungs via pressure changes inside the chest.
  3. **Oxygenation:** Movement of O2 down its concentration gradient into the blood and metabolizing cells, paired with CO2 removal.
  4. **Acid–Base Buffering:** Increasing respiratory rate and depth to exhale excess CO2 and reduce acid load alongside the kidneys and bicarbonate buffer system. *(Brain cells typically begin dying within **~5 minutes** if oxygen is cut off.)*

#### A. Upper & Lower Airway Structures
- **Upper Airway (Ends at the Cricoid Cartilage):**
  - *Nares & Nasal Cavity:* Warms, moistens, and filters inhaled air over mucous membranes and three bony scroll-like **conchae** ridges.
  - *Mouth / Oral Cavity:* Secondary air entryway.
  - *Pharynx (Throat):* Extends from nasal/oral cavities to larynx and esophagus; divided into three sections:
    1. **Nasopharynx:** Posterior nasal cavity to soft palate.
    2. **Oropharynx:** Soft palate to epiglottis (with mouth as anterior opening).
    3. **Laryngopharynx (Hypopharynx):** Hyoid bone at base of tongue to openings of **esophagus** and larynx.
  - *Larynx (Voice Box):* Located inferior to pharynx and superior to trachea:
    - **Thyroid cartilage** (*Adam’s Apple*): Bulky anterior cartilage.
    - **Cricoid cartilage:** Inferior boundary of the larynx and the **only full cartilaginous ring** of the upper airway (palpated immediately below thyroid cartilage).
    - **Vocal cords & Glottis:** Superior *false vocal cords* (vestibular folds) and inferior *true vocal cords* (vocal folds) vibrate to produce speech; the space between true vocal cords is the **glottis (glottic opening)**.
    - **Epiglottis:** Leaf-shaped cartilaginous flap posterior to tongue root that tips posteriorly during swallowing to cover the larynx and direct food/liquid into the esophagus; loss of protective reflexes in altered/unresponsive states risks **aspiration**.
- **Lower Airway, Lungs & Pleura:**
  - *Trachea (Windpipe):* Extends from larynx to ~`T5` (carina); reinforced anteriorly and laterally by **15–20** C-shaped cartilage rings bridged posteriorly by smooth **trachealis muscle**.
  - *Bronchi & Bronchioles:* Right and left mainstem **bronchi** branch into smooth-muscle **bronchioles**, which undergo *bronchoconstriction* or *bronchodilation* to alter airway resistance and work of breathing.
  - *Alveoli & Respiratory Membrane:* Microscopic air sacs surrounded by capillary networks forming the **respiratory membrane** (site of O2/CO2 diffusion), lined with **surfactant** to maintain surface tension and prevent alveolar collapse (critical in premature infants and drowning).
  - *Lungs & Pleura:* Lobed principal organs of respiration enclosed by **visceral pleura** (covers lungs) and **parietal pleura** (lines chest wall), separated by the negative-pressure **pleural cavity** containing lubricating fluid; dome-shaped **diaphragm** separates thoracic and abdominal cavities.

#### B. Ventilation Mechanics & Breathing Assessment
- **Mechanics of Breathing:**
  - *Inhalation (Active):* **Diaphragm** (provides **~60–70%** of breathing effort via phrenic nerve `C3–C5`) and **intercostal muscles** (between ribs, providing **~30–40%**) contract, expanding the thorax and creating negative intrathoracic pressure so air flows in. *(`C3–C5` spinal cord injury can paralyze the diaphragm and require artificial ventilation.)*
  - *Exhalation (Passive):* Diaphragm and intercostals relax, decreasing thoracic volume and creating positive pressure to expel air.
- **Adequate vs. Inadequate Breathing:**
  - *Adequate Breathing:* Requires both normal **respiratory rate** and adequate **tidal volume** (regular breath volume with effortless ~1-inch adult chest expansion):

| Age Group | Normal Respiratory Rate Range | Average / Notes |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Adults** | **8–24 breaths/min** | Average **12–20 breaths/min** |
| **Older Adults** | — | Average **20–22 breaths/min** |
| **Children** | **18–37 breaths/min** | Varies by age; faster than adults |
| **Infants** | **30–60 breaths/min** | Rate **>=60/min** indicates inadequate breathing |

  - *Inadequate Breathing Red Flags:* Abnormal rate or rhythm, diminished or absent breath sounds, unequal or shallow chest expansion, pale/bluish (cyanotic) or cool/clammy skin, accessory muscle retractions/neck muscle use, nasal flaring, infant "seesaw" breathing or head bobbing, newborn grunting, or **agonal respirations** (infrequent gasping with no pattern)—requiring immediate artificial ventilation.

### III. The Circulatory System
- **Core Functions:** Closed heart–vessel–blood system providing cellular perfusion (O2 and nutrient delivery + CO2 and waste removal), gas transport to/from alveoli, blood reservoir, acid–base buffering, immune delivery, clotting, and temperature regulation.

#### A. Heart Anatomy, Blood Flow & Electrical Conduction
- **Structure:** Fist-sized muscular organ in the thorax (two-thirds left of midline, apex resting just above diaphragm) enclosed by the lubricating *pericardium*, containing four chambers (**atria** and **ventricles**) and four one-way **valves**:
  1. **Right Atrium:** Receives O2-poor blood from upper body via *superior vena cava* and lower body via *inferior vena cava* (**venae cavae**).
  2. **Tricuspid Valve:** Prevents backflow into right atrium as blood enters the **right ventricle**.
  3. **Pulmonary Valve:** Prevents backflow into right ventricle as blood is pumped into the **pulmonary arteries** (the *only arteries* carrying O2-depleted blood) toward the lungs.
  4. **Left Atrium:** Receives freshly oxygenated blood from the lungs via the **pulmonary veins** (the *only veins* carrying O2-rich blood).
  5. **Bicuspid (Mitral) Valve:** Prevents backflow into left atrium as blood enters the **left ventricle**.
  6. **Aortic Valve:** Prevents backflow into left ventricle as blood is pumped into the **aorta** (major artery leaving the heart, dividing into iliac arteries near the navel).
- **Conduction System & Coronary Supply:**
  - *Electrical pathway:* Impulses originate at the *sinoatrial (SA) node* → *atrioventricular (AV) node* → *bundle of His* → right and left bundle branches → *Purkinje fibers* across the *myocardium*.
  - *Coronary arteries:* Branch directly off the aorta to supply oxygenated blood to the heart muscle itself.

#### B. Blood Vessels, Blood Components & Hemodynamics
- **Vascular Pathway:**
  - **Arteries** (carry blood away from heart) → **arterioles** (smallest arterial branches) → **capillaries** (single-cell-layer exchange vessels) → **venules** (smallest venous branches) → **veins** (carry blood toward heart with one-way valves).
  - *Major Arteries:* **Carotid** (neck/brain), **femoral** (thigh/groin/leg), **dorsalis pedis** (top of foot), **posterior tibial** (medial ankle), **brachial** (upper arm; used for BP and infant pulse), and **radial** (distal forearm; commonly palpated).
- **Four Components of Blood:**
  1. **Red Blood Cells (RBCs):** Contain hemoglobin to carry O2 to cells and CO2 away from cells.
  2. **White Blood Cells (WBCs):** Defend the body against infection.
  3. **Platelets:** Essential components for blood clot formation (hemostasis).
  4. **Plasma:** Liquid portion of blood carrying dissolved nutrients/wastes and proteins such as *albumin* (which exerts oncotic pull to retain fluid inside vessels).
- **Pulse, Blood Pressure & Fluid Balance:**
  - **Pulse:** Wave of blood propelled through arteries by left ventricular contraction (*central:* carotid, femoral; *peripheral:* radial, brachial, posterior tibial, dorsalis pedis; *apical:* heard over heart apex, reflecting mechanical contraction and persisting even when peripheral/carotid pulses weaken or vanish in severe blood loss).
  - **Blood Pressure:** Force exerted by blood on interior vessel walls:
    - *Systolic blood pressure:* Pressure when the left ventricle contracts.
    - *Diastolic blood pressure:* Pressure when the left ventricle is at rest (reflects arterial resistance; falls with vasodilation).
  - **Hydrostatic Pressure & Edema:** Elevated **hydrostatic pressure** ("push" force against vessel walls) forces water out of capillaries into interstitial spaces, causing **edema** (swelling)—which impairs gas exchange when fluid accumulates between alveoli and pulmonary capillaries.
- **Gas Transport, Perfusion & Cellular Metabolism:**
  - *O2 Transport:* **~97%** bound to hemoglobin; **~3%** dissolved in plasma.
  - *CO2 Transport:* **~70%** converted to bicarbonate; **~23%** bound to hemoglobin; **~7%** dissolved in plasma.
  - **Perfusion vs. Hypoperfusion:** Constant adequate capillary circulation (**perfusion**) delivers O2, glucose, and nutrients and removes wastes; inadequate perfusion (**hypoperfusion / shock**) causes cellular, tissue, and organ dysfunction.
  - **Aerobic vs. Anaerobic Metabolism:**
    - *Aerobic metabolism* (with O2): Produces abundant cellular energy plus water, heat, and CO2.
    - *Anaerobic metabolism* (without O2): Produces minimal energy and accumulates toxic acid—explaining why a quiet, hypoperfused patient lacking energy to yell is a higher-priority red flag than a loud patient yelling with intact perfusion.

### IV. The Nervous System
- **Central Nervous System (CNS):** Brain (~3 lb in adults, dependent on continuous O2 and glucose across the blood–brain barrier) and ~18-inch spinal cord (impulse conduction), protected by meninges and *cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)*:
  - **Cerebrum:** Largest part of the brain; controls sensation, thought, speech, associative memory, emotions, personality, and conscious motor control.
  - **Cerebellum:** Located beneath the cerebrum; coordinates balance, body movements, and muscle coordination using visual/vestibular inputs and maintains partial muscle tone.
  - **Brainstem:** Funnel-shaped inferior brain made up of the midbrain (*mesencephalon*), *pons*, and *medulla oblongata* (brain's connection to spinal cord, housing the *respiratory center*, *cardiac center* for HR/contractility, and *vasomotor center* for vessel tone/BP).
- **Peripheral Nervous System (PNS) & Functional Divisions:**
  - *Structural:* Nerves outside the brain and spinal cord—*afferent* nerves (carry sensory input to CNS) and *efferent* nerves (carry motor commands from CNS).
  - *Functional:* **Voluntary (somatic) nervous system** (conscious skeletal muscle movement) vs. **autonomic nervous system** (involuntary smooth muscle and gland control, divided into *sympathetic* "fight-or-flight" and *parasympathetic* normal/restorative balance).
- **Consciousness:** Requires an intact *reticular activating system (RAS)* in the brainstem ("wake/sleep" or "on/off" center) and at least one functioning *cerebral hemisphere*; dysfunction of the RAS or both cerebral hemispheres causes unconsciousness.

### V. The Endocrine System & Adrenergic Receptors

| Endocrine Gland | Location & Core Hormonal Functions |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Pineal Gland** | Brain; secretes melatonin (inhibits reproductive functions; circadian regulation) |
| **Pituitary Gland** | Base of brain; "master gland" regulating growth, thyroid/parathyroid, pancreas, gonads, fat/protein/glucose reactions, and urinary excretion |
| **Thyroid Gland** | Anterior neck; regulates metabolism, growth/development, and nervous system activity |
| **Parathyroid Glands** | Posterior to thyroid; regulate calcium and phosphorus bone metabolism |
| **Adrenal Glands** | Superior to kidneys; secrete epinephrine (adrenaline) and norepinephrine, delay muscle fatigue, increase sugar storage, and regulate kidney function and salt/water balance |
| **Islets of Langerhans** *(Pancreas)* | Pancreas; secrete *insulin* (facilitates cellular glucose entry) and *glucagon* (raises blood glucose) |
| **Gonads** *(Ovaries & Testes)* | Pelvis / scrotum; reproductive hormones and secondary sex characteristics |
| **Thymus Gland** | Superior neck/chest; secretes *thymosin* for immune system development/maturation |

- **Epinephrine & Norepinephrine Adrenergic Receptor Actions:**
  - **Alpha1:** Vasoconstriction (especially skin vessels, shunting blood to core and making skin cool/pale) and sweat gland activation (moist/clammy skin).
  - **Alpha2:** Helps regulate alpha1 release.
  - **Beta1:** Increases heart rate, myocardial contractile force, and electrical conduction speed.
  - **Beta2:** Smooth muscle dilation (bronchioles and certain blood vessels).
  - *Clinical Comparison:* **Epinephrine** stimulates alpha1, alpha2, beta1, and beta2 receptors (used in severe allergic reactions to reverse vasodilation and bronchoconstriction); **norepinephrine** acts primarily on alpha1/alpha2 with trace beta1/beta2 effects (producing prominent pale, cool, clammy skin signs).

### VI. Integumentary, Digestive, Urinary & Reproductive Systems
- **Integumentary System (Skin):**
  - *Functions:* Environmental and pathogen barrier, temperature regulation, sensory reception (heat, cold, touch, pain, pressure), and water/electrolyte (`Na+`, `Cl-`) balance.
  - *Three Layers & Accessory Structures:*
    1. **Epidermis:** Outermost layer composed of four cell layers (outer two dead/sloughing cells; deepest layers contain *melanin* pigment).
    2. **Dermis:** Thicker dense connective tissue giving elasticity and strength; contains blood vessels, hair follicles, sweat glands, oil glands, and sensory nerves.
    3. **Subcutaneous Layer:** Fatty tissue of variable thickness beneath the dermis.
    - *Accessory structures:* Nails, hair, sweat glands, and oil glands.
- **Digestive System:** Alimentary tract and accessory organs (most inside abdominal cavity except mouth and **esophagus**):
  - *Stomach:* Main digestive organ; secretes gastric juices to begin food breakdown.
  - *Pancreas:* Inferior/posterior to stomach; secretes *pancreatic juices* (fat/starch/protein digestion) plus insulin via islets of Langerhans.
  - *Liver:* Largest solid abdominal organ in RUQ beneath diaphragm; produces bile (fat digestion), stores sugars, makes immune/clotting/plasma components, and detoxifies digestion byproducts.
  - *Spleen:* LUQ solid organ; filters blood, acts as an emergency blood reservoir, and supports immunity (no digestive function).
  - *Gallbladder:* Bile duct pouch storing bile and releasing it into the small intestine when food enters.
  - *Small intestine:* Comprises **duodenum**, **jejunum**, and **ileum**; completes digestion and absorbs nutrients into the bloodstream.
  - *Large intestine (colon):* Absorbs water from unabsorbed matter to form stool passed via rectum and anus.
  - *Digestion Processes:* Mechanical (chewing, swallowing, *peristalsis*, *defecation*) and chemical (*enzymes* breaking carbohydrates → glucose, fats → fatty acids, proteins → amino acids).
- **Urinary (Renal) System:** Two *kidneys* (filter blood waste, control fluid/acid–base balance, regulate BP, support RBC production), two *ureters* (transport urine to bladder), one *urinary bladder* (stores urine), and one *urethra* (excretes urine outside body).
- **Reproductive Systems:**
  - *Male:* Two *testes* (produce sperm and *testosterone*), duct system (*epididymis*, *vas deferens*), accessory glands (including *prostate gland*, seminal vesicles, bulbourethral gland), and *penis*.
  - *Female:* Two *ovaries* (produce ova and *estrogen*/*progesterone*), two *fallopian tubes* (transport ovum to uterus), *uterus* (fetal development), *vagina* (birth canal/intercourse), and external genitals (*vulva* protecting vaginal orifice and urinary meatus; breasts produce milk).

## 3. Pediatric vs. Geriatric Anatomy & Physiology

### Pediatric Anatomy & Physiology (Infants & Children)
- **Special Health Care Needs:** Differences may vary considerably from typical developmental norms—utilize the caretaker to assist with assessment.

| System / Region | Key Pediatric Differences & Clinical Implications |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Airway** *(Primary Area of Difference)* | Proportionally larger tongue (high occlusion risk in AMS); newborn trachea is only **4 to 5 mm** wide (one-third of a dime / drinking straw size vs. **20 mm** in adults) and highly pliable (occludes with neck hyperextension or flexion); pressing soft tissue under chin pushes tongue into pharynx; **obligate nose breathers**; narrowest point is at **cricoid cartilage** until **~6 years** (vs. vocal cords in adults); higher **epiglottis** increases aspiration risk |
| **Head** | Proportionally large head/occiput flexes neck forward when supine → place shoulder padding for neutral alignment until **8–9 years** (adult proportions at **age 10**); infants **<6–7 months** cannot support head; **posterior fontanelle** closes by **3 months**, **anterior fontanelle** closes at **12–18 months** (do not press; rely on mental status for ICP and tachycardia/mottling/tears/wet diapers for dehydration) |
| **Chest & Lungs** | Pliable, horizontal ribs offer less organ protection (higher internal organ injury risk) and minimal AP expansion; fragile lung tissue (higher pulmonary contusion and pneumothorax risk); **abdominal breathing** pattern; early retractions (intercostal in mild distress → suprasternal, supraclavicular, and sternal in severe distress); **grunting** ("laryngeal braking") maintains positive airway pressure to keep alveoli open but quickly exhausts infants |
| **Respiratory Rate & Fatigue** | Rate **>=60 breaths/min** indicates inadequate breathing (subsequent drop signals impending respiratory failure); stress-induced apnea; children **<5 years** breathe **2 to 3 times faster** than adults; diaphragmatic fatigue leads to lethargy and loss of normal "attitude" (critical illness sign) |
| **Cardiovascular** | Smaller circulating blood volume; cannot meaningfully increase stroke volume—compensate via **tachycardia** and **peripheral vasoconstriction** (mottled/cool extremities); maintain BP until **>30%** blood volume is lost, then crash suddenly; bradycardia is a **late** hypoxia sign in infants/children, but the **initial** hypoxia sign in **newborns** |
| **Abdomen & Extremities** | Underdeveloped abdominal muscles + exposed liver and spleen until puberty; immature bones sustain **greenstick fractures** (bend and splinter); cephalocaudal (head-to-toe) motor development |
| **Metabolism & Skin** | Resting metabolic rate consumes O2 and glucose **2 to 3 times faster** (faster CNS hypoxic injury, earlier CO toxicity, rapid **hypoglycemia** with twitching/seizures/limpness/apnea); high surface-area-to-mass ratio, thin skin, minimal fat, and immature hypothalamus cause rapid **hypothermia** |

### Geriatric Anatomy, Physiology & Effects of Aging
- **Demographics & Clinical Masking:** Older adults (**65+ years**) are the fastest-growing U.S. group (**54.1 million / 16%** in 2019 → **81 million / 22%** projected by 2040; projected **>50%** of EMS calls by **2030**). Normal aging begins around **age 30**, reducing physiological reserves, while comorbidities and polypharmacy mask acute emergencies.

| Body System | Age-Related Changes & Clinical Impact |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Cardiovascular** | Valve calcification causing **stenosis** (narrowing) or regurgitation (mitral > tricuspid → pulmonary edema); **cardiac hypertrophy** with fibrous tissue reduces stroke volume and cardiac output; fewer pacemaker cells and blunted catecholamine response limit HR rise; **dysrhythmias**; **arteriosclerosis** raises afterload/systolic BP while blunted **baroreceptors** predispose to **orthostatic hypotension** |
| **Respiratory** | Weakening respiratory muscles and calcified rib-sternum joints stiffen chest wall; new alveoli stop forming around **age 20** and declining surface area impairs gas diffusion; blunted **chemoreceptors** reduce hypoxia/hypercapnia drive; weaker bronchiolar smooth muscle causes air trapping (airflow declines after **age 30**); weaker cough, reduced cilia, and dehydration raise pneumonia risk |
| **Neurologic & Sensory** | Decreasing brain mass increases CSF volume; plaques/tangles and neuron loss (starting **mid-20s**) slow reflexes and impair proprioception/balance; blunted hunger, thirst, and thermoregulation; vision loss (**cataracts** [lens clouding], **glaucoma** [high intraocular pressure], **macular degeneration** [central vision loss], **retinal detachment**); **presbycusis** (high-frequency bilateral hearing loss—keep hearing aids in/on, do not shout); **peripheral neuropathy** can mask MI pain in diabetics |
| **Gastrointestinal** | Reduced taste/smell, tooth loss, and lower saliva; weaker esophageal sphincter (reflux) and lower stomach acid; smaller liver with reduced blood flow/enzymes impairs drug metabolism; slower peristalsis and intestinal degeneration cause malnutrition, constipation, impaction, or incontinence |
| **Endocrine** | Reduced insulin sensitivity raises type 2 diabetes risk; elevated norepinephrine/vasopressin, altered renin/aldosterone/ANH, and reduced beta-receptor sensitivity contribute to hypertension, orthostatic hypotension, and fluid imbalance; lower thyroxine conversion affects thermoregulation and HR |
| **Musculoskeletal** | **Osteoporosis** (brittle bones, slow healing); narrowing disks cause **kyphosis** in **2 of every 3** elderly patients (mostly thoracic—requires extra void padding for spine motion restriction); thinner cartilage, thicker synovial fluid, and muscle loss raise fall risk |
| **Renal** | Loss of functional **nephrons** (kidney weight drops by **~one-third**) and reduced renal blood flow impair filtration and make **drug toxicity** common alongside polypharmacy |
| **Integumentary** | Thinning skin, weaker dermal–epidermal bond, slower cell turnover, reduced perspiration, and dulled touch cause skin tears/sores, slow healing, infection risk, and vulnerability to both hypothermia and hyperthermia |

## 4. Medical Terminology: Word Parts & Decoding Rules

### Word Construction & Breakdown Rules
- **Three Word-Part Types:**
  1. **Combining Form:** Root + combining vowel (usually `o`, sometimes `a`, `e`, or `i`) supplying the word's core medical meaning; written with a trailing hyphen (`cardi/o-`).
  2. **Suffix:** Added to the end of a combining form to modify or add specific meaning.
  3. **Prefix:** Added before a combining form to modify direction, time, or orientation.
- **Joining Rules & Breakdown Examples:** Drop the hyphen (and usually the combining vowel before a suffix starting with a vowel) when joining parts:
  - `cardi/o-` (*heart*) + `-logy` (*study of*) = **cardiology** (*study of the heart*)
  - `peri-` (*around*) + `cardi/o-` (*heart*) + `-um` (*structure*) = **pericardium** (*structure surrounding the heart*)
  - `my/o-` (*muscle*) + `cardi/o-` (*heart*) + `-al` (*pertaining to*) = **myocardial** (*pertaining to heart muscle*)
  - `bronch/o-` (*bronchus*) + `-itis` (*inflammation of*) = **bronchitis** (*inflammation of the bronchi*)
  - `hepat/o-` (*liver*) + `-ic` (*pertaining to*) = **hepatic** (*pertaining to the liver*)
  - `laryng/o-` (*larynx*) + `trache/o-` (*trachea*) + `bronch/o-` (*bronchus*) + `-itis` (*inflammation of*) = **laryngotracheobronchitis**
  - `endo-` (*within*) + `trache/o-` (*trachea*) + `-al` (*pertaining to*) = **endotracheal**
  - `hypo-` (*below normal*) + `vol/o-` (*volume*) + `-emic` (*pertaining to blood*) = **hypovolemic**
  - `hyper-` (*above normal*) + `glyc/o-` (*glucose/sugar*) + `-emia` (*condition of blood*) = **hyperglycemia**
  - `a-` (*without/no*) + `pne/o-` (*breathing*) + `-a` (*condition*) = **apnea**
  - `tachy-` (*fast*) + `cardi/o-` (*heart*) + `-ia` (*condition*) = **tachycardia**
  - `brady-` (*slow*) + `pne/o-` (*breathing*) + `-a` (*condition*) = **bradypnea**

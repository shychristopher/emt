# Chapter 3: Medical, Legal, and Ethical Issues
- **1. Key Terms & Professional Foundations**
  - **Key Terms (Grouped by Concept):**
    - *Foundations & Duties:* scope of practice, standard of care, duty to act, Good Samaritan law
    - *Consent & Refusal:* consent, informed consent, expressed consent, implied consent, minor consent, involuntary consent
    - *Advance Directives:* advance directive, living will, durable power of attorney, do not resuscitate (DNR) order, physician orders for life-sustaining treatment (POLST), medical orders for life-sustaining treatment (MOLST)
    - *Liability & Torts:* tort, negligence, proximate cause, intentional tort, abandonment, assault, battery, false imprisonment, defamation, slander, libel
    - *Privacy & Federal Regulations:* Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA), Consolidated Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act (COBRA), Emergency Medical Treatment and Active Labor Act (EMTALA)
  - **Core Legal & Ethical Responsibilities:**
    - EMT owes legal and ethical duties to **the patient**, **the medical director**, and **the public**
    - Thorough documentation + acting within scope of practice, local protocols, proper consent, and duty to act protects against successful lawsuits
  - **Scope of Practice vs. Standard of Care:**
    - **Scope of Practice:** Actions and care EMTs are *legally allowed* to perform (defined by state laws, NHTSA *National EMS Scope of Practice Model*, and *National EMS Education Standards*); answers *"Was the EMT allowed by law to do what was done?"* (e.g., oxygen = legal; suturing = illegal even if trained); defines boundaries, not protocols or standard of care
    - **Standard of Care:** Care expected from a reasonably prudent EMT with similar training in a similar situation ("reasonable person standard"); evaluates (1) right assessment/care and (2) proper performance; defined by EMT textbooks, regional peers, local/state protocols, NHTSA standards, and system SOPs; falling below constitutes **negligence**
  - **Duty to Act (5 Dimensions):**
    - **On-Duty:** Legally obligated to respond and provide care to any patient requiring and consenting to care, regardless of whether you think they need an ambulance
    - **Off-Duty:** Most states impose no legal obligation unless state law mandates aid; however, publicly responding to a page or stopping at a scene creates a **duty to act** (cannot leave until equal/higher training takes over; legally responsible for any patient property picked up)
    - **Duty to Patients:** Advocate for patient, protect privacy/confidentiality, lessen suffering, provide complete physical/emotional care, do no further harm
    - **Duty to Self:** Obtain/maintain current credentials and licensure, maintain skill proficiency, maintain physical and mental fitness
    - **Duty to Partner:** Ensure partner is physically/mentally fit; report to supervisor if partner reports under influence of alcohol or drugs
    - **Duty Regarding Equipment:** Ensure equipment works properly at shift start; malfunction from poor maintenance or missing equipment constitutes negligence
  - **Good Samaritan Laws & Legal Protections:**
    - **Good Samaritan Laws:** Originated in California (**1959**); protects unpaid individuals acting in good faith at emergency scenes from civil damages unless **gross negligence** (markedly below standard of care / willful or wanton intent to harm); many states grant partial immunity to EMTs and medical direction personnel; does *not* prevent being sued, only helps win if standard of care was met
    - **Sovereign (Governmental) Immunity:** Protects government-operated EMS systems from civil liability lawsuits (does *not* protect private EMS companies)
    - **Statute of Limitations:** State time limit (usually in years) to file a negligence claim after injury or discovery
    - **Contributory Negligence:** Patient contributed through their own negligence to the injury/damage claimed
  - **Medical Direction:**
    - Legal right to function prehospital depends on **medical direction**
    - Follow approved standing orders/protocols; communicate clearly via cell phone, video chat, telephone, or radio; follow orders and consult whenever scope/direction is uncertain; note law enforcement/EMS body cameras often record interactions
  - **Ethical Responsibilities:**
    - *Ethics* (study of morality) vs. *Morals* (concepts of right and wrong) vs. *Code of Ethics* (rules of ideal conduct; NAEMT issued EMT Code of Ethics in **1978**)
    - Core duties: serve all patients with human dignity regardless of background, maintain skill mastery, stay current, review performance, report honestly, protect confidentiality, collaborate with health care team
    - *Ethical/Moral/Legal Conflicts:* e.g., terminally ill patient refusing care with an expired DNR, mass-casualty triage rationing, or wilderness cardiac arrest → follow local protocols/laws and contact medical direction

- **2. Patient Consent, Advance Directives & Refusal of Care**
  - **General Principles of Consent:**
    - Conscious adult with capacity has legal right to accept or refuse care
    - **Standard 5-Step Approach:** (1) Introduce yourself → (2) state intent to assess, treat, and transport → (3) ask if patient understands and has questions → (4) request explicit permission before proceeding (even if patient called 911; extra caution for unproven research) → (5) listen carefully and never assume blanket consent
    - Touching body/clothing or treating without specific consent can constitute **battery**
  - **Four Types of Consent:**
    - **Expressed Consent:** Required from conscious, legal-age adult with rational capacity after being informed in understandable terms of procedures and risks; verbal confirmation preferred (nonverbal nodding acceptable); document approval
    - **Implied Consent (Emergency Doctrine):** Assumed in true emergencies posing significant risk of death, disability, or deterioration when patient is unresponsive, irrational (e.g., head injury), or becomes incapacitated after initial refusal
    - **Minor Consent:** Required from parent, legal guardian, or authorized caregiver (minor = under **18** or **21** per state law)
      - *6 Conditions for Emergent Minor Implied Consent (Parent/Guardian Unavailable):* (1) emergent threat to life/health; (2) legal guardian unavailable/unable; (3) care/transport cannot be safely delayed; (4) treat only immediate life/health threats; (5) when in doubt, act in minor's best interest; (6) contact medical direction if needed
      - *Emancipated Minor:* Married, pregnant, parent, armed forces, financially independent living away from home, or court decree → no parent/guardian consent needed
    - **Involuntary Consent:** Applies to mentally incompetent adults or individuals in law enforcement custody/incarcerated; obtained from third party with legal authority (legal guardian, law enforcement officer, court officer)
  - **End-of-Life Care Advance Directives:**
    - **Advance Directives:** Written instructions made in advance (*Patient Self-Determination Act*) specifying desired care and surrogate decision-makers; rules vary by state
    - **Living Will:** Directs end-of-life care when terminally ill or permanently comatose; requires **two physicians** to confirm incapacity and terminal/comatose state; may still allow insulin, antibiotics, food, or pain meds; can be rescinded anytime
    - **Durable Power of Attorney (Medical Power of Attorney / Health Care Proxy):** Names a proxy/agent to make health care decisions if patient loses capacity; requires **physician** certification of incapacity; ends if patient regains capacity
    - **Do Not Resuscitate (DNR) Orders:** Standardized form signed by physician and patient prohibiting resuscitation once pulseless and apneic
      - *DNR Comfort Care (DNRCC):* Allows comfort measures (oxygen, bleeding control, pain meds) without resuscitation upon arrest
      - *Verification & Non-Arrest Rules:* Cannot act on DNR until cardiac arrest (if pulse present, provide standard care/comfort measures); must confirm valid physical DNR (form, wallet card, bracelet)—never rely solely on family/staff word; if missing or questionable, begin resuscitation and contact medical direction
    - **POLST (MOLST / MOST / POST / TPOPP):** For serious/terminal illness with life expectancy **<=1 year**; specifies pre-arrest treatment limits (e.g., meds allowed, no intubation/defibrillation); voluntary; signed by physician (or PA/NP in some states) with valid dates and contact info
    - **Field Ambiguity & NASEMSO Guidelines:** Reject vague phrases (*"no heroics"*); do not waste resuscitative time reading living wills; when validity or family conflict arises, initiate care, contact medical direction, and continue care until resolved; verify state form/construction/intactness and patient/physician ID per NASEMSO
  - **Refusing Treatment & Protecting Yourself:**
    - *Capacity* (medical ability to understand and decide rationally) vs. *Competence* (legal court status; lacking capacity without court ruling = *de facto incompetent*)
    - Altered mental status, mental illness, intoxication, impairment, or suicidal intent impairs capacity → use implied consent/protective care and consult medical direction
    - **7 Steps During a Refusal:** (1) Perform thorough physical/vital/neuro/mental assessment (or document declined assessment) → (2) re-persuade patient/family in plain language and have patient read back report → (3) confirm capacity (not suicidal/impaired) → (4) consult medical direction per protocol → (5) stay safe (call police if needed; balance avoiding abandonment vs. forcing care) → (6) give specific warning symptoms to call back for → (7) document thoroughly and get signed release + witness signature (or document witnessed refusal to sign)

- **3. Liability, Negligence & Intentional Torts**
  - **Criminal vs. Civil Liability:**
    - **Criminal Liability:** Government prosecutes EMT on behalf of public → fine or incarceration
    - **Civil Liability:** Individual (**plaintiff**) sues EMT, agency, supervisor, or medical director (**defendants**) for a **tort** (wrongful act/injury/damage) seeking monetary compensation
  - **Negligence (All 4 Elements Required):**
    - Tort with *no intent to harm* where breach of duty occurred:
      1. **Duty to Act:** Legal obligation existed (cannot terminate patient contact without consent)
      2. **Breach of Duty to Act:** Deviated from standard of care (*simple negligence* = mistake/omission; *gross negligence* = willful, wanton, reckless care—may trigger criminal charges)
      3. **Damages:** Real, demonstrable, legally recognizable injury occurred
      4. **Proximate Cause (Causation):** Injury resulted directly from EMT's negligence rather than trauma or underlying disease
    - *Associated Doctrines:* **Res ipsa loquitur** ("the thing speaks for itself") and **Negligence per se** (violating a statute, regulation, protocol, or scope of practice)
  - **Five Common EMS Intentional Torts (Knowingly Committed Civil Wrongs):**
    - **Abandonment:** Stopping care without transferring to an equal/higher trained professional; leaving patient in ED with only a clerk/secretary without oral handoff to qualified clinician; downgrading ALS patient to BLS inappropriately
    - **Assault:** Willful threat to inflict harm creating apprehension of bodily harm without physical contact (e.g., threatening with an epinephrine auto-injector)
    - **Battery:** Unlawfully touching a patient or providing care without consent (even with good intentions)
    - **False Imprisonment (Kidnapping):** Transporting or detaining a patient with capacity against their wishes (physical restraint not required)
    - **Defamation:** Damaging a person's character/reputation publicly (*Slander* = spoken; *Libel* = written or mass media)
    - *Best Defense:* Provide care to the best of your ability within your scope of practice and standard of care

- **4. Confidentiality, HIPAA, COBRA & EMTALA**
  - **Confidentiality & HIPAA (1996):**
    - Keep history, exam, and treatment strictly confidential across all call types; deliberate breach risks loss of certification/licensure and lawsuits
    - Written release from patient or verified legal guardian generally required before disclosure
    - **5 Permitted Disclosures Without Permission:** (1) Continuing care by another health care provider; (2) mandatory public health/government reporting (rape, child/elder abuse, neglect, domestic violence, communicable diseases, gunshots, stabbings, animal bites, suspicious burns); (3) police criminal investigation request; (4) third-party billing; (5) court subpoena
    - **HIPAA:** Federal privacy law giving patients control over health info; never discuss patients in public areas (e.g., ambulance bay); obtain signed privacy acknowledgment; consult agency **Privacy Officer** for breaches or third-party requests
  - **COBRA, EMTALA & Transport Protections:**
    - **COBRA & EMTALA ("anti-patient-dumping statute"):** Guarantee public access to emergency care regardless of ability to pay; transferring facilities must stabilize patients and ensure EMS crew has scope/capability for transfer (e.g., EMTs cannot manage continuous ECG or IV drug infusions); hospital-based ambulances cannot bypass closest appropriate hospital due to lack of insurance
    - **Emergency Transport:** Never choose destination by ability to pay or broadcast insurance status over radio; document any protocol/medical-direction/patient-requested bypass
    - **5 Interfacility Transfer Safeguards:** (1) Full condition report → (2) confirm care is within scope/capability → (3) signed informed transfer consent → (4) written certification of transfer (transferring physician name/address, receiving facility, reason) → (5) know and take quickest route

- **5. Special Legal & Field Situations**
  - **Organ Donors & Medical Identification:**
    - **Organ Donors:** Requires legal signed document (donor card, driver's license indicator); treat **patient first, organ donor last**; (1) identify potential donors, (2) notify medical direction and receiving ED, (3) provide supportive care (e.g., CPR) to preserve organs if harvesting attempted
    - **Medical ID Insignia & Alert Tattoos:** Check for ID tags/necklaces/bracelets/fitness bands (allergies, diabetes, epilepsy) and inspect wrists, arms, ankles, neckline, and chest for non-standardized medical alert tattoos (noting infection/healing risks in diabetics)
  - **Recognizing Death in the Field & NASEMSO Protocol (2022):**
    - General rule: begin resuscitation if patient is warm without obvious signs of death; **exception = accidental hypothermia** (cold, rigid patients are not dead until rewarmed)
    - **5 Presumptive Signs of Death:** (1) Absence of pulse, breathing, and breath sounds; (2) complete unresponsiveness to stimuli; (3) no eye movement or pupil response; (4) absence of BP; (5) no reflexes
    - **4 Obvious Signs of Death:** (1) Decapitation; (2) rigor mortis (**2–12 hours**); (3) decomposition; (4) dependent lividity
    - **NASEMSO Withholding Resuscitation Exclusions (excludes lightning, drowning, hypothermia):**
      - *8 Biological Conditions:* (1) Decapitation; (2) decomposition/putrefaction (`>=24 hours` prior); (3) torso transection; (4) incineration (**90% BSA** full-thickness burns/ash/charred skin); (5) injuries incompatible with life (massive crush, complete exsanguination, severe brain matter displacement); (6) futile/inhumane per protocol; (7) blunt/penetrating trauma apneic, pulseless, and no signs of life on arrival; (8) nontraumatic arrest with dependent lividity or rigor mortis
      - *OR Valid Actionable Order:* Intact, state-compliant DNR or POLST/MOLST with patient (and required physician) name
      - *Management:* With signs of life + DNR/POLST, provide standard care/comfort measures; if status is unclear, start CPR immediately and contact medical direction
    - **Medical Examiner / Coroner Cases:** Homicides, suicides, violent deaths (suspected child/elder abuse), crash deaths (auto, motorcycle, ATV, snowmobile), unusual scenes (burns, electrocution), SIDS, DOA per local protocol
  - **Crime Scenes & Evidence Preservation:**
    - Dispatch police for suspected crimes (suicide, homicide, overdose, domestic dispute, rape, abuse, hit-and-run, riot, robbery, gunfire, weapons); stage until police clear scene
    - **Evidence Rules:** Single path in/out; touch only what is necessary and report to police; no crime-scene phone use without police permission; move patient without permission only for safety/care; avoid cutting through bullet/stab holes; cut away from knots in ropes/ties; do not cover body with a sheet; in rape cases, advise against washing, changing clothes, using bathroom, or eating/drinking
  - **Special Reporting & Baby Safe-Haven Laws:**
    - **Mandatory/Special Reporting:** Child/elder/spousal abuse or neglect (good-faith reports carry defamation immunity), gunshots, knife wounds, suspicious burns, poisonings, sexual assault, drug-related injuries (addiction is an illness per U.S. Supreme Court, not a crime), infectious exposures, involuntary restraints, mental incompetence/intoxication, attempted suicides, dog bites
    - **Baby Safe-Haven Laws:** Originated in Texas (**1999**), active in all 50 states + D.C.; allow safe relinquishment of unharmed infants to designated locations (hospitals, police, fire, or EMS depending on state) within state age limits (**3 days** up to **1 year** in North Dakota)

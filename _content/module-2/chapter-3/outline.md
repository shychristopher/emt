## 1. Professional Foundations: Scope, Standards, Duties & Ethics

### Overview & Core Premise
- EMTs regularly face complex medical, legal, and ethical decisions—such as stopping off duty, treating minors without parents present, or responding to family requests to withhold CPR.
- Providing and thoroughly documenting care within your scope of practice and local protocols, grounded in proper consent and duty to act, protects against successful lawsuits.
- The public and health care profession expect a competent EMT who understands and accepts legal and ethical responsibilities to:
  - The patient
  - The medical director
  - The public

### Scope of Practice vs. Standard of Care
- **Scope of Practice:**
  - Typically defined by state laws as the actions and care that EMTs are legally allowed to perform in the state where they provide emergency medical care.
  - Answers the core question: *Was the EMT allowed by law to do what was done?*
  - Identifies activities deemed illegal if performed without licensure or certification and establishes boundaries among health care professionals (e.g., administering oxygen when indicated is within an EMT's scope; suturing a laceration is outside scope and illegal even if the EMT knows how).
  - Commonly defined using:
    - NHTSA's *National EMS Scope of Practice Model*
    - *National EMS Education Standards* (minimum U.S. training standards)
    - State laws, regulations, and policies
  - Purely defines what actions can legally be performed—it is not a practice guideline or protocol and does not define the standard of care.
- **Standard of Care:**
  - Defined as the care expected to be provided by an EMT with similar training when managing a patient in a similar situation.
  - Applies the "reasonable person standard": *What would a reasonably prudent EMT with similar training do in the same situation?*
  - Evaluates two core principles:
    1. Did the EMT provide the right assessment and emergency care for the patient?
    2. Did the EMT perform the assessment and emergency care properly?
  - Falling below the standard of care constitutes negligence.
  - Sources that may define the standard of care include:
    - Recognized and accepted EMT textbooks
    - Care expected by other EMTs in the community or region
    - Local and state protocols
    - NHTSA's *National EMS Education Standards*
    - The EMS system's operating policies and procedures

### Duty to Act
- **On-Duty Obligation:**
  - Legal obligation to respond and provide service regardless of whether you think the patient needs an ambulance.
  - While on duty, you are legally obligated to care for any patient who requires and consents to care, rendering necessary emergency care to the best of your ability and training.
- **Off-Duty Rules & Assumed Duty:**
  - While off duty and not functioning in an EMS capacity, most states impose no greater legal obligation to act than on any private citizen, allowing you to:
    - Stop and help an accident victim at the scene
    - Pass the scene and call for help
    - Pass the scene and make no attempt to call for help
  - Some states *do* legally require EMTs to stop and render aid even when off duty.
  - Regardless of state mandate, if you are off duty (paid or volunteer) and publicly respond to a page for assistance or stop to provide emergency care at a scene, you create a **duty to act** (because others may assume the call is covered and not respond, and leaving after starting care can leave the patient worse off).
  - Once you stop and create a duty to act:
    - You cannot leave until someone with appropriate expertise—typically an equal or higher level of training—takes over care.
    - You are legally responsible for any of the patient's personal property that you pick up.
- **Duty to Patients:**
  - Respect patient rights and serve as the patient's advocate.
  - Protect privacy and confidentiality of medical status, history, and records.
  - Work to lessen suffering and provide complete, competent care covering both physical injuries/illnesses and emotional needs.
  - Do no further harm.
- **Duty to Self:**
  - Obtain necessary credentials and keep certifications and licensure current.
  - Maintain skills at a level of proficiency.
  - Maintain mental health and remain physically fit to perform job duties.
- **Duty to Partner:**
  - Ensure your partner is physically and mentally fit to provide emergency medical care and fulfill job responsibilities.
  - If your partner reports to work under the influence of alcohol or drugs, report the situation to your supervisor to protect yourself and the public.
- **Duty Regarding Equipment:**
  - Legally responsible for ensuring all equipment is in proper working order at the beginning of each shift.
  - Equipment malfunction due to improper maintenance—or lacking necessary equipment to treat a patient—constitutes negligence in providing care.

### Good Samaritan Laws & Legal Protections
- **Good Samaritan Laws:**
  - First enacted in California in **1959** to protect licensed individuals (such as physicians or surgeons) acting in good faith at emergency scenes from civil damages for acts or omissions; many states also have immunity laws specifically covering prehospital providers.
  - Generally protects an *unpaid* person from liability for good-faith acts unless those acts constitute **gross negligence** (requiring proof that care was markedly below the standard of care and, in some cases, involved willful or wanton intent to harm).
  - Most states have specific laws authorizing EMTs to perform prehospital emergency procedures without a medical license and granting partial immunity to nurses, physicians, supervisors, and others providing direction via cell phone, telephone, or radio.
  - Laws governing private vs. public providers vary by jurisdiction—know your local laws.
  - Does **not** prevent you from being sued, though it helps protect against losing a lawsuit if you performed according to the EMT standard of care.
  - Best defense against lawsuits (on or off duty): prevention—render care to the best of your ability, stay within scope of practice and standard of care, behave professionally, prioritize patients' best interests, and maintain adequate liability insurance or immunity protections.
- **Sovereign Immunity (Governmental Immunity):**
  - Prevents persons treated by government-operated EMS systems from suing the government for civil liability.
  - Private EMS companies are not government agencies and are not protected under sovereign immunity.
- **Statute of Limitations:**
  - Time limit set by each state (typically measured in years) for a patient to file a negligence claim following the injury/illness event or discovery that the problem existed.
- **Contributory Negligence:**
  - Exists when a patient is found to have contributed in any manner—through their own negligence—to the injury, illness, or damage done to them when filing a negligence claim against an EMS provider.

### Medical Direction
- Your legal right to function in the prehospital setting is contingent on **medical direction**, which provides medical oversight across all aspects of the EMS system.
- When providing emergency care:
  - Follow standing orders and protocols approved by medical direction.
  - Establish cell phone, video chat, telephone, or radio communications with medical direction when appropriate.
  - Communicate clearly and completely with medical direction, and follow orders given in response.
  - Consult medical direction whenever there is a question about the scope or direction of care.
- Most areas maintain protocols for interagency cooperation between EMS and other public safety services; interactions are frequently recorded via law enforcement and some EMS body cameras for potential review.

### Ethical Responsibilities
- **Ethics vs. Morals:**
  - *Ethics:* Branch of philosophy directed toward the study of morality or morals.
  - *Morals:* Concepts of "right and wrong."
  - *Code of ethics:* List of rules of ideal conduct (a Code of Ethics for EMTs was issued by the National Association of Emergency Medical Technicians in **1978**).
  - Placing patient welfare above all else helps prevent unethical acts.
- **Core Ethical Responsibilities:**
  - Serve patient needs with respect for human dignity regardless of nationality, race, gender, creed, or status.
  - Maintain skill mastery and respect the competence of other medical professionals.
  - Stay current with EMS changes affecting patient care and uphold professional standards.
  - Critically review performances to improve response time, patient outcome, and communication, while taking responsibility for individual professional actions and judgment.
  - Report honestly and keep professional information confidential unless required by law to divulge it.
  - Work harmoniously with other EMTs, nurses, physicians, and health care team members.
- **Ethical, Moral, and Legal Conflicts:**
  - Conflicts arise when legal, ethical, and moral obligations pull in different directions—such as a terminally ill patient requesting no treatment while presenting an expired DNR order (legal duty to treat vs. ethical desire to honor refusal vs. moral duty to preserve life), rationing care during mass-casualty triage, or managing cardiac arrest in wilderness environments with limited resources and long transport times.
  - Follow local protocols and applicable laws, and contact medical direction for guidance in difficult situations.

## 2. Patient Consent, Advance Directives & Refusal of Care

### General Principles of Consent
- A conscious patient with the capacity to understand and make an informed, rational decision has the legal right to accept or refuse emergency medical care.
- Always obtain **consent** (permission) before assessing or rendering care, and inform the patient of the care to be provided along with associated risks and consequences to obtain **informed consent**.
- **Standard Approach Before Proceeding:**
  1. Introduce yourself.
  2. State that you would like to perform an assessment to determine the patient's condition, provide emergency care, and transport to a medical facility.
  3. Ask if the patient understands and has any questions.
  4. Request explicit permission before proceeding—even if the patient placed the 911 call—and take extra care when recommending an assessment or treatment that is part of an unproven research program.
  5. Listen carefully to the patient's response and do not assume consent for all aspects of assessment, care, and transport.
- Touching a patient's body or clothing without proper consent—or providing care without consent for that specific treatment—can constitute **battery** (unlawful touching).

### Types of Consent
- **Expressed Consent:**
  - Required from every conscious adult who is of legal age, has the capacity to make a rational decision, and has been informed—in understandable terms—of the assessment, procedures, and all related risks that would affect a reasonable person's decision to accept or refuse treatment.
  - Verbal confirmation is preferable, though nonverbal cues (such as nodding when asked to perform an assessment) are acceptable.
  - Document the patient's approval.
- **Implied Consent** *(Emergency Doctrine)*:
  - Applies when a patient is unresponsive or unable to make a rational decision (e.g., disoriented from a head injury), or initially refuses care and subsequently becomes unresponsive, incapacitated, or irrational due to illness or injury.
  - In a true emergency posing significant risk of death, disability, or condition deterioration, the law assumes the patient would consent if able.
- **Minor Consent:**
  - Must be obtained from a parent, legal guardian, or someone granted limited decision-making rights by a parent/guardian (such as a teacher, stepparent, or authorized caregiver).
  - A minor is typically anyone under age **18** or **21** depending on state law, and is generally not legally competent to accept or refuse emergency care.
  - **Emergency Exception (Implied Consent for Minors):** Allowed across all states when a parent/legal guardian is unavailable or unable to consent, assuming they would consent if present; clearly document the circumstances under six conditions:
    1. The child has an emergent condition placing life or health in danger.
    2. The legal guardian is unavailable or unable to provide consent for treatment or transport.
    3. Treatment or transport cannot be safely delayed until consent can be obtained.
    4. The EMT administers only treatment for emergency conditions posing an immediate threat to the child.
    5. When authority is in doubt, act in what you believe is the minor's best interest.
    6. Contact medical direction for additional instructions if needed and no parent contact is possible.
  - **Emancipated Minor:** Parent or guardian consent is not required for an emancipated minor—typically defined as a minor who is married, pregnant, a parent, in the armed forces, financially independent and living away from home, or emancipated by court decree.
- **Involuntary Consent:**
  - Applies to a mentally incompetent adult or an individual in law enforcement custody or incarcerated.
  - Consent to assess and treat is obtained from a third party with legal authority to decide on the person's behalf, such as a legal guardian, law enforcement officer, or officer of the court.

### End-of-Life Care Advance Directives
- **Advance Directives:**
  - Written instructions made in advance (supported legally in part by the federal *Patient Self-Determination Act*) that predetermine and explain how medical decisions are made if a patient becomes unable to make their own decisions, including desired care and who should decide.
  - Vary by state; know your state's advance directives and how they affect prehospital care.
- **Living Will:**
  - Legal document directing future health care decisions only when a person becomes unable to make their own decisions at end of life (terminally ill or permanently comatose), including when to start or stop life-prolonging measures.
  - Requires **two physicians** to confirm the patient cannot make medical decisions and has a state-specified terminal illness or permanent coma before guiding care.
  - Does not necessarily refuse all medical care (treatments such as insulin injections, antibiotics, food, pain medications, or other predetermined care may still be included), may incorporate a DNR order, and can be ended or rescinded at any time.
- **Durable Power of Attorney** *(Medical Power of Attorney / Health Care Proxy)*:
  - Legal document naming a proxy (agent) to make all health care decisions if the patient becomes unable to do so, guided by what the proxy believes the patient would want if not previously expressed.
  - Requires the patient's **physician** to certify the patient cannot make medical decisions before taking effect, and ends if the patient regains decision-making ability; varies by state.
- **Do Not Resuscitate (DNR) Orders:**
  - Most common advance directive encountered prehospital; a standardized form typically signed by the physician and patient instructing not to attempt resuscitation once the patient has no pulse and no respirations (varies by state).
  - **DNR Comfort Care (DNRCC):** Indicated in many states for modified/comfort support—such as oxygen administration, bleeding control, and pain medication—without resuscitation attempts if breathing or pulse stops.
  - **Non-Arrest / Deterioration Dilemma:** A DNR cannot be acted upon unless the patient is in cardiac arrest (no breathing and no pulse). If a patient with a DNR still has a pulse on arrival, provide standard emergency care per protocol; if DNRCC applies, provide selected comfort measures without resuscitation upon arrest.
  - **Verification & Validity:** A legally valid DNR or DNRCC must be confirmed and present upon arrival (actual form, wallet card, or bracelet)—never rely solely on the word of family, nursing staff, or bystanders. If unavailable or validity is in question, begin full resuscitation and clarify with medical direction.
- **Physician Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment (POLST)** *(Also known as **MOLST**, **MOST**, **POST**, or **TPOPP**)*:
  - Used for patients with serious or terminal illness not expected to survive longer than **1 year**.
  - Unlike a DNR focused primarily on resuscitation, POLST lets the patient specify desired levels of treatment prior to arrest (e.g., continuing life-sustaining care with limitations such as accepting medications while refusing advanced airway procedures or defibrillation).
  - Voluntary (not mandatory), found in homes or long-term care facilities, and must be signed by a physician (or by a PA or NP in some states) with valid dates and physician contact information.
- **Field Challenges & Resolving Ambiguity:**
  - Instructions must be clear, concise, unambiguous, and on professional letterhead (vague phrases like *"no heroics"* or *"no extraordinary treatment"* are insufficient).
  - Living wills are generally suited to institutional settings; multi-physician verification is impractical in the field, and document review must not waste critical resuscitative time.
  - When validity, interpretation, or family conflict arises:
    1. Consider initiating treatment immediately to avoid negligence/delay claims.
    2. Contact medical direction for instructions.
    3. Continue treatment until the issue is resolved (err on the side of providing care when in doubt).
  - **NASEMSO Guidelines on Exclusion Documents:** Verify valid exclusion documents meet state form/construction/intactness and identification requirements; provide comfort measures and non-prohibited standard care when signs of life are present; stop CPR if a valid exclusion is verified and permitted by protocol/medical direction; and follow scene-specific considerations involving a personal physician with an ongoing patient relationship, an authorized home health/hospice nurse operating under private physician orders, or scene safety/family wishes.

### Refusing Treatment & Protecting Yourself
- **Decision-Making Capacity vs. Competence:**
  - *Capacity* (determined medically) is the ability to understand treatment options and respond adequately to make an informed, rational decision; *competence* is a legal status determined by a court.
  - A patient lacking rational decision-making capacity without a court ruling is *de facto incompetent*.
  - Adults with capacity—even if refusal could lead to death—may refuse care (verbally, by gesture/pushing/pulling away, or by withdrawing after treatment starts) or transport after being informed of and demonstrating understanding of the risks and consequences.
  - Altered mental status, mental illness, drug/alcohol intoxication, physical/mental impairment, or suicidal intent/attempt generally impairs capacity, warranting implied consent/protective care and consultation with medical direction.
- **Seven Steps to Protect Yourself During a Refusal:**
  1. Perform a thorough physical assessment (including vital signs, neurologic, and mental status) to support an informed decision; thoroughly document refusal even if assessment itself is declined by a patient with capacity.
  2. Re-persuade the patient (and enlist family if helpful) to accept needed care or transport, clearly explaining consequences in plain language and having the patient read back/read the report to verify understanding.
  3. Confirm the patient has capacity to make a rational, informed decision and is not suicidal or impaired by substances/illness/injury.
  4. Consult medical direction as needed or required by local protocol (especially if capacity is uncertain).
  5. Do not put yourself in danger trying to treat/transport a refusing individual—request law enforcement if needed—and balance avoiding abandonment against forcing care.
  6. Advise the patient to seek help later if specific warning symptoms develop (using clear, non-technical examples) and re-engage EMS if needed.
  7. Thoroughly document the assessment, explanations, risk/consequence discussions, persuasion efforts, advice to seek care for warning symptoms, and the patient's response; obtain a signed release-from-liability form and witness signature(s) (other than your partner where required), or document a witnessed refusal to sign.

## 3. Liability, Negligence & Intentional Torts

### Criminal vs. Civil Liability
- Breaching a legal duty creates liability, which can be **criminal** or **civil**:
  - **Criminal Liability:** Government brings legal action on behalf of the public against the EMT; may result in a fine or incarceration.
  - **Civil Liability:** An individual (the **plaintiff**) files a lawsuit against an individual EMT, EMS agency/company, or others indirectly involved such as a supervisor or medical director (the **defendants**), accusing the defendant of committing a **tort** (a wrongful act, injury, or damage) and usually seeking monetary compensation.

### Negligence & Its Four Elements
- Most EMS lawsuits involve **negligence**—a tort with no intent to harm where a breach in the duty to act occurred.
- The plaintiff must prove **all four elements** for the lawsuit to succeed and damages to be awarded:
  1. **Duty to Act:** Legal obligation to respond to the scene and provide emergency care; once established by contacting the patient with intent to provide care, the relationship cannot be terminated without patient consent.
  2. **Breach of Duty to Act:** Deviating from the standard of care:
     - *Simple negligence:* Failing to perform care or making a mistake in treatment.
     - *Gross negligence:* Willful, wanton, or extremely reckless patient care far beyond carelessness—construed as dangerous to the patient (intentionally causing harm is charged as gross negligence and may trigger criminal charges).
  3. **Damages:** Real, demonstrable, legally recognizable injuries—not trivial or minor.
  4. **Proximate Cause (Causation):** The injuries suffered were the direct result of the EMT's negligence rather than the traumatic event or underlying disease.
- **Associated Legal Doctrines:**
  - *Res ipsa loquitur* ("the thing speaks for itself"): Applies when a caregiver's inappropriate actions are obviously the cause of patient harm.
  - *Negligence per se:* An act is considered negligent simply because it violates a statute or regulation such as written EMS protocols or approved scope of practice.

### Intentional Torts
- An **intentional tort** is an action knowingly committed by an individual that is civilly wrong under the law (unlike negligence, which is a failure to meet the permissible standard of care).
- **Common EMS Intentional Torts:**
  - **Abandonment:**
    - Stopping treatment without transferring care to another competent professional of an equal or higher level of training and certification/licensure (e.g., transferring care to a flight crew).
    - Can occur in the emergency department if you place a patient in an ED room, notify only a unit clerk or secretary, and leave without giving an oral report to a qualified physician, nurse, or health care worker—simply moving the patient from stretcher to hospital bed is not a transfer of care.
    - At a scene, an EMT can fully assume transfer of care from a paramedic or AEMT only if the patient's condition/injuries can be managed by an EMT without ALS skills; transferring a multisystem trauma patient to an unqualified specialist (such as a dermatologist) risks abandonment.
    - Thorough assessment and acting in the patient's best interest help prevent abandonment.
  - **Assault:**
    - A willful threat to inflict harm on a patient; can occur without physical contact if the patient has apprehension of bodily harm and the EMT has the ability to carry it out (e.g., holding up an epinephrine auto-injector and threatening to stick the patient).
  - **Battery:**
    - Unlawfully touching a patient without consent; providing care without consent can be construed as battery even with good intentions.
  - **False Imprisonment (or Kidnapping):**
    - Transporting or failing to allow a patient who has capacity to understand and make a rational decision to refuse or withdraw from assessment, treatment, or transport against their wishes; does not necessarily require physical restraint.
  - **Defamation:**
    - Releasing written or spoken information damaging to a person's character, reputation, or standing in the community; discuss patient, partner, and emergency issues only in private settings, never in public.
    - *Slander:* Spoken defamation.
    - *Libel:* False or damaging statements in written form or via mass media; follow proper policies/procedures for complaints against others.
- **Best Lawsuit Defense:** Provide emergency care to the best of your ability and clearly understand your scope of practice and expected standard of care.

## 4. Confidentiality, HIPAA, COBRA & EMTALA

### Confidentiality & HIPAA
- **Confidentiality:**
  - Keep patient history, physical assessment, and treatment information strictly confidential across physical injury, illness, infectious disease, and emotional/psychological emergencies unless disclosure is required by law; deliberate privacy invasion can lead to loss of certification/licensure and legal action.
  - Do not share specifics with press, family, friends, or the public (including what a patient said, who they were with, unusual behavior/appearance, identifying details, or information received from other EMS personnel).
  - Releasing confidential information generally requires a signed written release from the patient or a verified legal guardian (confirm legal guardianship before releasing).
  - **Five Permitted Disclosures Without Patient/Guardian Permission (Per Law):**
    1. Another health care provider needs the information to continue medical care.
    2. Official public health or governmental agency requires mandatory reporting (e.g., rape, child abuse, elder abuse, neglect, domestic violence, certain communicable diseases, gunshots, stab wounds, animal bites, suspicious burns, or other specified crimes/conditions).
    3. Police request information as part of a potential criminal investigation.
    4. Third-party billing form requires it.
    5. Required by legal subpoena in court.
- **Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) of 1996:**
  - Federal law protecting patient health care information privacy and giving patients control over its use and distribution; mandates strict policies/procedures for record keeping, storage, access, release, and discussion.
  - Discuss patient-specific details only when medically necessary (never casually in public areas like an ambulance bay).
  - Complete agency privacy training, obtain patient signature acknowledging receipt of privacy policies/procedures (often paired with insurance release forms), and consult the agency's designated Privacy Officer—especially after inadvertent disclosure or third-party information requests.

### COBRA, EMTALA & Transport Protections
- **COBRA & EMTALA:**
  - Federal regulations (**Consolidated Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act [COBRA]** and **Emergency Medical Treatment and Active Labor Act [EMTALA]**) ensuring public access to emergency health care regardless of ability to pay and preventing "patient dumping" (turning away or transferring emergency patients due to inability to pay; EMTALA is the "anti-patient-dumping statute").
  - Applies to emergency medical conditions (injury or illness) and affects EMS—particularly hospital-owned/operated services—during hospital-to-facility transfers and destination decisions.
  - Transferring facilities must stabilize patients to the best of their ability before transfer and ensure the EMS crew is qualified and capable of managing the patient's condition (e.g., EMTs cannot perform transfers requiring continuous ECG monitoring or IV infusion monitoring/drug therapy beyond EMT scope; notify supervisors/policies or have the medical director consult the transferring physician before loading if capability is in question, as performing an inappropriate transfer can violate EMTALA).
  - Hospital-based ambulances must not bypass the closest appropriate facility for another facility due to lack of insurance/ability to pay unless requested by the patient or the receiving facility's capabilities better match the patient's condition.
- **Protecting Yourself in Transport & Transfer Situations:**
  - *Emergency transport:* Never base destination choice on ability to pay or broadcast payment/insurance status over the radio; do not bypass a capable medical facility unless directed by medical direction, the patient, or predetermined protocol, and clearly document the reason for any bypass.
  - *Five Interfacility Transfer Safeguards:*
    1. Obtain a full, clear report on the patient's condition.
    2. Confirm care needed during transport is within your capability and scope of practice.
    3. Obtain a signed informed consent form for transfer from the patient or legal guardian.
    4. Obtain written certification of transfer (transferring physician's name and address, receiving facility, and reason for transfer).
    5. Know the route and take the quickest possible route.

## 5. Special Legal & Field Situations

### Organ Donors & Medical Identification
- **Organ Donors & Harvesting:**
  - Organs may be donated only with a legal signed document (e.g., signed donor card; driver's license back sticker or "organ donor" specification indicates intent to donate).
  - Treat potential donors identically to any other patient—**patient first, organ donor last**—while assisting by:
    1. Identifying potential donors (patients about to die or who died within hours; hospital staff and family make the final decision).
    2. Communicating with medical direction and alerting receiving ED staff so other departments can be contacted.
    3. Providing supportive care such as CPR to help maintain vital organs if harvesting is attempted.
- **Medical Identification Insignia & Alert Tattoos:**
  - Check for medical ID tags (card, necklace, bracelet, fitness wristband) for conditions such as allergies, diabetes, or epilepsy and any listed phone numbers for treatment/medication details.
  - Inspect wrists, arms, ankles, neckline, chest, and other body areas on altered mental status patients for permanent medical alert tattoos, which lack standardized insignia/lettering/colors and may be mistaken for body art; note that obtaining tattoos carries infection and wound-healing risks, especially for diabetics.

### Recognizing Death in the Field & NASEMSO Protocol
- Physicians hold ultimate responsibility for determining time and cause of death, though EMTs may judge whether to start resuscitation; as a general rule, begin resuscitation if the patient is still warm without obvious signs of death, except in **accidental hypothermia** (extremely cold, rigid bodies can survive prolonged cardiac arrest and must not be considered dead until rewarmed).
- **Presumptive Signs of Death** *(relevant with DNR orders or terminal illness)*:
  - Absence of pulse, breathing, and breath sounds
  - Complete unresponsiveness to any stimuli
  - No eye movement or pupil response
  - Absence of blood pressure
  - No reflexes
- **Obvious Signs of Death:**
  - Decapitation (complete severing of head from body)
  - Rigor mortis (body stiffness within **2–12 hours**)
  - Decomposition of the body
  - Dependent lividity (skin discoloration from gravity-dependent blood pooling)
- **NASEMSO *Determination of Death/Withholding Resuscitative Efforts* Protocol (2022):**
  - *Goal & presentation:* Provide all available resuscitative efforts including CPR to clinically dead patients (unresponsive, apneic, no palpable carotid pulse) unless an exception applies.
  - *Exclusion criteria* *(does not apply to lightning strikes, drowning, or hypothermia)*:
    - **Biological/irreversible brain death conditions:**
      1. Decapitation
      2. Decomposition or putrefaction (bloated/ruptured skin with or without sloughed soft tissue, indicating death at least **24 hours** prior)
      3. Transection of torso (completely cut across below shoulders and above hips through major organs/vessels)
      4. Incineration (**90% BSA** full-thickness burns with ash rather than clothing, absent body hair, charred skin)
      5. Injuries incompatible with life (e.g., massive crush injury, complete exsanguination, severe brain matter displacement)
      6. Futile and inhumane attempts per agency policy/protocol for "compelling reasons"
      7. Blunt/penetrating trauma found apneic, pulseless, and without other signs of life (spontaneous movement, ECG activity, pupillary response) on EMS arrival
      8. Nontraumatic arrest with obvious signs of death including dependent lividity or rigor mortis
    - **OR valid actionable order:** Valid DNR (form, card, bracelet) or POLST/MOLST that conforms to state color/construction specifications, is intact (not cut, broken, or repaired), and displays patient name (plus physician name if required by state law/regulation).
  - *Management & safety:* Assess for dependent lividity with rigor mortis and/or inclusion criteria; withhold CPR only if all criteria are confirmed, stop CPR and contact medical direction if confirmed after starting, or immediately start/continue CPR (and use termination protocol if indicated) if findings differ. With a DNR/MOLST/POLST and signs of life (pulse and respirations), provide standard protocol care (contact medical direction to request withholding); with a DNI/MOLST/POLST, give full protocol care except prohibited interventions (contact medical direction before considering a prohibited intervention). When status or appropriateness of withholding resuscitation is unclear, start CPR immediately and contact medical direction; consider CPR for scene safety or family wishes even when death criteria are met, and minimize evidence disturbance at likely crime scenes.
- **Medical Examiner / Coroner Cases:**
  - Typically called for violent, unusual, or suspicious deaths—including homicides, suicides, violent deaths (suspected child/elder abuse), crash deaths (auto, motorcycle, ATV, snowmobile), unusual scenes (burns, electrocution), SIDS, and dead on arrival per local protocol. When in doubt, err on the side of benefiting the patient and begin resuscitation.

### Crime Scenes & Evidence Preservation
- Ensure police are dispatched whenever a potential crime scene is suspected—including potential/actual suicide, homicide, drug overdose, domestic dispute, rape, abuse, hit-and-run, riot, robbery, gunfire, or dangerous weapons.
- Never enter to provide care if a crime is in progress or a criminal is still active; wait until police declare the scene safe, and stay alert even after police clear entry.
- Once safe, prioritize patient care while minimizing evidence disturbance:
  - Use one path in and out.
  - Touch only what is necessary and report any moved/touched items to police.
  - Move only what is needed to protect the patient and deliver care; do not use a crime-scene phone without police permission, and move the patient without police permission only if in danger or required for care.
  - Observe and document unusual findings.
  - Avoid cutting through suspected bullet or stab holes in clothing if possible.
  - Do not cut through knots in ropes/ties (cut away from the knot) and do not cover the patient with a sheet.
  - In rape cases, do not wash or allow washing, and explain why the patient should avoid changing clothes, using the bathroom, or taking anything by mouth to preserve evidence.

### Special Reporting & Baby Safe-Haven Laws
- **Special Reporting Situations:**
  - Follow state laws and local protocols—or report to receiving medical staff if not mandated by state law—for:
    - Suspected abuse (child, elder, spousal abuse/neglect; good-faith reports often carry defamation immunity)
    - Crime-related injuries (gunshots, knife wounds, suspicious burns, poisonings, suspected sexual assault)
    - Drug-related injuries (noting U.S. Supreme Court precedent that drug addiction, not possession, is an illness rather than a crime)
    - Additional reportable situations: suspected infectious disease exposure, use of restraints to treat/transport against a patient's will, apparent mental incompetence or intoxication, attempted suicides, and dog bites
- **Baby Safe-Haven Laws (Infant Abandonment Laws):**
  - Originated in Texas in **1999** after 1990s infant abandonments and now enacted in all states and D.C. to allow parents to safely relinquish unharmed infants to designated authorities.
  - State laws vary widely in designated safe-surrender locations (e.g., only designated hospitals in California vs. hospitals, law enforcement, fire stations, or EMS agencies/stations in other states) and maximum infant age (from **3 days** in some states up to **1 year** in North Dakota); know your state's rules and required actions.

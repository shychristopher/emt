- **Data & Clinical Standards:** minimum data set, pertinent negatives

## 1. Purpose, Functions & Quality Standards of the PCR

### Core Role & Major Goals of Documentation
- Alongside patient assessment, treatment, and transport, EMTs must document data—via written or electronically generated records—for every patient contact, primarily through the **prehospital care report (PCR)**, which varies in format across EMS systems and states while collecting essentially the same core information.
- **Three Major Goals:**
  1. **Continuity of Care:** Provides receiving medical facility staff with a detailed yet concise and accurate record of assessment findings, condition trends, treatments delivered, and patient response to treatment.
  2. **Legal Protection:** May serve as the sole documentation available if a call becomes the subject of civil or criminal legal action months or years later, long after thousands of subsequent calls blur specific memories.
  3. **Reimbursement:** Supports proper reimbursement for EMS services amid rising operational costs.

### Accountability & Quality Standards
- High-quality documentation is the prime foundation for high-quality patient care and supports the patient, treating medical personnel, aiding organizations, researchers, and the EMT.
- The PCR serves as a record and measure of accountability demonstrating the quality of prehospital assessment and care.
- Every PCR must be completely accurate, true to what occurred during assessment and management, legible, and restricted to acceptable objective information and pertinent subjective information.
- Poorly completed PCRs—marked by unchecked boxes, misspelled words, or sloppy or illegible writing—reflect poorly on the care provided and suggest a lack of attention to accuracy, detail, or emergency care quality.
- If a lawsuit arises over patient care, the plaintiff's attorney will typically review the PCR first.

### Five Core Functions of the PCR
1. **Continuity of Medical Care:**
   - Conveys the scene and environment where the patient was found, which is often inaccessible to hospital personnel at the receiving facility.
   - Establishes a clinical baseline (mental status descriptions and vital signs recorded during assessment and transport) so emergency department (ED) staff can measure improvement or deterioration.
   - Clarifies the treatment course through the chief complaint, signs, and symptoms, while records of interventions performed or not performed show what steps are needed and prevent duplicating earlier efforts.
   - Captures details from unique sources ED personnel may not have direct access to, including bystanders, family members, or an awake patient.
2. **Administrative Uses:**
   - Typically becomes part of the patient's permanent hospital record and is used to prepare bills and submit records to insurance companies.
   - Contributes data to EMS operational statistics, such as average response time, time on scene for critical trauma patients, and number of cardiac arrest patients treated each year.
3. **Legal Document:**
   - Supports court testimony when a run stems from a crime or leads to a civil lawsuit months or years later, and serves as essential defense documentation.
   - Key items to document clearly: objective and pertinent subjective findings; patient status upon arrival on scene; what emergency care was provided and when; any changes in patient condition upon arrival at the medical facility; and discussions with the patient and/or family regarding care, especially when care is refused.
4. **Educational and Research Uses:**
   - Enables clinical researchers to study positive or negative effects of specific interventions at different stages of patient contact.
   - Helps administration experts evaluate how to deliver services more timely or cost-effectively.
   - Identifies patient presentations and conditions that point to needs for additional training and curriculum updates.
5. **Evaluation and Continuous Quality Improvement (CQI):**
   - Forms an integral part of continuous quality improvement through routine call documentation reviews.
   - Guides continuing or remedial education courses for EMTs based on identified needs.
   - Supports medical oversight in verifying adherence to local protocols and the established standard of care.

## 2. PCR Formats, Minimum Data Set & Core Sections

### Golden Rules of Documentation
- **"If it wasn’t written down, it wasn’t done"** — Without documentation, others (including ED staff) will not know care occurred, and you cannot prove it was performed in court.
- **"If it wasn’t done, don’t write it down"** — Recording unperformed care constitutes falsifying information.

### PCR Formats & Capabilities
- **Written PCR Format:**
  - Traditional format combining check boxes and write-on lines for vital signs and brief entries with dedicated space for a fuller narrative account of the patient contact.
  - May also include specific sections for crew roster/credentials, medical history, medications, allergies, primary/secondary EMT impressions, IV therapy, intubation, cardiac arrest, and treatment logs.
- **Computerized Direct Data Entry & Electronic Clipboards:**
  - Data entered directly into a laptop, mobile data device, smartphone app, or tablet (including systems with patient diagrams, drop-down menus, and styluses), typically synchronized or uploaded to a central database.
  - **Electronic (Computer) Clipboard:** Includes "pen-based" systems capable of recognizing and converting handwriting into electronic text, as well as selection lists requiring minimal writing.
  - **Key Advantages of Computerized Systems:**
    - Greater legibility and storage capacity than handwritten reports.
    - More efficient data storage, retrieval, and utilization.
    - Integration with diagnostic/monitoring equipment, electronic medical records (EMRs), computer-aided dispatch (CAD), fleet management, inventory control, email, personnel, and payroll systems.
    - Real-time upload capability to receiving hospitals to support pre-arrival preparation.
    - Built-in spelling and abbreviation checks, prompts/alerts for omitted required information, and support for quality assurance (QA) and quality improvement (QI) programs.

### U.S. DOT Minimum Data Set & Synchronous Timekeeping
- **Purpose of the Minimum Data Set:**
  - Standardizes core PCR data recommended by the U.S. Department of Transportation (DOT) to support a higher general standard of patient care nationwide, allow meaningful comparisons and analysis across systems, and speed adoption of improved emergency care methods.
- **Patient Information Elements (8):**
  1. Chief complaint
  2. Level of responsiveness (AVPU)—mental status
  3. Blood pressure for patients greater than **3 years** old
  4. Skin perfusion (capillary refill) for patients less than **6 years** old
  5. Skin color, temperature, and condition
  6. Pulse rate
  7. Respiratory rate and effort
  8. Patient demographics (age, sex, race, and weight)
- **Administrative Information Elements (6):**
  1. Time the incident was reported
  2. Time the unit was notified
  3. Time of arrival at the patient
  4. Time the unit left the scene
  5. Time the unit arrived at its destination (such as a hospital)
  6. Time of transfer of care
- **Synchronous Timekeeping:**
  - All parts of an EMS system (dispatch, crews, and timekeeping devices) must use accurately set clocks that agree with one another.
  - Supports accurate clinical timelines (such as time elapsed in cardiac arrest prior to CPR or defibrillation) and protects against administrative or legal questions regarding care quality.

### Core PCR Sections
- **Administrative Information (Run Data):**
  - Includes the six administrative times from the minimum data set, plus often:
    - EMS unit number and run or call number
    - Crew member names and certification levels
    - Dispatched address
- **Patient Demographics and Other Patient Data:**
  - Typically captures legal name, age, sex, race, birth date, home address, insurance or billing information, location where the patient was found, and any care provided prior to EMT arrival.
- **Vital Signs:**
  - Recorded via check boxes, write-on lines, drop-down menus, or data fields (including timestamps and minimum data set elements).
  - Ideally obtain and record **at least two complete sets** of vital signs, noting the patient's position when taken (e.g., supine, standing, sitting) and the exact times obtained.
- **Patient Narrative:**
  - Provides detailed context beyond check boxes, drop-down menus, and fixed fields, setting the foundation for assessment, treatment, and documentation.
  - **Chief Complaint:** Recorded in the patient's own words—or a bystander's words if the patient is unresponsive—enclosed in quotation marks (e.g., *"My leg hurts"*).
  - **History or Mechanism of Injury:** Details when the chief complaint started, how it progressed, and relevant patient history.
  - **Objective vs. Pertinent Subjective Information:**
    - *Objective information (signs):* Measurable or verifiable observations (e.g., pulse rate, discoloration below both eyes).
    - *Subjective information (symptoms):* Perceptions or interpretations from the patient or EMT (e.g., *"I feel light-headed"*; *"The patient seems to be in pain"*), limited to medically pertinent facts rather than diagnoses or irrelevant remarks (e.g., a husband offering to drive).
  - **Pertinent Negatives:** Expected signs or symptoms based on the chief complaint that the patient denies having (e.g., denying pain after a crash or denying shortness of breath with chest pain), giving receiving caregivers a fuller clinical picture.
  - **Physical Exam, Scene Details, Sensitive Sources & Reassessments:**
    - Briefly document physical assessment findings in the order performed (e.g., head, neck, chest).
    - Note relevant scene and patient findings (e.g., medication bottles, a suicide note, a collapsed ladder).
    - Attribute sensitive reports to their source (e.g., noting the wife as the source if she reports a husband's heroin addiction).
    - Record the time and findings of every reassessment to show condition trends.
  - **Style Rules:** Keep writing simple and direct; avoid radio codes and nonstandard abbreviations.
- **Treatment:**
  - Details all treatments administered in chronological order, the exact times given, and how the patient responded (improved, deteriorated, or unchanged) so receiving clinicians see what was done, when it happened, and the clinical effect.
  - Also document any refusal of specific treatments by the patient.

## 3. NASEMSO Universal Documentation Guideline (2022) & NEMSIS Standards

### Overview, Aliases & Goals
- **Aliases:** NEMSIS, Documentation.
- **Core Goals:** Support continuity of patient care and continuous performance improvement (CPI) via minimum documentation standards for all EMS events with a patient encounter; serves as a baseline alongside NEMSIS submission rules, state/local requirements, and billing reimbursement standards.
- **Inclusion Criteria:** All EMS events where a patient was encountered and at least one clinical guideline was used to guide treatment and/or disposition.

### Incident Demographics
- Captures incident type, location, time, dispatch data, response resources, and patient/incident disposition—applying even if a unit is cancelled before arrival or makes no patient contact, and supporting CPI filtering, aggregate data, and billing.
- **Key Fields:**
  - *Incident times:* Unit notified by dispatch date/time (NEMSIS mandatory), unit en route date/time, unit arrived on scene date/time (if arrived), arrived at patient date/time (if contact made), unit left scene date/time, patient arrived at destination date/time, unit back in service date/time (NEMSIS mandatory).
  - *Service & role:* Type of service requested (e.g., 911 vs. interfacility), primary unit role (transport vs. non-transport), complaint reported by dispatch (EMD reason).
  - *Crew & scene:* Crew member ID (name or license #), crew member level (license level for the call), crew member response role (primary vs. secondary caregiver), incident location type (e.g., used for CARES).
  - *Response mode & delays:* Response mode to scene (e.g., lights and sirens) and additional descriptors; type of response delay and type of scene delay.

### Patient Demographics, Medical History & Complaints
- **Patient Demographics & History (Minimum CPI Fields):** Gender, age, age units, medication allergies, environmental/food allergies, medical/surgical history, current medications, alcohol/drug use indicators, barriers to patient care, and estimated body weight in kilograms and length-based tape measure (without PHI unless required locally for billing or outcome linking).
- **Complaints, Signs, Symptoms & Key Times:** Possible injury; patient complaint group (complaint type, complaint, duration, duration time units, chief complaint anatomic location, chief complaint organ system); signs and symptoms (date/time of symptom onset, primary symptom, other associated symptoms, date/time last known well for stroke/CVA).
- **SAMPLE History Mapping in ePCR:**
  - **S**ymptoms: Primary symptom and other associated symptoms
  - **A**llergies: Medication allergies and environmental/food allergies
  - **M**edications: Current medications
  - **P**ast medical and surgical history: Medical/surgical history
  - **L**ast oral intake: If configured in software and/or in the PCR narrative
  - **E**vents leading to activation of EMS: Patient activity and/or PCR narrative
- **Condition-Specific Datasets:**
  - *Barriers/confounders:* Barriers to patient care; alcohol/drug use indicators.
  - *Stroke:* Date/time last known well (stroke/CVA).
  - *Trauma:* Possible injury (Yes/No based on mechanism, not listing an injury); cause/mechanism of injury (ICD-10 values, with intent embedded in ICD-10 rather than a separate NEMSIS v2 field); Trauma Center Criteria and vehicular/pedestrian/other injury risk factors (per ACS-COT 2022 National Guideline for Field Triage of Injured Patients); occupant safety equipment use; destination pre-arrival alerts (e.g., trauma alert and activation date/time).
  - *Cardiac arrest (Utstein Criteria & CARES-aligned fields):* Cardiac arrest, cardiac arrest etiology, resuscitation attempted by EMS, arrest witnessed by, CPR care prior to EMS arrival, who provided prior CPR, AED use prior to EMS arrival, who used AED, type of CPR provided, first monitored arrest rhythm, any return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), date/time of cardiac arrest, date/time resuscitation discontinued, reason CPR/resuscitation discontinued, cardiac rhythm on arrival at destination, end of EMS cardiac arrest event, and other EMS/public safety agencies at scene (agency ID and service type).

### Clinician Impressions, Disposition, Exams & Vitals
- **Clinician Impressions (Field Working Diagnosis):**
  - *Primary impression [single choice]:* Represents the **most acute (primary) problem**—not necessarily the first problem that occurred or the initial complaint.
  - *Secondary impressions [choose all that apply]:* Additional working impressions.
  - Symptoms (subjective) and signs (objective) must support—not simply repeat—the working diagnosis (except in rare cases like unexplained weakness when no etiology can be identified); accurate impressions are vital for reimbursement, registries (such as CARES), and CPI reviews (e.g., coding an opioid overdose reversed with naloxone as opiate/heroin overdose rather than unconsciousness or respiratory depression).
- **Incident/Patient Disposition:** Initial patient acuity (prior to EMS care), final patient acuity (after EMS care), incident/patient disposition, EMS transport method, transport mode from scene (e.g., lights and sirens) and additional descriptors, and destination/transferred-to name.
- **Assessments and Exams:**
  - *eExam Fields:* Optional in NEMSIS and sometimes omitted due to granular detail, though useful for targeted documentation in stroke, spinal exams, and trauma.
  - *Capacity Assessment Group:* Documents basic exam/history findings used in evaluating patient capacity for refusal or transport (barriers, alcohol/drug indicators, GCS, AVPU, mental status, neurologic exam), without serving as a legal definition of capacity or replacing clinical judgment and medical direction consultation.
  - *Targeted Condition Groups:*
    - **Stroke:** Initial vitals, date/time last known well, stroke score group, mental status, neurologic exam (speech, facial droop, arm drift, unilateral weakness), and reperfusion checklist (if used locally).
    - **Spinal injury/exam:** Capacity assessment group, back/spine assessment group (location and finding), and extremity assessment group (location and finding).
    - **12-lead EKG acquisition:** Unit arrived on scene date/time, arrived at patient date/time, EKG rhythm group, attached 12-lead graphic (via monitor integration or scanned printout), and 12-lead EKG procedure entry.
    - **Trauma/injury:** Targeted trauma findings (deformity, bleeding, burns) and pertinent negatives such as *"exam finding not present"* to show an area was examined without finding abnormality.
- **Vitals & Tiered Vitals Sets:**
  - Each entry requires a vitals date/time group (and whether obtained prior to this unit's EMS care).
  - *Core Vitals Groups:* GCS group (date/time, eye, verbal, motor, qualifier, total GCS); EKG rhythm group (date/time, cardiac rhythm/EKG, EKG type, interpretation method); Temperature group (date/time, temperature, method); Pain scale group (date/time, score, type); Stroke score group (date/time, score, type); Additional vitals (mean arterial pressure, pulse rhythm, respiratory effort, EtCO2, carbon monoxide [CO], blood glucose level, AVPU, APGAR).
  - *Tiered Vitals Sets:*
    - **Routine vitals:** Date/time group, BP (SBP/DBP), heart rate, pulse oximetry, respiratory rate, AVPU, and pain scale group.
    - **Initial vitals:** Routine vitals + blood glucose + GCS group + temperature group.
    - **Full vitals:** Initial vitals + pulse rhythm + respiratory effort + EtCO2 (if available/applicable) + EKG rhythm group (if available/applicable).

### Medications, Procedures, Responses & NEMSIS Data Architecture
- **Medications Given:** Date/time administered, given prior to this unit's EMS care, medication given (with pertinent negative qualifiers: *contraindication noted, medication already taken, denied by order, refused, medication allergy, unable to complete*), route, dosage, dosage units, response to medication, medication complication, administering crew ID, and role/license level.
  - *Protocol Medication Pearl:* When a protocol medication is already satisfied prior to arrival (e.g., a cardiac chest pain patient taking **324 mg** of aspirin per 911 pre-arrival instructions), record it as a prior-to-arrival medication with estimated time and the pertinent negative *"medication already taken."*
  - *Medication Response Definitions:*
    - **Improved:** Intended therapeutic effect achieved and symptoms decreased or condition improved/resolved (even if a side effect in another system occurs, which should also be logged as a complication, such as nitroglycerin relieving chest pain while dropping BP).
    - **Unchanged:** Ineffective or sub-therapeutic/unnoticeable effect with **no** deterioration in patient condition.
    - **Worse:** Patient condition deteriorated or kept deteriorating due to an ineffective medication, a sub-therapeutic effect failing to halt decline, or the wrong medication worsening the condition (e.g., giving glucose in hyperglycemia/DKA).
- **Procedures Performed:** Date/time performed, performed prior to this unit's EMS care, procedure (with pertinent negatives: *contraindication noted, refused, denied by order, unable to complete*), equipment size, number of attempts (always **"1"** per separate timestamped attempt), procedure successful, procedure complication, response to procedure, performing crew ID, role/license level, and vascular access location (if applicable).
  - *Procedure Response Definitions:*
    - **Not applicable:** Procedure has no direct expected clinical response (e.g., patient assessment, 12-lead EKG acquisition).
    - **Improved:** Intended outcome achieved and/or symptoms decreased or condition improved/resolved (e.g., defibrillation restoring a perfusing rhythm from VF or intubation securing the airway), even if a minor complication occurs (e.g., minor airway trauma during successful intubation).
    - **Unchanged:** Did not achieve intended effect without directly worsening condition (e.g., persistent VF after defibrillation, ongoing bleeding despite a bandage), or had no direct expected clinical response (assessment).
    - **Worse:** Condition/symptoms worsened as a result of the procedure (e.g., VF converting to asystole after defibrillation, or increased pain/neurovascular loss after splinting), noting that worsening does not automatically mean the procedure was performed incorrectly.
- **NEMSIS Data Standards, Qualifiers & Naming Structure:**
  - *Versions:* v2.2.1 (adopted 2006; accepted through 12/31/2016) and v3 (created 2011; v3.3.4 released March 2014 and accepted through 12/31/2017; **v3.4** released March 2015 and sole standard as of August 2021).
  - *Mandatory vs. Required elements:* **Mandatory** fields require real data to be stored/moved electronically and do not accept nil, not values, or pertinent negatives; **Required** fields must be present for electronic storage/transfer but allow nil, not values, or pertinent negatives.
  - *Not values (NV), nil, and pertinent negatives (PN):* Not values (*"not applicable"* or *"not recorded"*) clarify null/export rules (e.g., *"not applicable"* under `eInjury.03` Trauma Center Criteria for a simple sprained ankle); nil values are blank fields (accidental omission or intentional when nothing applies); pertinent negatives (up to 11 values depending on field) qualify findings, such as *"medication already taken"* or *"Chest → gunshot wound → Exam Finding Not Present."*
  - *Element & value naming:* Organized under **Demographic (`d`)** and **EMS (`e`)** parent datasets and groups (e.g., `eVitals.06 — SBP (systolic blood pressure)`), using background codes (from ICD-10, SNOMED, or RxNorm) paired with clinician-facing text labels and optional state/local custom elements.

## 4. Alternative Documentation Formats & Medical Abbreviations

### Alternative Narrative Documentation Methods
- Structured mnemonics organize PCR narrative information in logical sequences to reduce missed facts:
  - **Medical Narrative:** Chronological story format—intuitive and easy to start with, though structureless enough that details can be overlooked.
  - **SOAP:**
    - **S — Subjective:** Information the patient must tell you—symptoms you cannot see or feel during physical exam (e.g., *"I have terrible head pain"*).
    - **O — Objective:** Information identified during physical exam via inspection, palpation, auscultation, and smell—signs (e.g., observing a large laceration).
    - **A — Assessment:** Field assessment formed by combining subjective and objective findings with scene assessment, chief complaint, and bystander/family input (e.g., surmising hypoglycemia in a diabetic patient who is disoriented with pale, cool, clammy skin and tachycardia).
    - **P — Plan:** Plan of action and emergency care provided (e.g., administering oral glucose and oxygen and transporting).
    - *System Variation:* **SOAPIE** adds **I** (*intervention*) and **E** (*evaluation*) to track ongoing assessment.
  - **CHART (or DCHART):**
    - *(Optional **D** — Dispatch reason in DCHART)*
    - **C — Chief Complaint:** Main complaint of the patient.
    - **H — History:** Patient history, including SAMPLE history.
    - **A — Assessment:** Findings across primary assessment, secondary assessment, detailed physical exam, and ongoing assessment.
    - **R — Rx:** Treatment provided to the patient.
    - **T — Transport:** Condition changes en route and type of transport (e.g., emergency).
  - **CHEATED:**
    - **C — Chief Complaint:** Reason EMS was called.
    - **H — History:** Patient history, including SAMPLE history.
    - **E — Exam:** Physical examination findings.
    - **A — Assessment:** Field impression derived from history and exam findings.
    - **T — Treatment:** Treatment provided to the patient.
    - **E — Evaluation:** Ongoing assessment findings and any improvement or deterioration.
    - **D — Disposition:** Transfer of patient care at the medical facility or to another health care provider.

### Medical Abbreviations & Terminology Rules
- **Best Practices & Safety Rules:**
  - Medical abbreviations save space and time on the PCR, but **never make up your own abbreviations**, as they may convey a different meaning or no meaning to other clinicians.
  - **Writing terms out is preferred** over abbreviating because even accepted abbreviations can be interpreted differently (e.g., `PE` can mean *physical exam* or *pulmonary embolism*).
  - Follow your agency's approved abbreviation list; accurate usage also supports billing and reimbursement.
  - Verify proper medical terminology and spelling (consult a medical dictionary if unsure); if uncertain about a term's meaning or applicability, use everyday language instead to prevent confusion and treatment errors.

## 5. Legal Concerns in Documentation

### Confidentiality & Distribution
- **Confidentiality:**
  - Confidentiality is a patient's legal right; treat the PCR and all information on it as confidential under **HIPAA (1996)** and never show the form or discuss its contents with unauthorized individuals.
- **Distribution Rules:**
  - By law, confidential patient information may generally be shared with:
    1. A health care provider who needs the information to continue care
    2. Police requesting information as part of a criminal investigation
    3. A third party when required on a third-party billing form
    4. A court when required by a legal subpoena
  - **Typical Copy Allocation:** One copy left with the patient at the ED/receiving facility; original retained by the EMS agency; third copy optionally sent to medical oversight or a QI official (electronic data transfers follow the same model, using an abbreviated transfer-of-care/"drop" report when immediate return to service or lack of printing prevents leaving the full report immediately).

### Refusal of Treatment & Documentation Standards
- Because a patient's decision-making capacity is frequently challenged after the fact, document thoroughly across an **eight-step refusal protocol**:
  1. **Assess & Stay Safe:** Perform as thorough an assessment as permitted while prioritizing scene safety; document findings and any care rendered.
  2. **Re-Persuade & Evaluate Capacity:** Make one more effort to persuade the patient to accept hospital transport; verify rational capacity (not impaired by alcohol/drugs, illness/injury, or suicidal intent—noting alcohol use is nuanced because a patient may have consumed alcohol without lacking capacity, or be intoxicated while still understanding refusal risks).
  3. **Explain Risks & Confirm Understanding:** Clearly explain why hospital transport is needed and the consequences of delaying or refusing care; confirm understanding.
  4. **Consult Medical Direction & Family:** Contact medical direction per local protocol (physician input carries extra weight and can guide scene care) and enlist family members to help persuade the patient.
  5. **Document Thoroughly:** Record all findings, care provided, explanations of consequences (including potential death), any refused assessment/treatment elements, and explicit proof that the patient is alert and oriented to time, place, and person/self with capacity backed by an adequate assessment.
  6. **Obtain Refusal Form & Witnesses:** Have the patient sign a refusal-of-care form (e.g., *Acknowledgment of Refusal of Treatment and/or Transport*) along with a witness (family member, police officer, or bystander, with witness addresses if possible); never obtain a signature without full disclosure, and remember waivers have limited legal value and never replace thorough objective documentation.
  7. **Handle Refusal to Sign:** If the patient refuses to sign, have a family member, police officer, or bystander sign to verify the refusal, and complete every section of the form.
  8. **Offer Alternatives & Return Options:** Suggest alternative evaluation options (e.g., taxi to clinic, family/friends), explain that EMS will gladly return if the patient changes their mind, and advise on specific warning signs/symptoms of worsening condition.

### Falsification & Correcting Errors
- **Zero Tolerance for Falsification:**
  - Never falsify a PCR or cover up an error of omission or commission; document what actually happened and any corrective actions taken.
  - Falsification risks certification/license suspension or revocation, criminal charges, and patient harm from misleading clinical records.
  - High-risk temptations include fabricating unobtained vital signs, omitting unauthorized medication assistance, or claiming oxygen was administered when `SpO2 < 94%` was missed.
  - Exaggerating patient condition or care for higher insurance or **Medicare** reimbursement can constitute Medicare fraud and abuse and violate the **False Claims Act** (federally investigated).
- **Proper Error-Correction Methods:**
  - *Paper PCR before submission:* Draw a **single horizontal line** through the error, initial it, and write the correct information beside it—never erase or write over mistakes.
  - *Paper PCR after submission:* Draw a **single horizontal line** through the error (preferably in a different ink color), add a note with the correct information, initial it, include the **date and time** of correction, add a dated/initialed note for any omitted information, and notify recipients of the original incorrect report.
  - *Electronic PCR (ePCR):* Use the system's timestamped amendment feature if available; if unavailable, correct a printed copy using traditional paper-correction rules and resubmit as a corrected copy.

### Transfer-of-Care Reports, MCIs & Special Reports
- **Transfer-of-Care Report ("Drop Report"):**
  - When an immediate return to service or lack of on-site printing prevents leaving a full PCR at the receiving facility, use an abbreviated transfer-of-care/"drop" report to capture minimal data and obtain a receiving clinician's signature, use it as a reference to complete the full PCR later, and submit a copy of the transfer report with the full PCR.
- **Multiple-Casualty Incidents (MCIs):**
  - High patient volume may require brief initial recording on a **triage tag** attached to the patient (chief complaint, vital signs, treatment provided) to support later PCR completion.
  - MCI PCRs are typically less detailed than routine single-patient runs and must follow local MCI plan guidelines.
- **Special Reports:**
  - Required in addition to or outside the standard PCR when state law, local protocol, or circumstances require notifying external agencies/authorities, including:
    - Suspected child or elder abuse
    - Suspicious burns or gunshot wounds
    - Possible exposure to an infectious disease (e.g., meningitis, hepatitis, TB, HIV)
    - Injury to an EMS team member
  - **Potential State-Level Reporting Triggers:** Patient death/injury/fatality from direct EMS commission or omission of care; EMS unit crash injuring/killing a patient, crew member, or other person; EMS crew member killed or injured severely enough to require hospitalization while on duty; patient harm/compromised care from equipment failure; alleged alcohol or drug use by an EMS crew member responding to a call.
  - **Reporting Standards:** Keep special reports accurate, descriptive, and objective without jumping to conclusions; include names of all individuals, services, and facilities involved; submit promptly per local protocol; and retain a copy for agency records.

## 6. Medical Necessity, Reimbursement & the ET3 Payment Model

### Documenting to Support Medical Necessity
- How the EMS crew documents the scene, assessment findings, treatment, and transport directly determines whether commercial insurance, Medicare, or Medicaid reimburses the agency—and how much is paid.
- Reimbursement generally requires that ambulance transport be **"medically necessary."**
- **Commercial Insurance — The Prudent Layperson Standard:**
  - Evaluates whether a person of average health and medical knowledge would anticipate that the condition/situation would result in serious health impairment without emergency care—or whether a prudent person in that exact situation would seek an alternative mode of transport (e.g., obvious non-emergency = two-week-old toothache vs. obvious emergency = MVC with multiple-system trauma; borderline calls depend heavily on thorough documentation).
- **Medicare — Federal Law (42 CFR § 410.40(d)):**
  - Covers ambulance services (ground, fixed-wing, rotary-wing) **only** when furnished to a beneficiary whose medical condition makes other means of transport contraindicated.
  - Requires proving that the condition necessitated **both** the ambulance transportation itself **and** the level of service provided—demonstrating why lesser transport modes (private vehicle, wheelchair van, taxi, bus) were contraindicated.
- **Medicaid — State Law & Specific Criteria:**
  - Administered at the state level; medical necessity definitions vary by state and may enforce specific clinical criteria (such as requiring continuous oxygen administration) or qualifying condition lists.

### The ET3 Payment Model (Emergency Treat, Triage, and Transport)
- **Program Overview:**
  - Announced by **CMS** and **HHS** in **2019**; launched **January 5, 2020**, for a five-year voluntary performance evaluation period.
  - **Core Objectives:** Increase ambulance billing reimbursements; reimburse EMS providers for non-emergency transport to alternate (non-ED) destinations; reduce ED crowding and unnecessary ED visits; and save patients money.
- **Reimbursable Pathways:**
  - *Triage & Alternate Destination Transport:* Reimburses scene triage and transport to an approved non-ED facility.
  - *Non-Transport Reimbursement:* Reimburses non-transports when the patient is treated on scene by a qualified health care provider or via telehealth.
- **Documentation Standards to Support Medical Necessity:**
  - Record truthful, factual, and accurate details across five key elements:
    1. Dispatched and reported patient complaint or condition
    2. Patient's condition on the scene
    3. All treatments and services provided, explicitly including the patient's response
    4. Objective assessment findings, including patient appearance and mental status
    5. Factual, accurate detail supporting the medical necessity of ambulance transport

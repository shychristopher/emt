# Chapter 1: Emergency Medical Service Systems, Research, and Public Health

## Part I: EMS Systems, Clinical Roles, and Evidence-Based Practice

### 1.1: Introduction
- **Health Care Continuum:** Positions prehospital emergency care within the broader continuum from sudden illness or injury onset through hospital stabilization, rehabilitation, or discharge.
- **Dual Foundation of Modern EMS:** Combines immediate out-of-hospital clinical care with system organization, evidence-based guidelines, quality improvement, and population-level public health integration.
- **Public Health Burden:** Catastrophic accidents and sudden illnesses cause thousands of preventable deaths and permanent disabilities annually when EMS is inadequate or unavailable.

### 1.2: The Emergency Medical Services (EMS) System

#### A. Historical Evolution & Milestones
- **Early Origins:** Early ambulances frequently operated out of local funeral homes, prioritizing rapid transport over active medical treatment prior to hospital arrival.
- **Military Trauma Influence:**
  - *Korean & Vietnam Conflicts:* Demonstrated that immediate field stabilization before transport significantly improved survival, driving civilian EMS toward structured prehospital medical care.
  - *Iraq & Afghanistan Conflicts:* Continued to advance modern civilian trauma care through battlefield resuscitation and hemorrhage-control lessons.
- **Key Legislative, Policy & Educational Timeline:**

| Year | Milestone / Document | Significance to EMS Development |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1960s–1970s** | **CPR & Public BLS Training** | CPR techniques developed in the 1960s; AHA began teaching CPR/BLS to the public in the 1970s (now a standard EMT course prerequisite). |
| **1966** | ***Accidental Death and Disability: The Neglected Disease of Modern Society*** | National Academy of Sciences / National Research Council "White Paper" exposing severe deficiencies in prehospital care and spurring ambulance, training, and clinical reforms. |
| **1966** | **Highway Safety Act** | Required states to meet federal highway safety standards (including emergency services), placing DOT and NHTSA in charge of system and national curriculum development. |
| **1973** | **Emergency Medical Services System Act** | Provided major federal funding for regional EMS system planning, implementation, staffing, and training. |
| **1993** | **NREMT Education and Practice Blueprint** | Standardized training and education frameworks to guide national curriculum development. |
| **1996 & 2000** | **NHTSA *EMS Agendas for the Future*** | The 1996 Agenda pushed for full integration of EMS into the broader U.S. health care system; the 2000 *Education Agenda* promoted national consistency across education, certification, and licensure. |
| **2005–2006** | **Core Content, Scope of Practice & IOM Report** | NHTSA/HRSA published *National EMS Core Content* (2005) and *National EMS Scope of Practice Model* (2006, defining 4 provider levels). IOM published *EMS at the Crossroads* (2006), advocating state reciprocity, paramedic program accreditation, and national certification for licensure. |
| **2014–2018** | ***EMS Agenda 2050*** | Initiated via NEMSAC recommendation and developed through national stakeholder meetings to establish a 30-year, people-centered vision for EMS. |

#### B. Technical Assistance Program Assessment Standards & System Design
- **State Authority:** Individual states design and regulate their own EMS systems independently of the federal government, using NHTSA's **10 Technical Assistance Program Assessment Standards** as benchmarks:
  1. **Regulation and policy:** State laws, regulations, policies, procedures, and a lead state EMS agency.
  2. **Resource management:** Central control ensuring equitable community and patient access to emergency care.
  3. **Human resources and training:** Ambulance and transport personnel trained to at least the EMT level.
  4. **Transportation:** Safe, reliable ground or air ambulance transport.
  5. **Facilities:** Timely delivery of seriously ill or injured patients to an appropriate medical facility.
  6. **Communications:** Public access plus reliable communication among dispatch, EMS units, and hospitals.
  7. **Public information and education:** Injury prevention outreach and education on appropriate EMS activation.
  8. **Medical direction:** Physician medical director oversight, patient care supervision, and delegation of medical practice.
  9. **Trauma systems:** Specialized trauma networks encompassing trauma centers, rehabilitation, and triage/transport protocols.
  10. **Evaluation:** Quality improvement (QI) programs for continuous system evaluation and upgrades.
- **Six System Design Goals (Gunderson, 2015):** Clinical quality, service quality, economic efficiency, accountability, improvement, and resilience.
- **Modern EMS Service Lines (Gunderson, 2015):** Prevention and public education; triage; medical first response; ambulance response and transport; medical transportation; event coverage; disaster services; critical care transport; air medical transport; hazmat medical support; tactical medical support; and community paramedicine.

#### C. Access to the EMS System (911 vs. Non-911)
- **911 vs. 10-Digit Non-911 Numbers:** 911 provides universal, immediate access to police, fire, and EMS without requiring callers to look up local 10-digit numbers, enabling rapid simultaneous multi-agency dispatch.
- **Enhanced 911 (E-911) & PSAPs:**
  - **Public Service Answering Point (PSAP):** Receives, verifies, and records calls, selects appropriate agencies, and dispatches responders.
  - **E-911 Features:** Automatically displays **Automatic Number Identification (ANI)** and **Automatic Location Identification (ALI)** on landlines—crucial if a caller hangs up, is a young child, or is incapacitated (e.g., stroke or confusion).
  - **Emergency Medical Dispatchers (EMDs):** Trained dispatchers who provide pre-arrival care instructions (such as bleeding control or CPR) while units respond.
- **Wireless & VoIP Considerations:**
  - **Cellular Calls (~70% of 911 volume, rising):** Lack a fixed street address, may route to a boundary PSAP, and initially connect via cell towers.
  - **FCC Wireless Tiers:**
    - *Basic 911:* Transmits all 911 calls regardless of carrier subscription.
    - *Phase I E-911:* Provides callback number and cell tower/base station location.
    - *Phase II E-911:* Provides caller latitude/longitude within **50 to 300 meters** (many systems incorporate GPS tracking).
  - **VoIP:** FCC requires VoIP providers to supply 911 as a standard feature and transmit the subscriber's callback number and registered physical address to the PSAP.

#### D. EMS Service Delivery Models
- **Fire Department EMS:** Integrated into fire suppression services (paid, part-paid, or volunteer); may provide transport or non-transport first response, primarily focused on 911 emergencies.
- **Municipal EMS ("Third Service"):** Distinct from fire and police, operated directly by local government (e.g., public safety or public health department); primarily 911 response with occasional inter-facility/non-emergency transport.
- **Private EMS:** Operated by private companies under municipal contracts/regulations; provides 911 response, inter-facility transfers, and non-emergency transport.
- **Hospital-Based EMS:** Operated by for-profit or not-for-profit hospitals; functions similarly to private EMS across 911 and inter-facility transport.
- **Law Enforcement EMS:** Rare model seen in smaller, lower-volume jurisdictions leveraging existing patrol readiness.
- **Public Safety Officer:** Personnel cross-trained to perform fire, police, and EMS duties within a single unified agency.

#### E. Levels of Training (*National EMS Scope of Practice Model*)

| Provider Level | Core Scope of Practice & Capabilities |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Emergency Medical Responder (EMR)** | Immediate lifesaving care prior to ambulance arrival: basic airway/ventilation/oxygen devices, vital signs, spinal/extremity stabilization, eye irrigation, bleeding control, emergency moves, CPR, AED, and emergency childbirth. |
| **Emergency Medical Technician (EMT)** | Basic emergency care and ambulance transport: builds on EMR skills with advanced oxygen/ventilation equipment, pulse oximetry, automated BP monitors, and limited medication administration/assistance. |
| **Advanced EMT (AEMT)** | Basic plus limited advanced care and transport: adds advanced airway devices, blood glucose monitoring, IV and intraosseous (IO) fluid therapy, and select medications. |
| **Paramedic** | Highest prehospital tier: builds on EMT/AEMT care with advanced assessments, field impressions, invasive procedures, and expanded pharmacology to reduce morbidity and mortality. |

#### F. Health Care System & Specialty Referral Centers
- **Prehospital vs. Out-of-Hospital Care:** *Prehospital care* refers to emergency care prior to hospital arrival; *out-of-hospital care* more broadly includes scene care (with or without transport) and inter-facility transfers.
- **Specialty Referral Facilities:** Trauma center (rapid surgical intervention exceeding standard ED capability); Burn center; Obstetrical center; Pediatric center; Poison center; Stroke center; Cardiac center; Hyperbaric center (diving emergencies, carbon monoxide/toxic exposures); Spine injury center; Psychiatric center.
- **Interagency Coordination:** EMTs operate as part of a team alongside partners, fire, rescue, and law enforcement, serving as the medical liaison on multi-agency scenes.

### 1.3: The EMT — Roles, Responsibilities, and Professional Attributes

#### A. Core Roles & Responsibilities
- **Primary Domains:** Personal safety and safety of others; patient assessment and emergency care; safe lifting and moving; patient transport and transfer of care; record keeping and data collection; and patient advocacy.
- **Specific Duties:** Maintain vehicle and equipment readiness; ensure safety of the EMS crew, patient, and bystanders; operate the emergency vehicle safely; assess the patient and provide emergency care; safely lift, move, transport, and transfer patient care; prepare oral and written reports and perform record keeping/data collection; serve as patient advocate and provide emotional support to patients, relatives, and bystanders; integrate EMS with other emergency/nonemergency services; maintain medical/legal standards, provide administrative support, enhance professional development, and maintain community relations.
- **Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) of 1990:** Prohibits employment discrimination against individuals with documented disabilities and requires employers to provide reasonable accommodations.

#### B. Personal Safety & Scene Control (Top Priority)
- **Hierarchy of Safety:** Personal safety is the **#1 priority**—an injured EMT cannot help patients and forces other rescuers to divert resources. Once scene safety is assured, patient needs take priority.
- **Driving & Vehicle Safety:** Drive defensively with due regard; wear seat belts at all times unless actively performing essential patient care in the patient compartment.
- **Scene & Environmental Hazards:** Avoid high-traffic zones, downed power lines, fuel/chemical spills, fires, and radiation hazards. **Never enter volatile scenes** (riots, active crime scenes, hostage situations) until law enforcement has secured and cleared the area. Exercise heightened caution around intoxicated, behavioral, or emotionally volatile individuals.
- **Protective Measures & PPE:** Follow police, fire, and utility directives; establish safe treatment zones and redirect traffic; wear high-visibility reflective gear at night and deploy adequate scene lighting; use trauma/extrication gear (helmet/hard hat, protective outerwear, eye protection, leather gloves) around glass/metal, and standard **infectious disease PPE** (gloves, eye protection, mask, gown) as indicated.

#### C. Clinical Care, Lifting, Transport, Documentation & Advocacy
- **Assessment & Care:** Gain patient access, triage multi-patient scenes, perform a **primary assessment** first to resolve immediate life threats (airway, breathing, cardiac arrest, severe hemorrhage), and follow with a **secondary assessment** and continuous reassessment.
- **Safe Lifting & Moving:** Use proper body mechanics, sufficient personnel, and appropriate emergency, urgent, or nonurgent moves/devices.
- **Transport & Pre-Arrival Notification:** Select appropriate destination facility per protocol/medical direction; give early pre-arrival notification (patient count, condition/injuries, trauma/stroke alerts); consult on-line medical direction en route if condition changes; use lights and sirens judiciously while obeying emergency vehicle laws.
- **Transfer of Care:** Assist moving wheeled stretcher into ED, deliver both a **verbal and written report**, and never depart until care is formally transferred.
- **Documentation & Patient Advocacy:** Maintain shift call logs and complete the **Prehospital Care Report (PCR)** prior to leaving the hospital or ASAP; safeguard and document personal valuables; protect patient privacy and shield patients/deceased bodies from onlookers; ensure family knows how to reach receiving facility, honor reasonable patient requests, and relay vital scene context to hospital staff.

#### D. Professional Attributes, Physical Demands & Credential Maintenance
- **Professional Behavior:** Integrity, empathy, self-motivation, professional appearance and hygiene, self-confidence, time management, strong verbal/written communication, teamwork and diplomacy, respect, patient advocacy, careful service delivery, and lifelong learning.
- **Physical & Sensory Demands:** Ability to lift and carry up to **125 pounds**; good visual acuity (corrected or uncorrected) and **good color vision** (essential for driving and detecting subtle cyanosis, pallor, or flushing in skin, nail beds, lips, and mucosa); adequate hearing for radio traffic, scene communication, and auscultating blood pressures and breath/body sounds.
- **Certification & Licensure:** Each EMT is individually responsible for tracking continuing education, verifying skills, maintaining ethical/legal standing, and submitting renewal fees on time.

### 1.4: EMS System Organization, Medical Oversight, and Safety

#### A. State EMS Agency Role (NASEMSO)
- Every U.S. state and territory designates a **lead EMS agency** responsible for: (1) statewide EMS system **planning**, (2) **coordination**, (3) **regulation**, and (4) **licensing** of local EMS agencies and individual personnel (distinct from national NREMT certification).

#### B. Medical Oversight, Medical Direction, Protocols & Standing Orders
- **EMT as Designated Agent:** An EMT cannot practice independently; all clinical care is rendered as the designated agent of the physician **medical director** under state law.
- **Forms of Medical Oversight:**
  - **Indirect (Off-Line) Medical Oversight:** Routine administrative and clinical governance (majority of medical director's role), including training, QI audits, written **Protocols** (comprehensive guidelines defining triage, treatment, transport, and destination decisions), and **Standing Orders (Off-Line Medical Direction)** (predetermined written subsets authorizing EMTs to administer specific treatments *without* contacting a physician first).
  - **Direct Medical Oversight:** Real-time physician direction during active care via **On-Line Medical Direction** (radio, phone, video telemedicine) or **On-Scene Medical Direction** (medical director physically on scene).
- **Chest Pain Protocol Breakdown:**
  - *Off-Line / Standing Orders:* (1) Oxygen via NRB at 15 L/min; (2) position of comfort; (3) 1 patient-prescribed nitroglycerin if SBP > 90 mmHg; (4) 160–325 mg aspirin; (5) request ALS.
  - *On-Line Medical Direction:* (6) Contacting medical control by radio/phone to request authorization for additional nitroglycerin doses if pain persists.

#### C. Quality Improvement (QI / CQI) & Patient Safety
- **Purpose of CQI:** Continuous internal and external reviews/audits designed to identify and fix *system-level* weaknesses and elevate patient care—not to punish individual providers.
- **Six EMT Roles in QI:** (1) Documentation (thorough PCRs reveal system trends), (2) reviews and audits, (3) feedback (patient/hospital surveys and informal ED feedback), (4) continuing education, (5) preventive maintenance of ambulances and gear, and (6) regular skill maintenance.
- **High-Risk Prehospital Activities & Error Prevention:** High-risk activities include patient hand-offs/transfers of care, communication breakdowns, lifting/carrying/moving patients, ambulance collisions, and omitting/improperly applying spinal motion restriction. Root causes are skill execution failure, rule/protocol violation, or knowledge gaps. Prevent via clear protocols, adequate lighting, minimal interruptions, clear med labeling, reflection, questioning assumptions, decision aids, asking for help early, and **closed-loop communication** (receiver repeats order back; sender confirms "yes").

### 1.5–1.7: EMS Research, Evidence-Based Guidelines & MIH-CP
- **Evidence-Based Medicine (EBM):** Replaces anecdotal habit with rigorous research across **four steps**: (1) formulate specific clinical question → (2) search peer-reviewed literature → (3) appraise scientific validity/reliability → (4) update protocols and implement.
- **National Research Infrastructure:** 2001 *National EMS Research Agenda*; 2014 FICEMS Strategic Goal; **NEMSIS** (national standardized EMS database); **NIGMS** (research funding/collaboration); **CARES** (largest U.S. out-of-hospital cardiac arrest registry linking 911/EMS/hospital survival); NHTSA *Safety in Numbers* (MVC injury analysis).
- **Scientific Method (8 Steps):** Pose question → literature review → hypothesis → run study → collect/analyze data → support/reject hypothesis → identify limitations → publish peer-reviewed findings.
- **8-Step National Prehospital EBG Cyclical Process (FICEMS & NEMSAC):** (1) System inputs (existing protocols and AHA guidelines) → (2) Guideline initiation (EMS evidence accumulation/evaluation) → (3) Establish priorities → (4) Guideline development → (5) EMS protocol development → (6) Dissemination → (7) Implementation → (8) Evaluation of effectiveness, outcomes, clinical research, and QI *(loops directly back to Step 2)*.
- **Mobile Integrated Healthcare & Community Paramedicine (MIH-CP):** **MIH** delivers patient-centered out-of-hospital health care using mobile technology/resources by various clinicians integrated with EMS; **CP** is delivered directly by an EMS agency and its providers integrated with broader health care entities (expanding underserved access, telephone triage/advice instead of automatic ambulance dispatch, managing high-utilizer/high-readmission and chronic disease patients, and preventive/post-discharge follow-ups).

### 1.8–1.13: Public Health Framework, Achievements & EMS Prevention Roles

| Dimension | Public Health Approach | Individual Medical Care Approach |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Primary Focus** | Population health | Individual patient health |

Winslow [1920], IOM *The Future of Public Health* [1988], U.S. Public Health Service *Public Health in America* [1994]).
- **CDC Framework (5 Arms & 3 Core Functions / 10 Essential Services):**
  - *Five Arms:* Epidemiology; biostatistics; health policy and management/administration; behavioral/social science and health education; environmental health.
  - *Three Core Functions:* (1) **Assessment** (monitor health status, diagnose/investigate hazards, educate/empower, research solutions); (2) **Policy Development** (mobilize community partnerships, develop policies/plans); (3) **Assurance** (enforce laws/regulations, link people to personal health services, ensure competent workforce, evaluate effectiveness/accessibility/quality).

- **Constitutional Authority & SDOH:** Primary legal responsibility rests with **state and local governments**. EMTs monitor **Social Determinants of Health (SDOH — 5 domains: economic stability, education access/quality, health care access/quality, neighborhood/built environment, social/community context)**, capture EHR surveillance data, and refer vulnerable patients (human trafficking, child/elder abuse, untreated mental illness).
- **Three Levels of EMS Prevention:**
  - *Primary Prevention (Before Onset):* DUI/fall/fire/seatbelt/car-seat/helmet campaigns, community health education, flu/COVID-19 vaccinations.
  - *Secondary Prevention (Early Detection / Limit Progression):* Blood pressure screenings, reporting unsafe homes or abuse/neglect, public-access AED placement, disaster/pandemic planning.
  - *Tertiary Prevention (Acute Treatment / Limit Disability):* Immediate emergency care, stabilization, and transport.

# Chapter 2: Workforce Safety and Wellness of the EMT

## 2.1: Emotional Aspects of Emergency Care

### Emotional Crises, Death & Dying (Kübler-Ross)
- **Distress & Crisis:** Distress is acute physical or mental suffering (pain, anxiety, strain, sorrow); emotional pressures exceeding coping capacity trigger an emotional crisis, and emergency care helps reestablish equilibrium.
- **Elizabeth Kübler-Ross's Five Emotional Stages** *(non-linear; not all 5 seen on every call; applies to major nonfatal loss like amputation)*:
  1. **Denial ("Not me."):** Defense mechanism buffering shock against approaching death or illness/injury realities.
  2. **Anger ("Why me?"):** May target the EMT; avoid defensiveness and respond empathetically with active listening.
  3. **Bargaining ("Okay, but first let me . . ."):** Attempts to make agreements perceived to postpone death briefly.
  4. **Depression ("Okay, but I haven't . . ."):** Silence, distance, sadness, and despair over unfinished matters or those left behind.
  5. **Acceptance ("Okay, I am not afraid."):** Acknowledgment of impending death (not happiness); families often need greater support at this stage.
- **Nine Principles for Interacting with Dying Patients, Families & Bystanders:**
  1. Prioritize emergency care while addressing dignity, respect, sharing, communication, privacy, and control.
  2. Maintain dignity: avoid negative remarks; speak to unresponsive patients as if alert and explain care.
  3. Show respect & sincerity: watch words and body language; allow family presence during resuscitation when appropriate; show you never simply "gave up."
  4. Communicate clearly: orient patient, explain events/roles/transport plans, give straightforward answers, report facts without guessing, and update family without interrupting care.
  5. Allow emotional expression: permit crying, screaming, or venting if safe; do not take directed anger personally.
  6. Listen empathetically: honor final requests when possible, stay with family until the medical examiner or coroner arrives, and arrange ambulance transport for a family member if feasible.
  7. Avoid false assurances: be honest and tactful without confirming death or destroying hope.
  8. Use a gentle tone: explain injury scope and procedures kindly in understandable terms.
  9. **Handle postmortem contact & crime scenes:** Clean blood/secretions/vomitus and elevate head briefly if allowed by local protocol; warn of severe trauma and cover injured areas; **do not** clean the body, remove blood, or allow family access/touching in a possible crime scene until cleared by law enforcement, coroner, or medical examiner.

### Stressors, Stress Reactions & Warning Signs
- **Everyday vs. High-Stress Calls:** Routine stressors include long hours, shift work, boredom between calls, overwork, low recognition, instant response demands, life-and-death decisions, fear of serious errors, grief/dying exposure, and responsibility for another life. High-stress calls include **MCIs**, **abuse/neglect** (infants, children, adults, elderly), **pediatric emergencies**, **injury/death of a coworker**, **caring for a relative/bystander**, and **severe traumatic injuries** (amputations).
- **Three Types of Stress Reactions:**
  - *Acute stress reaction:* Immediate or shortly after a high-stress incident (nausea, elevated HR, sweating, tremors, appetite shifts, trouble concentrating, insomnia); seek immediate medical evaluation for chest pain or shortness of breath.
  - *Delayed stress reaction:* Appears days, months, or years later (classic example: **PTSD**; nightmares, irritability, insomnia, impaired concentration, flashbacks, interpersonal conflict, substance risk → requires mental health care).
  - *Cumulative stress reaction:* Ongoing stress buildup driving **burnout** (exhaustion and irritability), emotional exhaustion, severe physical illness, reduced care effectiveness, or leaving EMS.
- **10 Core Warning Signs:** Irritability; inability to concentrate; difficulty sleeping/nightmares; anxiety; indecisiveness; guilt; loss of appetite; loss of sexual desire/interest; isolation; loss of interest in work.
- **5 Categories of Stress Signs & Symptoms:**
  - *Thinking:* Confusion, impaired judgment/decision-making, loss of motivation, chronic forgetfulness, loss of objectivity.
  - *Psychological:* Depression, excessive anger, negativism, hostility, defensiveness, mood swings, feelings of worthlessness.
  - *Physical:* Persistent exhaustion, headaches, GI distress, dizziness, pounding heart.
  - *Behavioral:* Overeating, increased alcohol or drug use, teeth grinding, hyperactivity, lack of energy.
  - *Social:* Increased interpersonal conflicts, reduced ability to view patients as individuals.

### Stress Management & Critical Incident Stress Management (CISM)
- **Lifestyle, Family & Work Strategies:**
  - *Diet & habits:* Reduce sugar, caffeine, and alcohol; increase lean protein, limit carbs, reduce saturated fat, eat small frequent meals; exercise regularly; use deep breathing (inhale, hold, forceful exhale), meditation, visual imagery, and healthy diversions; avoid alcohol/pills.
  - *Balance & work:* Prioritize tasks numerically; share openly with loved ones and protect dedicated family time; accept human fallibility; use a coworker "buddy" system, positive support, brief aerobic breaks, adequate rest shifts, and duty rotations out of high-violence areas; seek mental health care as a sign of strength.
- **CISM System (Defusing vs. CISD & 10 Program Components):**
  - *Defusing:* Held **up to 8 hours** post-incident for those most directly involved; lasts **30 to 45 minutes**; less structured venting and initial info before CISD.
  - *Critical Incident Stress Debriefing (CISD):* Ideally held **24 to 72 hours** after a critical incident; confidential and non-punitive; led by peer counselors and mental health professionals across **seven phases** (facts, feelings, signs/symptoms, sorting feelings, coping strategies, return-to-work planning, follow-up care).
  - *10 CISM Components:* Preincident stress education; on-scene peer support; one-on-one support; disaster support services; defusing; CISD; follow-up services; spouse and family support; community outreach programs; other health and welfare programs.

## 2.2: Scene Safety, Infection Control & Injury Prevention

### Core Role Boundaries & Pathogens
- **Golden Rule of Boundaries:** **Never** act in any capacity other than a medical responder unless specifically trained and authorized to do so.
- **Five Pathogen Categories:**
  - *Bacteria:* Single-celled organisms reproducing independently or in a host (sinus/ear infections, bacterial pneumonia, strep throat, TB, UTIs); typically antibiotic-treatable.
  - *Viruses:* Smaller than bacteria; require host cell; **not** treatable with antibiotics (cold, flu, HIV/AIDS, hepatitis A/B/C, viral pneumonia, SARS, varicella, RSV; emerging infectious diseases [EID]: COVID-19, Ebola [EVD], Zika [ZVD]).
  - *Fungi:* Plantlike organisms generally causing disease only in immunocompromised hosts.
  - *Protozoa:* Single-celled motile organisms (mosquito-borne malaria—infectious but not person-to-person communicable—or fecal-oral/GI organisms).
  - *Helminths:* Parasitic worms (roundworms, flukes, tapeworms; e.g., hookworm larvae penetrating skin and causing intestinal blood loss/anemia).
- **Transmission Modes:** Direct contact (blood-to-blood, open wounds, mucous membranes), indirect contact (contaminated objects/needles), and airborne droplets.

| **Whooping cough (pertussis)** | Respiratory secretions, airborne droplets | 6–20 days | Gloves, surgical mask, hand washing |
| **Staphylococcal skin infection** | Direct contact with infected lesion or contaminated object | 1–3 days | Gloves, hand washing |
| **SARS coronavirus / COVID-19** | Respiratory airborne, direct contact | SARS: 10 days; COVID-19: 3–7 days | Gloves, N-95 or N100 respirator, hand washing, eye protection |
| **Ebola virus disease (EVD)** | Direct contact with blood or body fluids | 2–21 days (average onset 8–10 days) | Gloves, hand washing, N-95 respirator, surgical/isolation gown, boot covers, specialized training in donning/doffing PPE |
| **Zika virus disease (ZVD)** | *Aedes* mosquito bite, pregnant woman to fetus, sexual contact | 3–12 days | Avoiding mosquito bites, abstaining from sex with infected/potentially infected partner (condoms reduce risk), preventing pregnancy, avoiding mosquito exposure in areas where ZVD occurs |

### Standard Precautions, Hand Hygiene, PPE & Decontamination
- **OSHA Standard Precautions & Exposure Reporting:** Formerly body substance isolation (BSI); requires written exposure control plan, training, immunizations, PPE, and post-exposure policy.
  - *Airborne infection (e.g., TB):* Facility diagnoses disease in transported patient → notifies designated officer (DO) within **24 hours** → DO notifies EMT → employer arranges medical evaluation and follow-up.
  - *Bloodborne infection (e.g., HIV, HBV):* EMT contacts blood/body fluids → seeks immediate medical care and documents for workers' compensation → requests DO determination → DO gathers info/consults receiving facility → facility reports findings to DO within **48 hours** → DO notifies EMT.
- **Hand Washing (Single Most Important Infection-Prevention Measure):**
  - *6-Step CDC Sequence:* (1) Remove hand jewelry → (2) wet hands with clean running water, turn off tap, apply soap → (3) lather backs of hands, between fingers, and under nails → (4) scrub at least **20 seconds** (10–15 sec vigorous lather removes most contaminants per USPHS) → (5) rinse well under clean running water → (6) dry with clean towel or air dry.
  - *Alcohol-based hand sanitizer:* Use **>=60% alcohol** when soap/water unavailable; wash with soap and water ASAP (overuse cracks skin, creating a pathogen portal).
- **PPE (Treat All Used PPE as Contaminated):**
  - *Eye protection:* Clear plastic eye/full-face shields, protective eyewear with side shields, or removable side shields on prescription glasses.
  - *Protective gloves:* Vinyl, latex-free, or synthetic gloves for care (never reuse across patients; replace if torn; wash hands after doffing); utility gloves for cleaning.
  - *Gowns:* Wear during significant blood/fluid contact (e.g., childbirth); remove soiled uniforms, bag/label, wash in hot soapy water for at least **25 minutes**, and take a hot shower (for gross contamination, stand in a labeled biohazard bag and strip top-to-bottom directly into the bag).
  - *Masks:* Surgical mask for splashes/droplets (or masking contagious patients); **HEPA** or **N-95** respirator for suspected TB/COVID-19 (must be clean-shaven; annual N-95 fit test).
- **Decontamination, Sharps & Immunizations:**
  - *Cleaning* (mechanical soap/water + friction) → *Disinfecting* (cleaning + chemical germicide/hospital-grade disinfectant or bleach on intact-skin items like backboards, splints, BP cuffs) → *Sterilization* (superheated steam autoclave destroying **all** microorganisms on items entering body or touching mucous membranes like laryngoscope blades).
  - Double-bag HIV/Hep B biohazardous waste; place sharps immediately into mounted puncture-proof containers (never recap needles); log exposures/cleaning.
  - *Screening & Vaccines:* Initial-hire TB screening via PPD test (annual testing not recommended unless exposure/risk); COVID-19, tetanus (Tdap once, then Td/Tdap booster every **10 years**; Tdap each pregnancy), Hepatitis B (2-dose Heplisav-B or 3-dose Recombivax HB/Engerix-B; offered free within **10 days** of exposure-risk assignment + titer), annual flu, MMR (born >=1957: 2 measles, 2 mumps, >=1 rubella + immunity verification), and varicella.

### Injury Prevention: Hazmat, Rescue & Violence/Crime
- **Hazardous Materials:** Scout from a distance using binoculars before approaching and check placards against the U.S. DOT *Emergency Response Guidebook*; do not enter until trained hazmat teams control the scene and decontaminate patients.
- **Rescue Situations & Roadway Apparel:** Stay clear of downed power lines, fire/explosions, structural collapse, low-oxygen confined spaces, unsecured trenches, WMD, and busy roadway MVAs until specialized teams/law enforcement secure scene; wear turnout gear, puncture-proof gloves, helmet with reflective tape/chin strap, eye protection, and weather/dust gear.
  - *High-Visibility Road Apparel:* **ANSI/ISEA 107-2004 Class 2 or Class 3**, or **ANSI/ISEA 207-2006 Public Safety Vest [PSV]** with breakaway design (Class 1 = `<25 mph` parking lots; Class 2 = `>=25 mph` or diverted attention; Class 3 = high traffic risk/high distraction).
- **Violence & Crime:** Call law enforcement before entering suspected violent/crime scenes (domestic disputes, hostile intoxication, fights, suicide, angry bystanders, behavioral emergencies, clandestine drug labs, "chemical suicides") and wait until cleared; plan alternate routes during civil unrest; know body armor limits; preserve crime-scene evidence while providing care.

## 2.3: Wellness Principles & Safe Glove Removal

### Physical & Mental Well-Being
- **Six Dimensions of Wellness:** Physical, intellectual, emotional, social, environmental, and spiritual (core focus: physical & mental).
- **Five Core Components of Physical Fitness:**
  1. *Cardiovascular Endurance:* Aerobic activity **30–40 minutes at least 3 days/week** at target HR. **70% Formula:** `Target HR = [((220 - Age) - RHR) × 0.70] + RHR` (e.g., age 28, RHR 62 → `192 - 62 = 130` → `130 × 0.70 = 91` → `91 + 62 = 153 bpm`).
  2. *Muscle Strength:* Maximum force in a single contraction via resistance training.
  3. *Muscle Endurance:* Repeated contractions without undue fatigue for sustained carries.
  4. *Muscle Flexibility:* Full joint range of motion via stretching.
  5. *Body Composition:* Lower body-fat ratio lowers risks for **heart disease**, **hypertension**, and **diabetes**.
- **Sleep & NASEMSO Fatigue Guidelines:** Shift work disrupts **circadian rhythms**; need **8–10 hours** sleep/day (half of EMS providers get `<6 hours`); use cool dark room, consistent schedule, no late caffeine/heavy meals/exercise, silent phones.
  - *5 NASEMSO Fatigue Guidelines:* (1) Monitor personal fatigue with validated surveys; (2) keep shifts shorter than **24 hours**; (3) provide caffeine access; (4) allow on-duty napping; (5) provide fatigue education.
- **Substance Risks, Resilience & Suicide Prevention:** Avoid smoking (cardiovascular, pulmonary, cancer risks) and avoid alcohol/drugs to cope (use **EAP**). Over **30%** of first responders have a formal mental health diagnosis (depression, PTSD); **resilience** (NAEMT) is coping without lasting harm; untreated **PTSD** carries a **6x** higher suicide risk than the general population (**24/7 National Suicide Hotline: 1-800-273-8255**).

### Safe Glove Removal Procedure (7 Steps)
- *Core Rule:* Contaminated glove surfaces touch only contaminated glove surfaces; clean inside surfaces touch only clean inside surfaces.
  1. Hook gloved fingers under outer/cuff edge of opposite glove without touching clean inner surfaces.
  2. Pull cuff down, keeping contaminated surfaces against contaminated surfaces.
  3. Pull glove completely off and inside out so fluids remain contained inside.
  4. Crumple removed contaminated glove inside remaining gloved hand and dispose in biohazard receptacle.
  5. Slide bare, clean fingers under clean inside cuff of remaining glove.
  6. Roll/pull second glove off and inside out using only clean inside surfaces.
  7. Drop inverted glove into biohazard receptacle without bare-skin contact with exterior.

# Chapter 3: Medical, Legal, and Ethical Issues

## 3.1: Scope, Standards, Duties, Protections & Ethics
- **Scope of Practice vs. Standard of Care:**
  - *Scope of Practice:* Defined by state laws (guided by NHTSA *National EMS Scope of Practice Model* and *National EMS Education Standards*) as the actions/care EMTs are **legally allowed** to perform (*"Was the EMT allowed by law to do what was done?"*, e.g., oxygen = legal; suturing = illegal even if trained). Purely defines legal boundaries, not protocols or standard of care.
  - *Standard of Care:* Care expected from a reasonably prudent EMT with similar training in a similar situation ("reasonable person standard"), evaluating (1) right assessment/care and (2) proper performance. Defined by EMT textbooks, regional peers, local/state protocols, NHTSA standards, and system SOPs. Falling below constitutes **negligence**.
- **Duty to Act (5 Dimensions):**
  - *On-Duty vs. Off-Duty:* On duty = legal obligation to respond and treat. Off duty = no legal duty in most states unless state law mandates aid, **except** if you publicly respond to a page or stop at a scene, which creates a **duty to act** (cannot leave until equal/higher training takes over; legally responsible for any patient property picked up).
  - *Duties to Patient, Self, Partner & Equipment:* Advocate/protect privacy/do no harm (patient); maintain credentials, skills, and physical/mental health (self); ensure partner is fit and report alcohol/drug impairment to supervisor (partner); verify equipment works at shift start (equipment malfunction/absence = negligence).
- **Legal Protections:**
  - *Good Samaritan Laws:* First enacted in California (**1959**); protects unpaid good-faith rescuers from civil damages unless **gross negligence** (markedly below standard of care / willful or wanton intent to harm); does *not* prevent being sued.
  - *Sovereign (Governmental) Immunity:* Protects government-operated EMS from civil liability (private EMS companies are *not* protected).
  - *Statute of Limitations:* State time limit (usually years) to file a negligence claim after injury or discovery.
  - *Contributory Negligence:* Patient's own negligence contributed to the injury/damage claimed.
- **Medical Direction & Ethics:** Legal right to function prehospital depends on **medical direction**. *Ethics* (study of morality) vs. *Morals* (right and wrong) vs. *Code of Ethics* (rules of ideal conduct; NAEMT issued EMT Code of Ethics in **1978**). When legal, ethical, and moral obligations conflict (e.g., expired DNR, MCI triage rationing, wilderness arrest), follow local protocols/laws and contact medical direction.

## 3.2: Patient Consent, Advance Directives & Refusal of Care
- **5-Step Consent Approach:** (1) Introduce yourself → (2) state intent to assess, treat, and transport → (3) ask if patient understands/has questions → (4) request explicit permission (even if patient called 911; extra care for unproven research) → (5) listen carefully without assuming blanket consent. Treating/touching without consent can constitute **battery**.
- **Four Types of Consent:**
  1. *Expressed Consent:* From conscious, legal-age adult with rational capacity after understandable explanation of procedures and risks (verbal preferred; nodding acceptable; document).
  2. *Implied Consent (Emergency Doctrine):* Assumed in true emergencies posing significant risk of death, disability, or deterioration when patient is unresponsive, irrational (head injury), or becomes incapacitated after initial refusal.
  3. *Minor Consent:* From parent, legal guardian, or authorized caregiver (under **18** or **21** per state law). **Emergent Minor Implied Consent** applies under 6 conditions (emergent threat, guardian unavailable, cannot safely delay, treat immediate threats only, act in minor's best interest when in doubt, contact medical direction). **Emancipated minors** (married, pregnant, parent, armed forces, financially independent living away, court decree) do not need parent/guardian consent.
  4. *Involuntary Consent:* For mentally incompetent adults or individuals in law enforcement custody/incarcerated; obtained from third party with legal authority (legal guardian, police officer, court officer).
- **End-of-Life Care Advance Directives (*Patient Self-Determination Act*):**
  - *Living Will:* Directs end-of-life care when terminally ill or permanently comatose; requires **two physicians** to confirm incapacity and terminal/comatose status; may still allow insulin, antibiotics, food, or pain meds; can be rescinded anytime.
  - *Durable Power of Attorney (Medical Power of Attorney / Health Care Proxy):* Names a proxy to make health care decisions if patient loses capacity; requires **physician** certification of incapacity; ends if capacity returns.
  - *Do Not Resuscitate (DNR) / DNR Comfort Care (DNRCC):* Standardized form signed by physician and patient prohibiting resuscitation once pulseless and apneic (DNRCC permits oxygen, bleeding control, pain meds). Cannot act on DNR until cardiac arrest (if pulse present, give standard/comfort care). Must confirm valid physical DNR (form, card, bracelet)—never rely solely on family/staff word; if missing/questionable, resuscitate and clarify with medical direction.
  - *POLST (MOLST / MOST / POST / TPOPP):* For serious/terminal illness with life expectancy **<=1 year**; specifies pre-arrest treatment limits (e.g., meds allowed, no intubation/defibrillation); signed by physician (or PA/NP in some states) with valid dates and contact info.
  - *Field Ambiguity & NASEMSO Guidelines:* Reject vague phrases (*"no heroics"*); do not waste resuscitative time reading living wills; when validity or family conflict arises, start care immediately, contact medical direction, and continue until resolved.
- **Refusing Treatment (Capacity vs. Competence & 7-Step Protection):**
  - *Capacity* (medical ability to understand and decide rationally) vs. *Competence* (legal court status; lacking capacity without court ruling = *de facto incompetent*). Altered mental status, mental illness, intoxication, impairment, or suicidal intent impairs capacity.
  - *7 Steps:* (1) Perform thorough assessment (or document declined exam) → (2) re-persuade patient/family in plain language and have patient read back report → (3) confirm capacity (not suicidal/impaired) → (4) consult medical direction per protocol → (5) stay safe (call police if needed; balance avoiding abandonment vs. forcing care) → (6) advise specific warning symptoms to call back for → (7) document thoroughly and get signed release + witness signature (or document witnessed refusal to sign).

## 3.3: Liability, Confidentiality, Federal Laws & Special Field Situations
- **Criminal vs. Civil Liability & Negligence:**
  - *Criminal Liability* (government prosecutes → fine/incarceration) vs. *Civil Liability* (**plaintiff** sues **defendants** for a **tort** seeking monetary compensation).
  - *Negligence (All 4 Elements Required):* (1) **Duty to act** + (2) **Breach of duty** (*simple negligence* = mistake/omission; *gross negligence* = willful/wanton/reckless) + (3) **Damages** (real, legally recognizable injury) + (4) **Proximate cause** (injury directly caused by EMT's breach). *Res ipsa loquitur* = "the thing speaks for itself"; *negligence per se* = violating a statute, protocol, or scope of practice.
- **Five EMS Intentional Torts (Knowingly Committed Civil Wrongs):**
  1. *Abandonment:* Stopping care without transferring to an equal/higher trained clinician (leaving patient in ED with only a clerk/secretary without oral report to qualified clinician; downgrading ALS patient to BLS inappropriately).
  2. *Assault:* Willful threat to inflict harm creating apprehension of bodily harm without physical contact (e.g., threatening with an epinephrine auto-injector).
  3. *Battery:* Unlawfully touching a patient or providing care without consent (even with good intentions).
  4. *False Imprisonment (Kidnapping):* Transporting or detaining a patient with capacity against their wishes (physical restraint not required).
  5. *Defamation:* Damaging reputation publicly (*Slander* = spoken; *Libel* = written/mass media).
- **HIPAA (1996), COBRA & EMTALA:**
  - *HIPAA:* Keep all patient info strictly confidential; disclose without written release only for (1) continuing health care providers, (2) mandatory public health/government reporting (rape, child/elder/spousal abuse or neglect, communicable diseases, gunshots, stabbings, animal bites, suspicious burns), (3) police criminal investigation, (4) third-party billing, or (5) court subpoena. Consult agency **Privacy Officer** for breaches.
  - *COBRA & EMTALA ("Anti-Patient-Dumping Statute"):* Guarantee emergency care regardless of ability to pay. Transferring facilities must stabilize patients and ensure EMS crew has scope/capability (e.g., EMTs cannot manage continuous ECG or IV drug infusions). Hospital-based ambulances cannot bypass closest appropriate hospital due to lack of insurance.
  - *5 Interfacility Transfer Safeguards:* (1) Full condition report → (2) confirm care is within scope/capability → (3) signed informed transfer consent → (4) written certification of transfer (transferring physician name/address, receiving facility, reason) → (5) know and take quickest route.
- **Organ Donors, Death Recognition, Crime Scenes & Safe-Haven Laws:**
  - *Organ Donors & Medical IDs:* Treat **patient first, organ donor last**; requires legal signed document (donor card/license); identify donors, notify medical direction/ED, and maintain perfusion (CPR) if harvesting attempted. Check medical ID jewelry/bands and non-standardized medical alert tattoos.
  - *Recognizing Death & 2022 NASEMSO Protocol:* Begin resuscitation if patient is warm without obvious signs of death, except in **accidental hypothermia** (not dead until rewarmed). **5 Presumptive Signs:** Absent pulse/breathing/breath sounds; complete unresponsiveness; no eye movement/pupil response; absent BP; no reflexes. **4 Obvious Signs:** Decapitation; rigor mortis (**2–12 hours**); decomposition; dependent lividity. **NASEMSO Exclusions (excludes lightning, drowning, hypothermia):** 8 biological conditions [(1) decapitation; (2) decomposition/putrefaction (`>=24 hours` prior); (3) torso transection; (4) incineration (**90% BSA** full-thickness burns/ash/charred skin); (5) injuries incompatible with life; (6) futile/inhumane per protocol; (7) blunt/penetrating trauma apneic, pulseless, no signs of life on arrival; (8) nontraumatic arrest with dependent lividity or rigor mortis] **OR** valid, intact, state-compliant DNR or POLST/MOLST. When unclear, start CPR immediately and contact medical direction.
  - *Crime Scenes & Evidence Preservation:* Dispatch police and stage until safe; single path in/out; touch only what is necessary and report moved items; no crime-scene phone use without police permission; move patient without permission only for danger/care; avoid cutting through bullet/stab holes; cut away from knots in ropes/ties; do not cover body with sheet; in rape cases, advise against washing, changing clothes, using bathroom, or eating/drinking.
  - *Special Reporting & Baby Safe-Haven Laws:* Report suspected abuse/neglect (defamation immunity for good-faith reports), crime/drug injuries (addiction is an illness per U.S. Supreme Court, not a crime), exposures, restraints, incompetence/intoxication, suicide attempts, and dog bites. **Baby Safe-Haven Laws** (started in Texas **1999**, active nationwide) allow safe relinquishment of unharmed infants at designated locations within state age limits (**3 days** up to **1 year** in North Dakota).

# Chapter 4: Documentation

## 4.1: PCR Functions, Formats, Minimum Data Set & NEMSIS
- **Goals, Functions & Golden Rules:**
  - *Three Major Goals:* (1) **Continuity of care**, (2) **Legal protection** (reviewed first by plaintiff attorneys), and (3) **Reimbursement**.
  - *Five Core Functions:* (1) Continuity of medical care, (2) administrative/billing/operational stats, (3) legal record/testimony defense, (4) education and research, and (5) evaluation and CQI.
  - *Two Golden Rules:* *"If it wasn’t written down, it wasn’t done"* and *"If it wasn’t done, don’t write it down."*
  - *Formats:* Written PCRs vs. computerized ePCRs / pen-based electronic clipboards (superior legibility, storage, EMR/CAD/monitor integration, real-time hospital pre-arrival uploads, spell/omission prompts, QA/QI tools).
- **U.S. DOT Minimum Data Set & Five Core PCR Sections:**
  - *8 Patient Elements:* (1) Chief complaint; (2) level of responsiveness (AVPU); (3) BP for patients **>3 years** old; (4) skin perfusion (capillary refill) for patients **<6 years** old; (5) skin color, temperature, and condition; (6) pulse rate; (7) respiratory rate and effort; (8) demographics (age, sex, race, weight).
  - *6 Administrative Elements + Synchronous Timekeeping:* (1) Time incident reported, (2) time unit notified, (3) time arrived at patient, (4) time unit left scene, (5) time unit arrived at destination, (6) time of transfer of care—all clocks must agree across dispatch, crews, and devices.
  - *Five Core PCR Sections:* (1) Administrative info (times, unit/run #, crew names/levels, address); (2) patient demographics & prior care; (3) vital signs (**at least two complete sets** with patient position and timestamps); (4) patient narrative (chief complaint in quotes, history/MOI, objective signs vs. pertinent subjective symptoms, **pertinent negatives**, exam in order performed, scene clues, source attribution for sensitive info, reassessment trends, no radio codes/nonstandard abbreviations); (5) chronological treatments, times, clinical responses, and refused care.
- **2022 NASEMSO Universal Documentation Guideline & NEMSIS v3.4:**
  - *Incident & Condition Datasets:* Mandatory NEMSIS timestamps (**unit notified by dispatch** and **unit back in service**); SAMPLE history mapping; **Stroke** (last known well, stroke scale, neuro exam); **Trauma** (ICD-10 mechanism/intent, 2022 ACS-COT triage criteria, safety gear, trauma alert time); **Cardiac Arrest** (Utstein/CARES fields).
  - *Impressions, Vitals Tiers & Responses:* **Primary impression [single choice]** = most acute problem (not necessarily first), supported by signs/symptoms (e.g., code naloxone-reversed overdose as opiate/heroin overdose). **Tiered Vitals:** **Routine** (time, BP, HR, SpO2, RR, AVPU, pain) → **Initial** (+ blood glucose, GCS, temp) → **Full** (+ pulse rhythm, respiratory effort, EtCO2, EKG rhythm). Log patient self-administered aspirin (**324 mg**) per 911 as prior-to-arrival with pertinent negative *"medication already taken."* Medication responses = *Improved, Unchanged, Worse*; Procedure responses = *Not applicable, Improved, Unchanged, Worse* (each attempt logged separately with attempts = **"1"**).
  - *NEMSIS v3.4 (sole standard since Aug 2021):* **Mandatory** fields require real data (no nil/NV/PN); **Required** fields allow nil, Not Values (*"not applicable"*, *"not recorded"*), or Pertinent Negatives (up to 11); parent datasets **`d`** (Demographic) and **`e`** (EMS).

### Legal Documentation, Error Correction & Medical Necessity
- **8-Step Refusal Protocol:** (1) Assess within scene safety → (2) re-persuade & evaluate capacity (nuanced review of alcohol/drugs/illness/suicide) → (3) explain risks/consequences & confirm understanding → (4) consult medical direction & enlist family → (5) document findings, explanations (including death), refused items, and A&O capacity → (6) obtain signed refusal form + witness signature/address (waivers never replace thorough objective documentation) → (7) if patient refuses to sign, get witness signature verifying refusal → (8) suggest alternative evaluation (taxi/family), state EMS will return anytime, and give worsening warning signs.
- **Zero Tolerance for Falsification & Proper Error Correction:**
  - Never fabricate vitals, hide omissions, or claim oxygen given when `SpO2 < 94%` was missed; inflating care for **Medicare**/insurance violates the **False Claims Act**.
  - *Paper before submission:* Single horizontal line through error + initials + correct info (never erase/write over).
  - *Paper after submission:* Single horizontal line (preferably different ink color) + correction note + initials + **date and time** + notify original recipients.
  - *ePCR:* Use timestamped amendment feature (or print, correct per paper rules, and resubmit).
- **Drop Reports, MCIs & Special Reports:** Use abbreviated **transfer-of-care ("drop") reports** when immediate return to service prevents leaving a full PCR; use **triage tags** during MCIs; file separate objective **special reports** for child/elder abuse, suspicious burns, GSWs, infectious exposures, crew injury, or state triggers (patient harm from EMS error, EMS crash with injury/death, crew hospitalization, equipment failure, alleged crew substance use).
- **Medical Necessity & Reimbursement:**
  - *Commercial Insurance (Prudent Layperson Standard):* Would a person of average health/medical knowledge anticipate serious health impairment without emergency care (e.g., 2-week toothache = no vs. multisystem MVC = yes)?
  - *Medicare (42 CFR § 410.40(d)):* Covers ambulance transport **only** when other transport modes (car, wheelchair van, taxi, bus) are contraindicated, and condition necessitates **both** ambulance transport and level of service provided.
  - *Medicaid:* State-administered; criteria vary by state (e.g., continuous oxygen requirement).
  - *ET3 Payment Model (Emergency Treat, Triage, and Transport):* Announced by **CMS/HHS (2019)**, launched **January 5, 2020** (5-year voluntary evaluation); reimburses scene triage + transport to alternate (non-ED) destinations and non-transport care (on-scene clinician or telehealth) when supported by truthful documentation across 5 medical-necessity elements.

# Chapter 5: Communication

## 5.1: Hardware, FCC Regulation & System Radio Protocols
- **Evolution & System Components:**
  - **EMS Act of 1973 (Public Law 93-154)** made communications 1 of **15 essential EMS components** and funded VHF/UHF radios; **FirstNet (2012, Public Law 112-96)** established a dedicated nationwide public safety broadband network.
  - *Base Station:* Central dispatch/coordination hub on high terrain near command hospital; **80–150 watts**; antenna efficiency offsets FCC power limits and man-made noise.
  - *Land Mobile Radio Systems (LMRS):* UHF, VHF, or **700- to 800-MHz** networks.
  - *Mobile Radios:* Vehicle-mounted; **20–50 watts**; **10- to 15-mile** range on average terrain.
  - *Portable Radios:* Handheld; **1–5 watts**; limited range boosted via repeaters.
  - *Repeaters:* Vehicle or fixed stations receiving low-power signals and rebroadcasting at another frequency and higher power.
  - *Digital Equipment:* **Encoder** converts sound to digital code; **decoder** recognizes matching codes so multiple units share frequencies; **Mobile Data Terminal (MDT)** displays/prints dispatch info and status buttons.
  - *Cellular Phones, Telemetry, Telemedicine & Satellite:* Cellular cells (**2–40 sq mi**) offer high audio quality/privacy/data transfer but can overload in MCIs; **telemetry** automates wireless vitals/12-lead ECG transmission; **telemedicine** supports MIH/ET3 consults; **satellite** serves remote rural areas.
- **FCC Regulation & Maintenance:** FCC licenses base stations, assigns call signs/frequencies, approves gear, sets power limits, prevents interference, and prohibits profanity/obscenity. Clean gear with damp cloth/mild detergent, check frequency drift, test batteries daily + keep charged spares, and maintain generator/cellular backups.
- **11-Step Transmission Progression:** Dispatch sends call → unit acknowledges → unit en route → notify other agencies if needed → on scene → contact medical direction → leaving scene → radio report to receiving hospital → arrival at hospital → leaving hospital → back at station.
- **16 Radio Ground Rules:** Close windows; listen first; push PTT and wait **1 second**; hold mic **2–3 inches** from lips; speak calmly/slowly in monotone; address target unit first, then ID yourself; wait for *"go ahead"* (or pause on *"stand by"*); pause every **30 seconds** on long transmissions; use plain English (no slang, *"Be advised"*, excessive *"Please/Thank you"*, or unapproved codes); state confusing numbers as word + digits (*"thirteen, one-three"*); state objective/pertinent subjective facts only (no diagnoses); never broadcast patient names; never use profanity; use **closed-loop ("echo") communication** repeating orders word for word; write down addresses/orders; use *"we"* instead of *"I"*; use *"affirmative/negative"* instead of *"yes/no"*; end with *"Over"*.
- **Dispatch, AACN & Military Time:** **AACN** transmits crash location, velocity change, force direction, airbag deployment, rollover, and multiple impacts. Communicate at **9 key dispatch points** (acknowledge, en route, ETA/delays, on scene, departing scene + destination/patient count/ETA, hospital arrival, clear/available, leaving hospital, back at station). Use 24-hour **military time** (`0100–1200` A.M., `1300–2400` P.M.).

| Radio Term | Meaning | Radio Term | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Break** | Pause so hospital can respond/interrupt | **Landline** | Telephone communications |
| **Clear** | End of transmission | **Over** | End of message; awaiting reply |
| **Come in** | Requesting acknowledgment | **Repeat / say again** | Did not understand message |
| **Copy / 10-4** | Message received and understood | **Spell out** | Phonetic spelling of unclear words |
| **Go ahead** | Proceed with your message | **Stand by** | Please wait |

- **Reports to Medical Direction, Receiving Facility & Handoffs:**
  - *Radio Report Elements:* Unit ID/level, age/sex, chief complaint, special alerts (trauma, stroke, STEMI), pertinent HPI/scene/MOI, major PMH, mental status, baseline vitals, pertinent exam findings, emergency care given, patient response, current condition, requests/orders, ETA. Repeat orders word for word (including *"do not"* orders) and question orders that seem inappropriate.
  - *SBAR Format:* **S**ituation (reason/chief complaint) • **B**ackground (PMH/response so far) • **A**ssessment (mental status, vitals, neuro, BGL, GCS) • **R**ecommendation (specific order/action requested).
  - *In-Person Oral Handoff Report:* Chief complaint, en-route vitals, en-route treatment + response, pertinent history omitted earlier, plus written PCR copy.
  - *Taking Charge on Scene:* Project confidence; take a `<=1 minute` update from EMRs if patient is alert without life threats, or go straight to critical patients. Ask police/rescue *"We’re Emergency Medical Technicians. Is there anything we need to know before we provide emergency care?"*; ask physicians *"We’re certified EMTs, Doctor. How can we help?"*.

## 5.2: Therapeutic Communication, Nonverbal Cues & Special Populations
- **Communication Loop & Nine Therapeutic Responses:** **Sender** encodes message → **Receiver** decodes message (filtered by values/experience) → **Feedback** is encoded and returned continuously. **9 Responses:** (1) **Facilitation** (*"go on"*), (2) **Clarification** (ask to repeat/clarify), (3) **Summary** (rephrase & verify), (4) **Explanation** (plain-language preview before procedures), (5) **Silence** (time to think; use carefully in depression), (6) **Reflection** (mirror feelings/statements), (7) **Empathy** ("in patient's shoes"), (8) **Confrontation** (assertive, non-aggressive reality check), (9) **Facilitated Communication** (physical support for letterboard/keyboard without influencing message).
- **Patient Contact, Nonverbal Cues & Interviewing:**
  - Convey **Competence, Confidence, and Compassion**; use formal titles (*Mr./Mrs.*) with older adults; ask explicit permission before helping; expect initial denial from **defense mechanisms** and use calm distraction; protect privacy; control lighting and noise.
  - *Nonverbal Communication (Dominates When Verbal Cues Conflict):* Open arms/hands/relaxed shoulders; match eye level (stand above only for hostile safety; crouch below for children/elderly); respect distance zones (**Intimate `<1½ ft`**, **Personal `1½–4 ft`**, **Social `4–12 ft`**, **Public `>=12 ft`**); read facial **gestures**; adjust eye contact for cultural norms; ask permission before comforting touch (**haptics**) if reluctant.
  - *Interviewing:* Use **open-ended questions** (*"How are you feeling?"*) for rich detail and **closed-ended questions** (*"When did pain start?"*) for specific facts or overly talkative patients. Avoid **leading questions** (*"It really hurts, doesn't it?"*), interrupting, false assurances/lying, diagnosing, or blame-implying *"why"* questions. Stage for police on hostile scenes and set clear behavioral boundaries.
- **Transcultural & Special Care:** Overcome stereotyping and **ethnocentrism** (viewing your culture as superior); respect folk medicine, space, and eye-contact norms; use interpreter lines and document who interpreted. Speak slowly/distinctly without talking down to elderly patients (assist with glasses/hearing aids); keep parents calm, crouch to eye level, and always tell children the truth; practice **Family-Centered Care** (originated **1987**, Surgeon General Koop / EMSC).

# Chapter 6: Lifting and Moving Patients

## 6.1: Body Mechanics, Posture, Lifting & Move Categories
- **Four Principles of Body Mechanics, Posture & Teamwork:**
  - *Golden Priorities:* Choose easiest safe method; let equipment do the work; get maximum help; never risk falling or injuring yourself.
  - *Four Basic Principles:* (1) **Keep weight close to the body**; (2) **engage large lower-body and core muscles** (legs, hips, glutes, abs—never back); (3) **“stack” and align as a unit** (shoulders over hips over feet; never lift and twist simultaneously); (4) **reduce lift height or travel distance** (move closer or lift in stages).
  - *Posture & Teamwork:* Avoid **excessive lordosis** (swayback) and **excessive kyphosis** (slouch); align ears, shoulders, and hips vertically; combine flexibility, cardio, strength training, and nutrition; match partners of comparable strength/height, coordinate lifts verbally, and explain plans to alert patients beforehand.
- **Lifting, Reaching, Pushing & Pulling Techniques:**
  - *Power Lift & Power Grip:* **Power grip** = palms and fingers in full contact with all fingers bent at the same angle. **Power lift (9 steps)** = feet shoulder-width apart and slightly outward, bend knees (sit down), tighten abs/back with neutral head, straddle object with weight forward of heels, hands **>=10 inches** apart with power grip, drive through heels and arches bringing upper body up before hips.
  - *Squat Lift & One-Handed Carry:* **Squat lift** (one weak leg/ankle) = weaker leg slightly forward with foot flat, squat with power grip, push up with stronger leg. **One-handed carry** = staggered feet (one knee up, one down), bend at hips with trunk tilt **<=45 degrees**, drive up simultaneously through front heel/arch and back ball.
  - *Time, Distance & Force Limits:* Limit two-rescuer heavy carries to **<=1 minute**; sustained effort caps at **100% for 6 seconds** and **50% for 1 minute**; limit forward reach (e.g., log roll) to **15–20 inches** (move closer if `>20 inches`); push rather than pull whenever possible (between waist and shoulder height; kneel for low loads; never push/pull overhead).
- **Emergency, Urgent & Nonurgent Moves:**
  - *Emergency Moves (Immediate Danger to Patient or Rescuer):* Fire/danger of fire, explosives/hazmat, uncontrolled traffic, unstable structures, extreme weather, hostile crowds, or inability to access patient for lifesaving care (CPR, defibrillation, hemorrhage control). Pull along the **long axis** with head/neck neutral and nose aligned with umbilicus: **Armpit-Forearm Drag**, **Shirt Drag** (never T-shirt alone; engage armpits without choking neck), and **Blanket Drag** (pleat half lengthwise, roll/tuck, wrap, drag beneath head).
  - *Urgent Moves (Immediate Threat to Life Requiring Rapid Care/Transport):* **Rapid Extrication (MVC)** (indicated for airway/breathing/circulation abnormalities, AMS, shock, head/neck/chest/abdomen/pelvis trauma, bilateral femur fractures, or major bleeding → manual in-line stabilization + C-collar + rotate in short coordinated moves onto long backboard → move off backboard onto stretcher/vacuum mattress per protocol) and **Self-Extrication** (to reach a trapped critical patient behind a non-critical patient → apply C-collar, coach neutral head/neck alignment, turn as a unit, and exit).
  - *Nonurgent Moves (No Immediate Threat to Life):* **Walking assist** (never walk patients who are light-headed/sweaty, or have chest pain, dyspnea, lower-extremity injury, or suspected spinal injury), **Direct Ground Lift** (2–3 rescuers on same side on one knee; not for heavy patients), **Extremity Lift** (contraindicated in spinal/extremity injuries), **Direct Carry** (stretcher perpendicular at foot of bed), and **Draw Sheet Method** (stretcher alongside bed, slide across).

## 6.2: Packaging, Carrying Devices, Positioning & Air Transport

| Carrying Device | Weight Rating / Spec | Key Uses, Advantages & Contraindications |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Wheeled Stretcher** | Up to **650 lbs** | Primary transfer device; manual roll-in (**~70–95 lbs**) vs. power (**up to 140 lbs**, including "no-lift" horizontal loaders); load hanging stretchers first; never leave patient unattended |
| **Bariatric Stretcher** | **850–1,600 lbs** | Wide deck, heavy-duty frame, stability wheels, optional ramp and winch |

- **Carrying & Stair Rules:** Minimum **3 straps**; secure patient's hands inside device; **feet first** going **down** stairs/hills; **head first** going **up** stairs/hills. In a **two-person carry**, place the **stronger rescuer at the head** (most body weight is there); foot EMT walks backward (use **spotter** behind foot EMT downstairs, ahead of head EMT upstairs). **Four-person carry** = **Diamond** (1 head, 1 foot facing forward, 2 sides) or **Rectangular** (2 at head sides, 2 at foot sides).
- **Clinical Positioning, Special Populations & Neonatal Isolette:**
  - *Unresponsive non-trauma:* **Left lateral recumbent** facing rescuer.
  - *Chest pain / dyspnea:* Sitting position of comfort (if normotensive).
  - *Shock:* Supine unless protocol directs otherwise.
  - *Pregnancy >20 weeks:* **Left side** (preferred) or supine with right hip elevated to prevent **supine hypotensive syndrome**; if **prolapsed umbilical cord**, supine with hips elevated on pillow.
  - *Special populations & Neonatal Isolette:* Undamaged car seat padded with rolled towels for infants/toddlers; gentle handling for osteoporosis in elderly; pad fused/twisted limbs in disabilities; **Neonatal Isolette** maintains warmth to prevent hypothermia and locks to ambulance stretcher mount.
- **13 Rules for Packaging Patients for Air Transport:**
  1. Ensure hazmat-contaminated patients are thoroughly decontaminated first (fumes incapacitate cabin crew and can force permanent aircraft removal).
  2. Manage airway with endotracheal tube before aircraft arrival if indicated.
  3. Keep chest accessible on intubated patients so flight crew can auscultate breath sounds.
  4. Confirm backboard fits responding helicopter model.
  5. Secure patient well to backboard against jostling.
  6. Secure loose equipment, blankets, and sheets with tape/straps so nothing blows into rotors/engines.
  7. Inform patient and prepare them for incoming helicopter noise and rotor wash.
  8. Cover patient's eyes, ears, and exposed wounds against noise, dust, and debris.
  9. Consider having engine company wet landing zone to reduce blowing dust/debris.
  10. Have rescuers remove loose clothing or hats that could be drawn into rotors/engines.
  11. Wait for pilot/crew instruction before approaching; let crew assist loading unless instructed otherwise, stay clear of tail rotor, observe placards, and follow airframe-specific approach rules.
  12. Lay IV bag on patient's chest during movement to helicopter rather than holding it up.
  13. Minimize personnel under rotors at all times during loading.

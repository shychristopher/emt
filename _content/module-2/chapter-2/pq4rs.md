### 1. Emotional Aspects of Emergency Care
- **Emotional Crises in Emergency Care:**
  - Patients and family members experience *distress*—acute physical or mental suffering caused by pain, anxiety, strain, or sorrow.
  - Emotional pressures exceeding coping capacity trigger an *emotional crisis*.
  - Emergency care helps patients and families move toward reestablishing emotional equilibrium.
- **Death and Dying:**
  - *EMT Responsibilities:* Care for both the dying patient's emotional needs and injuries or illnesses; support family members and bystanders, especially after sudden death; recognize that grief stages also apply to nonfatal emergencies involving major loss (e.g., losing limbs in an industrial accident).
  - *Elizabeth Kübler-Ross's Five Emotional Stages* (individuals progress at their own rates and in their own ways; not all five stages are seen during emergency care):
    1. **Denial ("Not me."):** Defense mechanism buffering shock against approaching death or illness/injury realities.
    2. **Anger ("Why me?"):** May target the EMT; avoid defensiveness and respond empathetically with active listening and communication.
    3. **Bargaining ("Okay, but first let me . . ."):** Attempts to make agreements perceived to postpone death briefly.
    4. **Depression ("Okay, but I haven't . . ."):** Silence, distance, sadness, and despair over unfinished matters or those left behind.
    5. **Acceptance ("Okay, I am not afraid."):** Acknowledgment of impending death (not happiness); families often need greater support at this stage.
  - *Nine Principles for Interacting with Dying Patients, Families, and Bystanders:*
    1. **Prioritize care & individual needs:** Prioritize emergency care while addressing needs for dignity, respect, sharing, communication, privacy, and control across different grief rates.
    2. **Maintain dignity:** Avoid negative remarks; speak to unresponsive patients as if alert and explain care.
    3. **Show respect & sincerity:** Watch both words and body language; allow family presence during resuscitation when appropriate, explain efforts, and show you never simply "gave up."
    4. **Communicate clearly:** Orient the patient to surroundings, explain events, roles, and transport plans, give straightforward answers, report known facts without guessing, and update family without interrupting care.
    5. **Allow emotional expression:** Permit crying, screaming, or venting if safe; do not take directed anger personally.
    6. **Listen empathetically:** Honor final requests/messages when possible, stay with family until the medical examiner or coroner arrives, and arrange ambulance transport for a family member if feasible.
    7. **Avoid false assurances:** Be honest and tactful without confirming death or destroying hope (e.g., state that everything possible is being done and encourage not giving up).
    8. **Use a gentle tone:** Explain injury scope and procedures kindly in understandable terms.
    9. **Handle postmortem contact & crime scenes:** Clean blood/secretions/vomitus and elevate the head briefly if allowed by local protocol; warn of severe trauma and cover injured areas; **do not** clean the body, remove blood, or allow family access/touching in a possible crime scene until cleared by law enforcement, coroner, or medical examiner, preserving evidence and documenting thoroughly.
- **High-Stress Situations in EMS:**
  - *Definition:* Stress is any change in the body's internal balance when external demands exceed personal resources.
  - *Everyday stressors:* Long hours, shift work, boredom between calls, overwork, low recognition, instant response demands, life-and-death decisions, fear of serious errors, grief/dying exposure, and responsibility for another life.
  - *High-stress calls:* Multiple-casualty incidents (MCIs); abuse and neglect of infants, children, adults, or elderly individuals; emergencies involving infants and children; injury or death of a coworker; caring for a relative or bystander; severe traumatic injuries (such as amputations).
- **Stress Reactions & Warning Signs:**
  - *Three Types of Stress Reactions:*
    1. **Acute stress reaction:** Immediate or shortly after a high-stress incident, affecting cognitive, physical, behavioral, or psychological function. Minor responses (nausea, elevated heart rate, sweating, tremors, appetite shifts, trouble concentrating, insomnia) are common; absence of symptoms reflects different coping rather than superior strength. Seek immediate medical evaluation for possible medical emergencies (e.g., chest pain, shortness of breath).
    2. **Delayed stress reaction:** Signs/symptoms appear days, months, or years later (classic example: **PTSD**). Delayed onset can obscure the link to the triggering event; includes nightmares, irritability, insomnia, impaired concentration, flashbacks, interpersonal conflict, reduced ability to relate to others, and risk of substance misuse. Requires mental health professional care.
    3. **Cumulative stress reaction:** Results from ongoing exposure to stress building over time; a major driver of **burnout** (state of exhaustion and irritability) that can progress to emotional exhaustion, severe physical illness, reduced care effectiveness, or leaving EMS.
  - *10 Core Warning Signs:* Irritability with coworkers, family, and friends; inability to concentrate; difficulty sleeping and nightmares; anxiety; indecisiveness; guilt; loss of appetite; loss of sexual desire or interest; isolation; loss of interest in work.
  - *5 Categories of Stress Signs & Symptoms:*
    - **Thinking:** Confusion, impaired judgment/decision-making, loss of motivation, chronic forgetfulness, loss of objectivity.
    - **Psychological:** Depression, excessive anger, negativism, hostility, defensiveness, mood swings, feelings of worthlessness.
    - **Physical:** Persistent exhaustion, headaches, GI distress, dizziness, pounding heart.
    - **Behavioral:** Overeating, increased alcohol or drug use, teeth grinding, hyperactivity, lack of energy.
    - **Social:** Increased interpersonal conflicts, reduced ability to view patients as individuals.
- **Stress Management:**
  - *Lifestyle changes:*
    - **Diet:** Reduce sugar, caffeine, and alcohol; increase lean protein, limit carbohydrates, reduce saturated fat, and eat small, frequent meals on shift.
    - **Exercise:** Releases pent-up crisis emotions, helps prevent injury, and triggers endorphin release.
    - **Relaxation & diversions:** Deep breathing (inhale, hold, forceful exhale), meditation, visual imagery, and healthy diversions (funny movies, books, concerts).
    - **Avoid self-medication:** Alcohol or pills worsen underlying stress once effects wear off.
  - *Life balance:* Prioritize activities numerically and address them in order; confide in trusted friends/loved ones so they understand your experience and feel included; accept human fallibility—mistakes happen and do not diminish your worth; clinical skill grows with experience, time, and practice.
  - *Family & friends' reactions:*
    - **Lack of understanding:** Misinformation from media/entertainment.
    - **Fear of separation or being ignored:** Frustration over long hours, physical strain, or perceived job priority.
    - **Worry about on-call situations:** Exaggerated perception of call dangers.
    - **Inability to plan:** Frustration over call restrictions or leaving events early.
    - **Frustrated desire to share:** Difficulty discussing shift events leaves loved ones feeling locked out.
    - **Support strategies:** Share feelings and safety practices openly, invite loved ones into fitness/healthy meals, and protect dedicated time with them.
  - *Work-environment changes:* Use a coworker "buddy" system to monitor stress and encourage breaks or diversions; foster positive coworker support and avoid destructive criticism; take brief aerobic exercise breaks (brisk walk, stationary bike, treadmill, elliptical); request shifts allowing adequate rest and family/friend time; request duty rotations (such as moving from high-violence/crime areas to higher-medical call areas).
  - *Professional help:* Seeking mental health care is a sign of strength and protects patient care; untreated severe stress, anxiety, or depression impairs clear thinking and field performance.
- **Critical Incident Stress Management (CISM):**
  - *Purpose & overview:* Addresses **critical incident** stress (which can mirror burnout and cause intrusive mental images, fear of EMS work, or impaired performance at later calls); combines structured peer and mental health support across two core debriefing/defusing processes within a broader system.
  - *Critical Incident Stress Debriefing (CISD):*
    - Ideally held **24 to 72 hours** after a critical incident.
    - Led by peer counselors and mental health professionals through **seven phases:** (1) reviewing facts, (2) sharing feelings, (3) identifying signs/symptoms, (4) sorting feelings with professional guidance, (5) learning stress-coping strategies, (6) planning return to work, and (7) arranging follow-up care.
    - Non-punitive (not for assigning blame) and confidential; may include EMS, law enforcement, fire, dispatch, ED staff, and affected family members (or multiple sessions for large MCIs/disasters).
    - Relieves stress rather than serving as standalone professional counseling; ongoing scientific debate regarding effectiveness.
  - *Defusing:*
    - Held **up to 8 hours** post-incident for those most directly involved.
    - Lasts **30 to 45 minutes** and is less structured than CISD, offering immediate venting and initial information before a larger CISD session.
  - *10 Components of a Comprehensive CISM Program:* Preincident stress education; on-scene peer support; one-on-one support; disaster support services; defusing; CISD; follow-up services; spouse and family support; community outreach programs; other health and welfare programs.

### 2. Scene Safety
- **Core Scene Safety Principles:**
  - Keep yourself safe at illness or injury scenes through disease protection, rescue procedures, and violence/crime scene awareness.
  - Advocate for protective measures to keep both fellow emergency personnel and yourself safe and well.
  - **Never** act in any capacity other than a medical responder unless specifically trained and authorized to do so.
- **Protecting Yourself from Disease:**
  - *Five Pathogen Categories:*
    - **Bacteria:** Microscopic, single-celled organisms capable of reproducing independently or in a host (e.g., sinus/ear infections, bacterial pneumonia, strep throat, tuberculosis, UTIs); typically respond to antibiotics.
    - **Viruses:** Smaller than bacteria; require a host cell to reproduce and are **not** treatable with antibiotics (e.g., common cold, flu, HIV/AIDS, hepatitis A/B/C, viral pneumonia, SARS, chicken pox, RSV; emerging infectious diseases [EID] include COVID-19, Ebola virus disease [EVD], and Zika virus disease [ZVD]).
    - **Fungi:** Plantlike microorganisms generally causing disease only when immune function is compromised (e.g., pneumonia in HIV-infected patients).
    - **Protozoa:** Single-celled, motile organisms typically found in soil; often affect immunocompromised hosts (fecal-oral entry, mosquito vector for malaria), though some affect immunocompetent hosts (e.g., certain gastroenteritis or vaginal infections).
    - **Helminths:** Parasitic worms (roundworms, flukes, tapeworms) acquired by ingesting worm eggs in contaminated food/water or skin penetration by larvae (e.g., hookworm attaching to intestine, causing blood loss and anemia).
  - *Transmission Modes:*
    - **Infectious vs. communicable:** Infectious diseases result from pathogen transmission; communicable diseases spread person-to-person (e.g., malaria is vector-borne via mosquitoes and not spread person-to-person).
    - **Direct contact:** Blood-to-blood contact, open wounds/exposed tissue, mucous membranes of eyes and mouth.
    - **Indirect contact:** Contaminated objects such as needles.
    - **Airborne droplets:** Coughing, sneezing, or exhaling infected droplets inhaled into the respiratory tract.
  - *All 13 Notable Pathogens (Transmission | Incubation | Protection):*

| Pathogen | Mode of Transmission | Incubation Period | Required Protection |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |

| **Hepatitis B and C** | Blood, semen, vaginal fluid, needlestick, transplacental, human bite, sexual contact | Weeks or months | Gloves, eyewear, hand washing |
| **Tuberculosis** | Respiratory secretions, airborne or direct contact | 2–6 weeks | Gloves, eyewear, HEPA or N-95 respirator, hand washing |

  - *Standard Precautions & Exposure Reporting:*
    - Formerly called body substance isolation (BSI); required under OSHA guidelines against blood and other potentially infectious substances/body fluids.
    - **Shared employer & employee duties:** Employers must establish a written exposure control plan, provide training, required immunizations, and protective equipment, and maintain a written post-exposure policy (including documentation, baseline/interval testing where required, and post-exposure notification procedures); employees must complete training and follow plan procedures.
    - **Airborne infection (e.g., TB) workflow:** Facility diagnoses disease in transported patient → notifies designated officer (DO) within **24 hours** → DO notifies EMT → employer arranges medical evaluation and follow-up.
    - **Bloodborne infection (e.g., HIV, HBV) workflow:** EMT contacts blood/body fluids → seeks immediate medical care and documents for workers' compensation → requests DO determination → DO gathers info/consults receiving facility → facility reports findings to DO within **48 hours** → DO notifies EMT.
    - **General exposure reporting:** Promptly report suspected exposures to supervisor (date, time, fluid type, volume, incident details) per local protocol and agency policy.
  - *Hand Washing:*
    - Single most important infection-prevention measure; reduces spread to EMT and between patients (including MRSA and *Clostridium difficile* colitis), even when gloves are worn.
    - U.S. Public Health Service notes 10–15 seconds of vigorous lathering/scrubbing with plain soap removes most contaminants.
    - **6-Step CDC Hand Hygiene Sequence:** (1) Remove hand jewelry → (2) wet hands with clean running water (warm or cold), turn off tap, apply soap → (3) lather backs of hands, between fingers, and under nails → (4) scrub at least **20 seconds** (e.g., hum "Happy Birthday" twice) → (5) rinse well under clean running water → (6) dry with clean towel or air dry.
    - **Alcohol-based hand sanitizer:** Use when soap and running water are unavailable in the field (at least **60% alcohol** per CDC); does not eliminate all germs, so wash with soap and water as soon as possible; overuse can dry/chafe/crack skin and create a pathogen portal of entry.
  - *Personal Protective Equipment (PPE):*
    - Barrier protecting skin and mucous membranes from blood and body fluids; requires instruction/training before use, and all used PPE must be treated as contaminated.
    - **Eye protection:** Clear plastic eye or full-face shields, protective eyewear with side shields, or removable side shields on prescription glasses (form-fitting goggles optional/not required).
    - **Protective gloves:** High-quality vinyl, latex-free, or synthetic gloves for patient care; remove safely, wash hands, and replace if torn; consider double-gloving when appropriate; never reuse or wear between different patients; use proper doffing technique and wash hands after removal; wear utility gloves when cleaning vehicles/equipment.
    - **Gowns:** Wear during significant blood/fluid contact (e.g., childbirth); use disposable gowns when possible; change uniform after large splashes and wash contaminated uniforms separately from personal clothes.
    - **Masks:** Disposable surgical mask against splashes/airborne droplets (may also mask patients with suspected airborne illness); wear HEPA or N-95 respirator for suspected TB or COVID-19 (must be clean-shaven, and N-95 requires annual fit testing).
  - *Routine Care, Disposal, Decontamination & Immunizations:*
    - **Equipment & waste handling:** Prefer disposables; never reuse single-use items; bag, seal, and label infectious/soiled disposables as biohazardous waste (double-bag HIV, hepatitis B, or similar infectious waste).
    - **Contaminated uniforms:** Remove, bag, and label; wash in hot soapy water for at least **25 minutes**; take a hot, thorough shower (for gross contamination, stand in a labeled biohazard bag and strip top-to-bottom directly into the bag).
    - **Cleaning vs. Disinfecting vs. Sterilizing:**
      - *Cleaning:* Mechanical washing with soap and water using friction to remove soil/residue.
      - *Disinfecting:* Includes cleaning plus chemical germicide/hospital-grade disinfectant to kill many surface microorganisms (used for intact-skin items like backboards, splints, blood pressure cuffs; clean visible soil on nonhorizontal surfaces as needed; clean blood/body fluids promptly with hospital-grade disinfectant or household bleach solution; wash hands afterward).
      - *Sterilization:* Chemical or physical process (e.g., superheated steam in an autoclave) destroying **all** microorganisms on a surface; required for items entering the body or touching mucous membranes (e.g., laryngoscope blades) after thorough cleaning, following local protocols, manufacturer instructions, and chemical hazard precautions.
    - **Sharps safety & logging:** Immediately place needles/sharps into a rigid, mounted, puncture-proof sharps container; never recap needles; record blood/body fluid contact and resulting cleaning in logbook or flow sheet.
    - **Immunizations & screening:** Pre-duty physician clearance and initial-hire TB screening via purified protein derivative (PPD) tuberculin test (CDC includes individual TB risk assessment, symptom evaluation, and test; annual TB testing not recommended unless known exposure, ongoing transmission, or increased occupational risk). Recommended immunizations: COVID-19; tetanus (one-time Tdap if not previously received, then Td/Tdap booster every **10 years**; Tdap each pregnancy for pregnant HCWs); hepatitis B (2-dose Heplisav-B or 3-dose Recombivax HB / Engerix-B; offered free by OSHA-covered employers within **10 days** of exposure-risk assignment); annual influenza; MMR (for those born in/after 1957: 2 measles, 2 mumps, >=1 rubella); and varicella if needed; verify rubella, measles, and mumps immunity and obtain a hepatitis B titer, while continuing Standard Precautions even when immunized.
- **Protecting Yourself from Accidental and Work-Related Injury:**
  - *Everyday prevention:* Vehicle restraints, safe lifting/moving, adequate sleep, physical fitness, proper nutrition, and Standard Precautions.
  - *Hazardous materials:* Invisible vapors/gases can travel far; scout from a distance using binoculars before approaching and check placards/labels against the U.S. DOT *Emergency Response Guidebook* (carried on every EMS vehicle and available as a mobile app). Do not enter until trained hazmat teams control the scene, contamination is limited, and patients are decontaminated; hazmat personnel use SCBAs and specialized protective suits.
  - *Rescue situations:*
    - High-risk hazards: downed power lines (dangerous even without fire/sparks), fire/explosions/spills, hazmat, structural collapse, low-oxygen confined spaces (e.g., grain silos), unsecured trenches, WMD (biological, nuclear, chemical), and highway/busy roadway MVAs.
    - Request specialized teams (power company, fire, specialized rescue) and law enforcement for traffic control before entering life-threatening rescues; once safe, wear required PPE per protocol/minimum scene standards (turnout gear, puncture-proof gloves, helmet with reflective tape and chin strap, eye protection, weather/dust gear such as rubber/waterproof boots, cold layers, or dust respirator).
    - **High-visibility apparel:** Required on/near roadways per federal regulations: **ANSI/ISEA 107-2004 Class 2 or Class 3**, or **ANSI/ISEA 207-2006 Public Safety Vest [PSV]** with breakaway design, turnout fit, and service markings (Class 1 is for `<25 mph` parking lots/low-speed areas; Class 2 is for `>=25 mph` or diverted attention; Class 3 is for high traffic risk/high distraction).
  - *Violence and crime:*
    - Call law enforcement before entering suspected violent or crime scenes—especially when weapons or perpetrators may be present (domestic disputes, agitated/hostile drug or alcohol intoxication, street/gang/bar fights, potential suicide, angry family/bystanders, hostile behavioral emergency, any crime scene)—and wait until law enforcement controls the scene and removes weapons.
    - Watch for emerging hazards including clandestine drug laboratories and "chemical suicides" using hazardous materials.
    - During civil unrest or urban transports outside normal coverage areas, coordinate with law enforcement on blocked routes/violence and plan alternate routes in advance.
    - Understand the capabilities and limitations of body armor if issued or worn.
    - At crime scenes, disturb the scene only as needed to provide medical care to preserve the chain of evidence.

### 3. Wellness Principles & Practical Skills
- **Overview of Wellness:**
  - Foundational requirement for performing EMT responsibilities effectively; goes beyond simply being free of illness.
  - Encompasses six interconnected dimensions: **physical**, **intellectual**, **emotional**, **social**, **environmental**, and **spiritual**.
  - Imbalance in any dimension can lead to poor health; core clinical focus centers on **physical** and **mental** well-being.
- **Physical Well-Being:**
  - *Five Core Components of Physical Fitness:*
    1. **Cardiovascular Endurance:** Built through aerobic activity (walking, jogging, swimming, elliptical, step machine) that strengthens heart, lungs, and vessels to supply oxygen and nutrients during prolonged exertion. Requires reaching and maintaining a target heart rate for **30–40 minutes at least 3 days per week**.
       - *70% Target Heart Rate Formula:* (1) Measure resting heart rate (RHR) → (2) `HRmax = 220 - Age` → (3) `(HRmax - RHR) × 0.70` → (4) `Target HR = [(HRmax - RHR) × 0.70] + RHR`.
       - *Worked Example (28-year-old, RHR = 62 bpm):* `HRmax = 220 - 28 = 192 bpm` → `192 - 62 = 130 bpm` → `130 × 0.70 = 91 bpm` → `91 + 62 = 153 bpm`.
    2. **Muscle Strength:** Maximum force a muscle exerts in a single contraction (measured by maximum weight lifted); developed through weightlifting and resistance exercises; essential for repetitive lifting of heavy equipment and patients.
    3. **Muscle Endurance:** Ability of muscles to perform repeated contractions without undue fatigue; developed by lifting heavier-than-accustomed weights via weight training; crucial for sustained carries (e.g., from a bedroom to the ambulance).
    4. **Muscle Flexibility:** Ability to move joints through a full range of motion, primarily improved through stretching; supports safe lifting and moving mechanics and helps prevent muscle and joint injuries.
    5. **Body Composition:** Primarily evaluated by ratio of body fat to total weight; lower body fat ratios correlate with better health, whereas higher ratios increase risks for **heart disease**, **hypertension**, and **diabetes**; maintained or reduced through proper nutrition and exercise.
  - *Adequate Sleep:*
    - 24/7 EMS shift schedules—especially hours outside normal waking/alertness windows—disrupt natural **circadian rhythms**, impairing physical coordination, cognitive processing, and social functioning.
    - Sleep deprivation increases accident and injury risk for the EMT and compromises patient care quality; chronic insufficient sleep contributes to heart disease, weight gain, and impaired mental function.
    - Need **at least 8–10 hours of sleep daily**, yet research indicates roughly half of EMS providers get **under 6 hours per day**.
    - **Four Sleep Strategies:** Create a cool, dark environment that mimics nighttime; maintain a consistent sleep schedule allowing an uninterrupted block of sleep; avoid heavy meals, caffeine, or exercise immediately before sleeping; minimize interruptions (turn off phone ringers and pagers, set cell phones to silent, and place a sleeping sign on the door).
    - **Five Evidence-Based Fatigue-Mitigation Guidelines (NASEMSO):**
      1. Measure and monitor personal fatigue using reliable and/or valid fatigue/sleepiness surveys.
      2. Keep shifts shorter than **24 hours**.
      3. Provide EMS personnel access to caffeine as a fatigue countermeasure.
      4. Allow on-duty napping opportunities to mitigate fatigue.
      5. Provide fatigue and fatigue-related risk education and training.
  - *Smoking Cessation:* Undermines an EMT's role as a public health promoter and elevates personal risk for **cardiovascular disease**, **pulmonary disease**, and **cancer**.
  - *Alcohol- and Drug-Related Issues:* Using alcohol or prescription/illegal drugs to cope with occupational stress is harmful, ineffective at resolving stress, and impairs health and mental function; requires professional help and **Employee Assistance Programs (EAP)**.
- **Mental Well-Being:**
  - Unmanaged EMS stress contributes to chronic physical illness, emotional distress, relationship strain, and personal life imbalance.
  - Talk with close friends or family, practice exercise, relaxation, and enjoyable hobbies (such as fishing or hiking), and use professional counseling or **EAP** resources.
  - Over **30%** of first responders have received a formal mental health diagnosis (such as **depression** or **PTSD**).
  - *Resilience (per NAEMT):* The ability to cope with stress and adversity without lasting physical or psychological harm; shaped by nutrition, exercise, sleep, smoking/drinking habits, and social interaction, keeping an EMT flexible, engaged, satisfied, and relatively relaxed.
  - *Suicide in the EMT:* EMS suicide rates are rising; EMTs with often-undiagnosed **PTSD** face a suicide rate **six times higher** than the general population. **24/7 Crisis Resource:** National Suicide Hotline at **1-800-273-8255**.
- **Safe Glove Removal Procedure (7-Step Sequence):**
  - *Core rule:* Contaminated glove surfaces may touch only other contaminated glove surfaces; clean inside glove surfaces may touch only other clean inside glove surfaces. Never touch a contaminated surface with bare hands or fingers, and never touch clean surfaces with contaminated gloves.
  - *Steps:*
    1. Hook gloved fingers under the outer/cuff edge of the opposite glove without touching clean inner surfaces.
    2. Pull the cuff down, keeping contaminated surfaces against contaminated surfaces and avoiding the clean inside.
    3. Pull the glove completely off and inside out so fluids/contaminants remain contained inside.
    4. Crumple the removed contaminated glove inside the remaining gloved hand and dispose of it in a biohazard receptacle without touching clean surfaces.
    5. Slide bare, clean fingers under the clean inside cuff of the remaining glove.
    6. Roll/pull the second glove off and inside out using only clean inside surfaces with your clean hand.
    7. Drop the inverted glove into the biohazard receptacle while keeping clean hands and clean surfaces free of contact with the contaminated exterior.

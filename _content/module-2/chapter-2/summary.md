# Chapter 2: Workforce Safety and Wellness of the EMT — TL;DR

## Workforce safety and shared responsibilities

EMT safety is fundamental to effective patient care: prevent occupational injuries, infectious exposures and entry into uncontrolled hazards while maintaining the physical and psychological capacity to provide care. Advocate for crew safety, use vehicle restraints and safe lifting techniques, and reassess environmental hazards throughout an incident. An EMT is a medical responder, not a technical rescuer or tactical operator unless separately trained, equipped and authorized. [National EMS Education Standards (2021)][education].

| Clinician | General clinical capabilities (not an authorization list) |
| --- | --- |
| EMT | Basic assessment, airway positioning, suction, bag-mask ventilation, hemorrhage control, CPR/AED and authorized medications. |
| AEMT | EMT foundation plus selected vascular access, medications and advanced airway adjuncts. |
| Paramedic | Broader advanced assessment, ECG interpretation, pharmacology, airway management and electrical therapies. |

> [!CAUTION]
> **Knowledge is not authorization.** Evidence informs guidelines; local protocols specify approved actions; legal scope and individual credentialing limit performance; medical direction provides oversight. A partner's request cannot expand scope. AEMTs and paramedics are not interchangeable. [National scope model (2019, updated 2021)][scope].

All levels share responsibility for safety, infection prevention, communication and reassessment. Assign assessment, equipment, transport and communication roles before contact; speak up about hazards regardless of certification.

## Scene hazards and safe operations

### Hazardous materials and rescue

- **Hazmat:** Toxic gases and vapors may be invisible and travel considerable distances. Identify placards from a safe distance using binoculars and the [2024 Emergency Response Guidebook][erg]. Placards distinguish hazards such as explosives, gases, flammables, oxidizers, poisons, radioactive materials and corrosives. The ERG assists initial hazard recognition, not definitive medical treatment.
- **Rescue hazards:** Downed electrical lines (even without sparks), fire, explosions, structural collapse, unsecured trenches, oxygen-deficient confined spaces, chemical/biological/nuclear hazards and moving traffic require specialized scene control.
- Request appropriate resources (fire, hazmat, technical rescue, utility personnel or law enforcement). Trained hazmat personnel may need SCBAs and specialized protective suits. Wait for safe scene control and required patient decontamination before routine EMS care and transport.
- Select PPE for the controlled hazard: helmets, protective eyewear, puncture-resistant gloves, turnout gear and weather-appropriate clothing. A dust respirator does not make an oxygen-deficient confined space safe.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Advanced clinical equipment does not make an unsafe scene safe. Toxic exposures can impair breathing, consciousness and circulation, but monitoring and substance-specific ALS care cannot replace safe removal from exposure and decontamination. Communicate substance, exposure route, findings and decontamination status; avoid contaminating the ambulance outside specialized response arrangements.

**ALS teamwork:** During patient movement, assign responsibility for the airway, oxygen, monitor and vascular lines, secure equipment, use adequate personnel and reassess afterward. Report changes or device disconnection; authorized clinicians verify advanced device function. [NAEMSP airway guidance (2022)][airway].

### Roadway safety

The historical ANSI/ISEA 107-2004 classification described Class 1 for traffic below 25 mph, Class 2 for diverted attention or traffic at least 25 mph, and Class 3 for substantial traffic exposure and distraction. Historical ANSI/ISEA 207-2006 public safety vests added breakaway features and accommodation for turnout gear.

> [!CAUTION]
> These are historical standards, not current apparel-selection rules. The 2023 FHWA MUTCD, § 6C.05, references Class 2 or 3 apparel under ANSI/ISEA 107-2015 or equivalent revisions for workers in roadway temporary traffic control zones, with specified exceptions. Follow adopted rules, incident command and agency-approved apparel. [FHWA MUTCD, 11th edition][mutcd].

### Violence and crime scenes

Stage until law enforcement adequately controls potentially violent scenes, including domestic disputes, fights, threats, hostile behavioral emergencies and crime scenes. Clandestine drug laboratories and chemical suicides may combine chemical and violence hazards. During civil unrest, coordinate safe routes and alternatives with law enforcement. Body armor has limitations; it does not authorize entry into danger.

Preserve evidence unless disturbance is necessary for patient care; document actions. Apparent fatal injury alone does not establish authority to pronounce death. Follow local resuscitation, triage and death-determination procedures. [AHA ethics (2025)][ethics].

**ALS connection — agitation and trauma:** After clearance, assess agitation for possible medical causes (hypoxia, hypoglycemia, intoxication, head injury or delirium), rather than assuming psychiatric illness. Use de-escalation; authorized sedation for severe agitation can impair ventilation, airway protection and circulation. Never use it for punishment or merely to facilitate custody. Avoid restraints that compromise breathing, observe continuously and report deterioration. [Joint EMS restraint statement (2021)][restraint].

In pediatric thoracic trauma, impaired ventilation and hemorrhage threaten oxygenation and tissue perfusion; initially preserved blood pressure does not exclude shock. Prioritize BLS airway, breathing, perfusion, hemorrhage control, heat-loss prevention, age-appropriate assessment and transport. ALS may add monitoring, vascular access or other indicated trauma interventions according to findings and protocol, without unnecessarily delaying definitive care. [NAEMSP pediatric severe trauma (2025)][pediatric]; [AHA/AAP PALS (2025)][pals].

## Infection prevention and occupational exposure

### Pathogens and transmission

| Pathogen | Principal characteristics |
| --- | --- |
| Bacteria | Single-celled organisms capable of independent reproduction in suitable conditions; susceptible infections may respond to antibiotics. |
| Viruses | Require host cells to reproduce; antibiotics do not treat viral infection. Examples include HIV, influenza and SARS-CoV-2. |
| Fungi | May cause opportunistic infections, particularly in immunocompromised patients. |
| Protozoa | Single-celled organisms; transmission may be fecal-oral or vector-borne (e.g., malaria). |
| Helminths | Parasitic worms acquired through ingestion or larval skin penetration; hookworm can cause blood loss and anemia. |

**Infectious** disease results from pathogens; **communicable** disease spreads between people. Not all infectious diseases are ordinarily transmitted person-to-person. Routes include direct contact with blood, wounds or mucous membranes; indirect contact through contaminated objects or sharps; and respiratory particles. Droplet and airborne precautions are distinct infection-control categories despite the particle-size continuum. [CDC isolation guidance][isolation].

| Exposure pattern | Representative diseases and significance |
| --- | --- |
| Blood/body fluids | HIV, hepatitis B and C: sharps and mucous-membrane exposure require prompt evaluation. |
| Respiratory — airborne | Pulmonary/laryngeal TB; varicella also requires contact precautions in addition to airborne and standard precautions. |
| Respiratory — disease-specific | Influenza, pertussis, bacterial meningitis and rubella; select precautions according to suspected organism and exposure. SARS-CoV-2 requires current disease-specific guidance. |
| Contact | Staphylococcal skin infection: infected lesions and contaminated objects. |
| High-consequence body-fluid exposure | Ebola: use trained, EMS-specific risk assessment, PPE and designated transport procedures. |
| Vector-borne and other routes | Zika: infected mosquitoes, sexual and maternal-fetal transmission. |

> [!CAUTION]
> The textbook's historical incubation/PPE table is not a clinical protocol. A surgical mask alone is insufficient crew respiratory protection for varicella. Pneumonia has many possible causes and no universal incubation or precaution category; HIV has distinct acute-infection, diagnostic-window and AIDS-progression timeframes. Ebola requires specialized procedures, not a short generic PPE list. [CDC isolation appendix][isolation]; [CDC VHF EMS guidance][vhf]; [CDC VHF PPE guidance][vhfppe].

**ALS connection:** Infection precautions depend on exposure, not illness severity. Infection can also produce respiratory compromise or sepsis-related circulatory dysfunction. Report breathing, mental status, temperature history, perfusion, blood pressure and trends; ALS monitoring and supportive care may be indicated, but these findings alone neither identify the organism nor establish sepsis. [National model guidelines v3 (2022)][model].

### Standard Precautions, hand hygiene and PPE

Standard Precautions (replacing older BSI terminology) apply regardless of known infection. OSHA requires employers to maintain exposure-control plans, provide training, appropriate PPE and required immunization access; employees must follow procedures and report exposures. [OSHA bloodborne pathogens][osha]; [CDC Standard Precautions][standard].

**Hand hygiene** is central even when wearing gloves. Perform before clean/aseptic tasks, after patient contact and after glove removal. Wash visibly dirty hands with soap and water: wet, lather all surfaces including between fingers and under nails, scrub at least **20 seconds**, rinse and dry. CDC prefers alcohol-based hand rub in most clinical situations when hands are not visibly soiled; the chapter identifies at least **60% alcohol**. Follow pathogen-specific exceptions and agency policy. [CDC clinical hand hygiene][hands].

| PPE | Purpose and limitations |
| --- | --- |
| Gloves | Barrier against contamination; change between patients and contaminated-to-clean tasks. Never reuse; replace torn gloves as soon as safe, clean hands and report exposure if indicated. |
| Eye/face protection | Protects mucous membranes from anticipated splashes. |
| Gown | Protects clothing and skin against significant fluid exposure. |
| Surgical mask | Splash protection and patient source control when appropriate; not equivalent to a respirator. |
| Respirator (e.g., N95) | Respiratory protection for indicated airborne/exposure risks; requires appropriate respiratory-protection program. |

Tight-fitting respirators require fit testing before use and at least annually, a seal check every time they are donned, and no facial hair interfering with the seal or valves. Choose PPE by suspected pathogen and anticipated exposure, including during suction, bag-mask ventilation or advanced airway management. [OSHA respiratory protection][resp]; [CDC empiric precautions][empiric].

**Glove removal:** Grasp the contaminated exterior of the first cuff with a gloved hand, peel inside out and hold it in the remaining glove. Slide bare fingers inside the clean cuff of the second glove and peel it over the first. Dispose appropriately and perform hand hygiene.

**ALS connection — ventilation monitoring:** Pulse oximetry estimates arterial oxygen saturation; waveform capnography provides information about ventilation and indirectly perfusion. Normal saturation, especially with supplemental oxygen, does not establish adequate ventilation. Capnography can be affected by poor perfusion, leaks and equipment faults. Report chest rise, respiratory effort, consciousness and monitor trends; effective BLS ventilation may avoid unnecessary advanced airway placement. [NAEMSP airway guidance (2022)][airway]; [AHA adult ALS (2025)][aha].

### Equipment, waste and environmental control

Cleaning removes soil mechanically; disinfection kills many microorganisms; sterilization destroys all microorganisms. Clean reusable items before appropriate reprocessing.

| Equipment classification | Required principle |
| --- | --- |
| Noncritical — intact skin (BP cuffs, backboards, cables) | Clean and appropriately disinfect. |
| Semicritical — mucous membranes (reusable laryngoscope blades) | At least high-level disinfection; sterilize when required by instructions or policy. |
| Critical — sterile tissue or bloodstream | Sterile equipment; discard single-use sterile devices after use. |

Never reuse disposable equipment. Segregate clean supplies from contaminated equipment and protect sterile components. Use approved disinfectants and required wet contact times. Dispose of sharps immediately in rigid, puncture-resistant sharps containers; **never recap needles**. Handle spills, contaminated laundry and labeled regulated waste through approved employer procedures. Secondary containment depends on leakage/contamination, not diagnosis alone. Never take contaminated work clothing home or improvise bleach mixtures or complex device reprocessing. [OSHA bloodborne pathogens][osha]; [CDC reprocessing][disinfect]; [CDC equipment classification][classification].

### Occupational screening, immunization and exposure

Initial TB screening includes risk assessment, symptoms and IGRA blood testing or tuberculin skin testing as appropriate; prior positive results require different management. Routine annual testing is generally unnecessary without exposure or ongoing transmission. Occupational immunization includes hepatitis B (2- or 3-dose series depending on product), annual influenza, Tdap followed by Td/Tdap every **10 years**, Tdap during each pregnancy, and indicated MMR, varicella and COVID-19 vaccination. OSHA requires hepatitis B vaccine offered without cost within **10 working days** of initial occupational-exposure assignment, subject to exceptions. Individual schedules depend on immunity, age, contraindications and risk; immunization never replaces Standard Precautions. [CDC/NTCA TB (2019)][tb]; [CDC schedules][vaccines]; [OSHA][osha].

> [!WARNING]
> After suspected bloodborne exposure, wash injured skin with soap and water or flush exposed mucous membranes; promptly report to the supervisor/designated officer, document time, fluid and circumstances, and obtain urgent occupational-health evaluation. **Do not wait for source-patient testing or facility notification.** Occupational HIV PEP, when indicated, should begin as soon as possible and within **72 hours** under the 2025 US PHS guidance; unusually high-risk later presentations require specialist consultation. PEP and follow-up are clinician-directed, and other infections require different evaluation. [2025 US PHS occupational HIV guidelines][pep].

For qualifying airborne disease, the Ryan White Act requires facility notification to the designated officer as soon as practicable, no later than **48 hours after determination**, not the historical 24-hour estimate. For suspected bloodborne exposure, the designated officer may request facility information, with findings reported through the applicable notification process. This pathway is separate from immediate medical care; local requirements may be more stringent. [42 USC § 300ff–132][ryanlaw]; [CDC/NIOSH notification process][ryan].

## Emotional care, death and dying

Distress is physical or mental suffering; emotional crisis occurs when demands exceed coping capacity. Care includes restoring emotional stability while addressing illness or injury.

The traditional Kübler-Ross model describes possible responses to death or other major loss:

| Response | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Denial | Difficulty accepting the reality of loss or impending death. |
| Anger | Frustration or perceived injustice, sometimes directed at responders. |
| Bargaining | Attempts to negotiate postponement of loss. |
| Depression | Sadness, withdrawal or despair as reality becomes apparent. |
| Acceptance | Recognition of the loss, not necessarily happiness about it. |

These responses are neither universal nor sequential. Continue physiological and mental-status assessment; withdrawal or confusion may reflect acute illness rather than grief.

Preserve dignity, privacy, respect and control. Explain care to patients, including unresponsive patients; orient them, communicate clearly, listen empathetically and allow safe expressions of grief. Give honest, compassionate information without false reassurance or speculation. Offer family presence during resuscitation when safe and feasible, without interfering with care. Honor feasible patient requests and arrange support, spiritual assistance when requested, or contact with the deceased according to policy.

> [!CAUTION]
> At a potential crime scene, do not clean the body or permit family contact until appropriate authorities grant permission; preserve evidence and document actions. At other scenes, viewing or touching the deceased depends on policy and circumstances.

**ALS connection:** EMT compressions, ventilation, AED use and history support advanced assessment and treatment. Report documented patient wishes or possible DNR orders; a family statement alone does not establish a valid order. Rhythm monitoring does not guarantee survival, and ALS certification does not automatically authorize death pronouncement. Withholding or terminating resuscitation follows applicable law, protocols and medical direction. Communicate death compassionately within assigned responsibilities. [AHA ethics and adult ALS (2025)][ethics]; [adult ALS][aha].

## Occupational stress and critical incident support

EMS combines long or irregular shifts, immediate response demands, life-and-death decisions, fear of errors and repeated exposure to suffering. Pediatric emergencies, abuse, severe trauma, multiple-casualty incidents and coworker injuries or deaths may be particularly stressful.

| Stress reaction | Timing and significance |
| --- | --- |
| Acute | Immediately or shortly after an incident; may involve sweating, tachycardia, tremors, nausea, poor concentration or disturbed sleep. Absence of these reactions is also normal. |
| Delayed | Symptoms may appear days, months or years later; PTSD may involve flashbacks, nightmares, irritability, insomnia and difficulty relating to others. Professional evaluation is important. |
| Cumulative | Repeated stress builds over time, potentially progressing from anxiety and irritability to emotional exhaustion, physical symptoms and burnout, impairing work performance. |

Recognize changes in thinking (poor judgment, forgetfulness), emotions (anxiety, depression, anger), physical functioning (exhaustion, headaches, gastrointestinal distress), behavior (substance use, appetite and sleep changes) and relationships (withdrawal, conflict, reduced empathy).

> [!WARNING]
> Do not assume chest pain, syncope or breathing difficulty is a stress reaction. Sympathetic activation can resemble hypoglycemia, hypoxemia, cardiac ischemia or other acute illness. Stop unsafe work and obtain prompt medical assessment; ALS may add ECG, glucose testing or monitoring as indicated.

Manage stress early: prioritize responsibilities, discuss concerns with trusted people, acknowledge mistakes and learn from them, maintain family time, exercise and pursue restorative activities. Explain shift demands and safety precautions to loved ones. At work, use a buddy system, supportive communication, exercise breaks and feasible changes in shifts or assignments. Seek professional counseling or an Employee Assistance Program (EAP) when needed.

**ALS teamwork:** Stress and fatigue increase error risk. Use clear task assignments, cross-checks and closed-loop communication (repeat the request and confirm completion). Clarify ambiguous instructions rather than guessing. [NAEMSP airway guidance (2022)][airway].

### Critical Incident Stress Management (CISM)

Critical incident stress may produce intrusive memories, burnout, fear of returning to duty and impaired performance. Comprehensive CISM can include preincident education, peer support, individual and family assistance, disaster support, follow-up and referral.

| Traditional approach | Source-described structure |
| --- | --- |
| Critical Incident Stress Debriefing (CISD) | Historically held within 24–72 hours; seven phases cover facts, feelings, symptoms, processing, coping suggestions, return-to-work planning and follow-up. Intended as confidential, nonblaming support, not psychotherapy. |
| Defusing | Historically within 8 hours, lasting 30–45 minutes; less structured, involving those most directly affected. |

> [!CAUTION]
> Traditional timing and phases are descriptive, not evidence of effectiveness. NICE (2018) and WHO (2012) recommend against psychologically focused debriefing for PTSD prevention; NICE also advises against it for treatment. Do not compel emotional disclosure or promise that debriefing prevents PTSD. Voluntary peer support, practical assistance, professional evaluation and operational quality-improvement review are distinct activities. Apparent early recovery does not eliminate the need for follow-up when indicated. [NICE NG116][nice]; [WHO guidance][who].

## Physical, mental and social wellness

Wellness extends beyond absence of disease and includes six interrelated dimensions: **physical, intellectual, emotional, social, environmental and spiritual**. An imbalance can affect health and clinical performance.

### Physical fitness, nutrition and sleep

| Fitness component | EMS significance |
| --- | --- |
| Cardiovascular endurance | Sustained delivery of oxygen and nutrients during prolonged exertion; improved through aerobic exercise. |
| Muscle strength | Force produced in a single contraction; supports lifting. |
| Muscle endurance | Repeated or sustained muscular work without excessive fatigue; supports prolonged carrying. |
| Muscle flexibility | Joint range of motion; supports safe movement and reduces injury susceptibility. |
| Body composition | Proportion of body fat to total weight; related to chronic disease risk and influenced by exercise and nutrition. |

The chapter's aerobic exercise example uses 30–40 minutes at target heart rate at least three days weekly, with the following estimated target (not an individualized prescription):

`Target HR = resting HR + 0.70 × [(220 − age) − resting HR]`

Age is in years; heart rates are in beats per minute. Resistance training develops strength and endurance; stretching supports flexibility. Favor balanced nutrition, lean protein and moderation of sugar, caffeine, alcohol and saturated fat. Use relaxation, meditation, enjoyable activities and social support instead of self-medication.

**Sleep and fatigue:** Shift work disrupts circadian rhythms, impairing cognition, coordination and social functioning. Sleep deprivation increases injury, driving and patient-care errors and contributes to chronic health problems. CDC generally recommends at least **7 hours** for adults aged 18–60; the source's 8–10-hour figure is not a universal adult minimum. Protect an uninterrupted sleep period in a cool, dark environment and avoid caffeine, heavy meals and exercise immediately before sleep. [CDC sleep guidance][sleep].

The 2018 evidence-based EMS fatigue recommendations include:

1. Monitor fatigue/sleepiness using reliable or valid tools.
2. Work shifts shorter than **24 hours**.
3. Provide access to caffeine as a fatigue countermeasure.
4. Provide opportunities for on-duty naps.
5. Educate personnel about fatigue and risk mitigation.

Caffeine and naps do not replace adequate sleep or safe staffing. Fatigue affects all levels of care, including driving, airway management, medication verification and handoff; escalate when unable to work safely. [EMS fatigue guidelines (2018)][fatigue].

### Mental health, resilience and substance use

Smoking increases cardiovascular, pulmonary and cancer risks; cessation supports health. Alcohol or drug use as a coping strategy can worsen stress and impair functioning. Seek professional assistance for concerning reliance on substances.

**Resilience** is the ability to cope with adversity without lasting physical or psychological harm. Sleep, nutrition, exercise, relationships and healthy coping support resilience, but workplace conditions, supportive leadership, safe staffing and access to professional care also matter. Resilience is not a guarantee against mental illness or suicide. EMS personnel experiencing persistent distress, PTSD symptoms, depression or impaired functioning should have access to peer support, EAP or professional evaluation.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> For a suicide or mental-health crisis in the United States, call or text **988**; call **911** for an immediate life-threatening emergency. [SAMHSA crisis resources][crisis].

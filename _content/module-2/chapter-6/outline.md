# Chapter 6: Lifting and Moving Patients

## Key Terms (Grouped by Concept)
- **Body Mechanics & Posture:** body mechanics, kyphosis, lordosis, power grip, power lift
- **Patient Move Categories:** emergency move, urgent move, nonurgent move

---

## 1. Foundations of Body Mechanics, Posture & Teamwork

### Core Priorities of Lifting & Moving
- Lifting and/or moving a patient—from where they are found to the stretcher, into the ambulance, and onto the hospital stretcher—occurs on almost every patient contact.
- Proper body mechanics protect EMT health, longevity, and effectiveness across repeated lifts of patients and heavy equipment.
- **Golden Priorities:**
  - Choose the easiest method that does not injure or cause pain to you or the patient.
  - Let equipment do the work whenever possible, or use a device designed for lifting when lifting is required.
  - Get as much help as possible to carry patients and equipment.
  - Never risk falling or injuring yourself, and follow the rules of body mechanics consistently.

### Four Basic Principles of Body Mechanics
- **Body mechanics** are the safest and most efficient methods of using your body to gain a mechanical advantage, built on four foundational principles:
  1. **Keep weight close to the body:** Hold objects as close to your body as possible. Reaching far to lift a light object is more likely to cause a back injury than lifting a heavy object held close.
  2. **Engage large lower-body and core muscles:** Use your leg, hip, and gluteal (buttocks) muscles along with contracted abdominal muscles to generate power safely when moving heavy objects, and avoid using back muscles for heavy lifting.
  3. **“Stack” and align as a unit:** Keep shoulders stacked over hips and hips stacked over feet (your base), moving them together. Misalignment creates twisting forces that can harm the lower back.
  4. **Reduce lift height or travel distance:** Move closer to the object, reposition it before lifting, or move it in stages.
- **Spinal, Joint & Equipment Practices:** Keep a normal inward curve in the lower back to reduce spinal injury risk, keep wrists and knees in normal alignment to prevent extremity injuries, and substitute equipment for manual force whenever possible.

### Posture and Physical Fitness
- **Impact of Posture:** Because EMTs spend much time sitting or standing, poor posture fatigues back and abdominal muscles and leaves the back vulnerable to injury.
- **Extremes of Poor Posture:**
  - *Excessive lordosis (swayback):* Stomach is positioned too far anterior and buttocks too far posterior, placing excessive stress on the lumbar spine.
  - *Excessive kyphosis (slouch):* Shoulders roll forward, fatiguing the lower back and increasing pressure across every region of the spine.
- **Proper Standing Posture:** Align ears, shoulders, and hips vertically with knees slightly bent and pelvis slightly tucked forward.
- **Proper Sitting Posture:** Distribute weight evenly across both ischia (lower pelvic bones), align ears, shoulders, and hips vertically, keep feet flat on the floor or crossed at the ankles, and support the lower back against the chair when possible.
- **Role of Physical Fitness:** Body mechanics alone cannot fully protect an unfit EMT; a proactive fitness routine combining flexibility training, cardiovascular conditioning, strength training, and proper nutrition supports injury prevention, performance, and stress management.

### Communication and Teamwork
- **Team Composition & Strength Matching:** All team members must know proper lifting techniques. Mismatched partners create hazards for both: an overloaded weaker partner risks injury, and a stronger partner can be injured if a weaker partner cannot lift. Ideally, lifting/moving partners should have comparable, adequate strength and height.
- **Coordination During Lifts:** Communicate continuously throughout lifting and moving using clear, easy-to-understand commands and verbally coordinating each lift from start to finish.
- **Key Benefits of Teamwork:**
  - Size up the scene immediately and accurately.
  - Assess patient weight and recognize when extra help is needed.
  - Account for each team member's physical abilities and limitations.
  - Choose the most appropriate equipment for the task.
- **Communicating with the Patient:** A startled or frightened patient who shifts body weight mid-lift can cause disabling rescuer injuries or serious additional patient injury. Whenever a patient can understand, explain the plan before acting to build confidence and enlist their help.

## 2. General Guidelines for Lifting, Reaching, Pushing & Pulling

### Core Lifting & Carrying Principles
- Know your physical abilities and limitations, and never overestimate yourself or other rescuers.
- Check patient weight and equipment weight limits before lifting, and call for extra help whenever needed rather than proceeding before knowing it is safe.
- Use an even number of rescuers whenever possible for balance.
- Limit two-rescuer carries of heavy loads to **1 minute or less** to prevent muscle fatigue and higher injury risk.
- Transport patients and equipment on wheeled stretchers or other rolling devices whenever possible.
- Keep carried weight close to your body, maintain a locked-in back position, avoid hyperextending or leaning back from the waist, and never lift and twist simultaneously (lift first, then turn as a unit).

### The Power Lift & Power Grip
- **The Power Lift:** Offers the strongest defense against injury while protecting the patient with a safe, stable move, and also helps rescuers with weak knees or thighs; keep your back locked and avoid bending at the waist:
  1. Place feet a comfortable distance apart (typically shoulder width, or slightly wider for taller rescuers to get closer to the object).
  2. Turn feet slightly outward for comfort and stability.
  3. Bend knees to lower your center of gravity toward the object so it feels like sitting down rather than falling forward.
  4. Tighten back and abdominal muscles to splint the lower back, keeping the back as straight as comfortable (with a normal slight inward curve) and head neutral facing forward.
  5. Straddle the object with feet flat and weight evenly distributed just forward of the heels.
  6. Position hands a comfortable distance apart (usually at least **10 inches**) for balance during the lift.
  7. Use a **power grip** so palms and fingers make full contact with the object and all fingers bend at the same angle for maximum hand force.
  8. Begin lifting with a locked-in back, driving force through the heels and arches of your feet while bringing the upper body up before the hips.
  9. Reverse the steps to lower a wheeled stretcher or other object.

### The Squat Lift & One-Handed Carrying Technique
- **The Squat Lift:** Alternative technique for a rescuer with one weak leg or ankle—or with strong, healthy knees and legs—when avoiding waist bending:
  1. Keep your weaker leg slightly forward with that foot flat on the ground throughout the lift.
  2. Squat down to grasp the stretcher or patient-moving device using a power grip.
  3. Push up with your stronger leg while keeping your back locked, leading with your head, and raising the upper body before the hips.
- **One-Handed Equipment-Carrying Technique:** Maintain a locked back, proper body mechanics, and balanced alignment without leaning excessively to the opposite side:
  1. Stagger your feet with one knee up and the other pointing toward the ground.
  2. Bend at the hips (not the waist) and keep trunk forward tilt at **45 degrees** or less.
  3. On command from the rescuer at the patient's head, drive upward simultaneously through the front foot's arch and heel and the back foot's ball.

### Reaching, Pushing & Pulling
- **Reaching:**
  - Sustained effort limits generally cap out at **100 percent** for **6 seconds** and **50 percent** for **1 minute** before fatigue sharply raises injury risk; avoid prolonged strenuous effort over **1 minute**.
  - Reposition objects or move closer to reduce reaching and lifting whenever possible.
  - Limit forward reach for equipment or patients (such as during a log roll) to **15–20 inches**—if farther than **20 inches**, move closer before reaching and lifting.
  - Keep the back locked without twisting when reaching, support upper-body weight with your free arm when possible, and avoid leaning back from the waist (hyperextending) on overhead reaches.
  - During a log roll, lean from the hips with a straight back and use stronger shoulder muscles to assist when possible.
- **Pushing and Pulling:**
  - Push rather than pull whenever possible.
  - *Pulling:* Keep the load close to your body between shoulders and hips, maintain a straight back with slightly bent knees to keep the line of pull through the center of your body.
  - *Pushing:* Push from between waist and shoulder height when possible, use a kneeling position for loads below waist level to avoid bending, keep elbows bent with arms close to your sides for greater force, and never push or pull overhead objects due to high injury risk.

## 3. Patient Moves: Emergency, Urgent & Nonurgent

### Three Categories of Patient Moves
- **Emergency move:** Performed when there is *immediate danger to the patient or rescuer*.
- **Urgent move:** Performed when the patient has an *immediate threat to life* and must be moved quickly and transported for care.
- **Nonurgent move:** Performed when *no immediate threat to life* exists and the patient can be moved normally when ready for transport.

### Emergency Moves
- **Priority Shift & Precautions:**
  - Airway, breathing, and circulation—and immediate life threats—normally take top priority before moving a patient, except when an unstable or threatening scene forces moving the patient first.
  - Use emergency moves only when no other options are available, follow local protocol, and protect yourself from becoming an additional victim.
- **Indications:**
  - *Immediate environmental danger:* Fire or danger of fire; exposure to explosives or hazardous materials requiring immediate removal; inability to protect the patient from uncontrolled traffic, physically unstable surroundings, extreme weather, or hostile crowds.
  - *Access for lifesaving care:* Inability to provide lifesaving care due to the patient's location or position (e.g., to control hemorrhage, defibrillate, or perform CPR).
- **Spinal & Axial Alignment Rule:**
  - Greatest danger in a rapid emergency move is aggravating a spinal column or spinal cord injury because full spine protection is impossible when moving quickly.
  - Keep the head and neck neutral, align the nose with the umbilicus (navel), and pull along the long axis of the body.
- **Three Emergency Move Techniques:**
  - **The Armpit-Forearm Drag:** From behind a patient on the floor or ground, slide hands under the armpits, grasp the left forearm with your right hand and right forearm with your left hand, and drag along the long axis of the body.
  - **The Shirt Drag:** Do not use if wearing only a T-shirt; otherwise, loosely fasten hands/wrists together (and link to a belt/pants with a Velcro strap or self-adherent bandage if possible to prevent arms from flopping or slipping out of the shirt), grasp the shirt collar/shoulders so the head rests on your fists, and pull toward you while engaging the armpits rather than strangling or straining the neck (reposition hands if excessive neck pressure occurs).
  - **The Blanket Drag:** Single-rescuer drag using a blanket (or a coat if no blanket is available):
    1. Spread the blanket alongside the patient and gather about half into lengthwise pleats.
    2. Roll the patient away from you onto their side and tuck the pleated portion as far beneath the patient as possible.
    3. Roll the patient back onto their back centered on the blanket.
    4. Wrap the blanket securely around the patient.
    5. Grasp the blanket fabric beneath the patient's head and drag toward you.

### Urgent Moves
- **General Use:** Used for critical motor vehicle collision patients needing rapid removal for emergency care and immediate transport, as well as medical patients needing urgent repositioning (such as moving a cardiac arrest patient from a couch onto the floor or hard surface for effective resuscitation).
- **Rapid Extrication:**
  - *Indications:* Any abnormality of airway, breathing, oxygenation, or circulation, or critical injuries/illnesses—including altered mental status, inadequate respiratory rate or tidal volume, signs of shock (altered mental status; pale, cool, clammy skin; tachycardia; tachypnea), head/neck/chest/abdomen/pelvis injuries, bilateral femur fractures, or major bleeding.
  - *Procedure Summary:*
    1. One rescuer brings the head into a neutral in-line stabilization position and holds manual stabilization from behind or the side.
    2. A second rescuer applies a cervical-spine immobilization collar while a third places a long backboard near the door and moves to the passenger seat.
    3. The second rescuer supports the thorax while the third frees the legs from pedals or under the dashboard.
    4. The second and third rescuers rotate the patient in short, coordinated moves until the back is in the open doorway and feet rest on the seat.
    5. Another rescuer supports the head while the first rescuer exits to take over head stabilization from the door opening.
    6. Place the end of the long backboard on the seat beside the buttocks; assistants support the far end while the first and second rescuers lower the patient onto the board with ongoing manual in-line stabilization.
    7. Slide the patient into proper position on the board in short, coordinated moves while maintaining manual stabilization.
  - *Safety & Post-Extrication Notes:* Accomplish rapidly without compromising the spine; support your weight inside the vehicle with a free arm or by resting your chest against seat backs to protect your lower back; per local protocol, the patient may be moved off the backboard onto a vacuum mattress or stretcher mattress once at the stretcher (using the board solely for extrication/movement rather than ongoing immobilization).
- **Self-Extrication:**
  - Used as an urgent move to quickly reach another patient in the vehicle when the blocking patient is not critically injured and has no injuries preventing self-movement.
  - Coach the patient to keep head and neck neutral (no flexion, extension, or lateral movement; nose aligned with umbilicus), apply a rigid or soft cervical collar as a movement reminder, have the patient turn as a unit with legs swung out facing outside the door, stand, and exit while coaching neutral head/neck alignment and assisting as needed.

### Nonurgent Moves
- **General Principles:**
  - Choose the easiest safe method that avoids injury or pain when no immediate threat to life exists—including "walking" a capable patient with support (never walk a patient who becomes light-headed or sweaty on standing, or has chest pain, respiratory distress, a lower-extremity injury, or suspected spinal injury).
  - Move the patient as a unit, keep head and neck neutral, take full spinal precautions if head/neck/spinal injury is suspected, align the plan with all rescuers beforehand, and assign one rescuer to call commands.
- **Direct Ground Lift:**
  - Not recommended for heavier patients (a long backboard is usually safer and more efficient from the ground); when used:
    1. Position two or three rescuers on the same side of the patient, kneeling on one knee (preferably the same knee for all).
    2. Second rescuer places patient's arms on chest if possible.
    3. First rescuer cradles head/neck/shoulder with one arm and lower back with the other.
    4. Second rescuer places one arm under knees and one above buttocks.
    5. If a third rescuer is present, they place both arms under the waist while the other two slide arms up to midback or down to buttocks as appropriate.
    6. On the first rescuer's signal, lift to knees and roll the patient in toward chests (shoulders on one knee, hips on the other, arms crossed, legs hanging at the knee).
    7. On the first rescuer's signal, stand and carry supine to the stretcher/device; reverse steps to lower.
    8. Bend at hips (not waist), keep back straight, and lift with legs and buttocks.
- **Extremity Lift:**
  - Used to move a patient from the ground to a carrying device; **do not use** with suspected spinal or extremity injuries:
    1. First rescuer kneels at head and second rescuer kneels at side by knees.
    2. First rescuer places a hand under each shoulder while second rescuer grasps wrists.
    3. First rescuer slips hands under arms and grasps wrists.
    4. Second rescuer slips hands under knees.
    5. Both move to a crouching position with straight backs and neutral heads aligned with the back (avoiding hyperextension or excessive forward flexion).
    6. On the first rescuer's signal, stand together using leg and gluteal muscles and carry to the carrying device.
- **Direct Carry Method:**
  - Transfers a supine patient from a bed to a wheeled stretcher or between carrying devices:
    1. Place the wheeled stretcher perpendicular to the bed with the stretcher head at the foot of the bed.
    2. Prep the stretcher (unbuckle straps, remove items) and have both rescuers stand between bed and stretcher facing the patient.
    3. First rescuer slides an arm under neck and cups shoulder.
    4. Second rescuer slides a hand under hip and lifts slightly while first rescuer slides an arm under back, then second rescuer places arms under hips and calves.
    5. Slide patient to bed edge, lift and curl toward chests (rolling toward rescuers), and rotate/place gently supine onto the wheeled stretcher.
- **Draw Sheet Method:**
  - Transfers a supine patient from a bed to a wheeled stretcher or between carrying devices:
    1. Loosen the bottom bed sheet.
    2. Position the wheeled stretcher alongside the bed and prepare it (adjust height, lower rails, unbuckle straps).
    3. Reach across the stretcher grasping the sheet firmly at head, chest, hips, and knees (with foot support under calves if needed), bracing hips against the stretcher.
    4. Slide the patient gently onto the wheeled stretcher while contracting abdominal and gluteal muscles to splint the lower back.

## 4. Packaging, Carrying Devices & Special Transport Considerations

### Overview & General Packaging Principles
- **Definition:** Readying a patient for transport once assessment and emergency care are complete—selecting and preparing the carrying device, safely transferring and securing the patient, and moving the packaged patient to the ambulance (or aircraft) for loading and unloading.
- **Core Precautions:**
  - Verify the carrying device is locked in the open position before positioning the patient.
  - Typically place a sheet or blanket on the device first, and cover the patient appropriately with sheets or blankets after positioning to maintain body temperature.
  - Secure the patient with straps, tucking or positioning all straps and ties so nobody trips or falls.
  - Confirm both the patient and carrying device are properly secured inside the ambulance before moving, and reassess strap and device security after every move.
  - **Spine Motion Restriction (SMR):** If head, neck, or spinal injury is suspected, limit spine movement before, during, and after packaging.
  - Choose devices that do not worsen medical or trauma conditions, practice frequently, and follow manufacturer instructions for inspection, cleaning, repair, and maintenance.

### Patient-Carrying Devices & Equipment
- **Wheeled Stretchers (Ambulance Gurneys):**
  - Most commonly used, safest, and most comfortable transfer device; most accommodate up to **650 pounds**, adapt to nearly any patient position, and carry/secure equipment.
  - *Manual Stretchers:* Traditional lift-in models have largely been replaced by *roll-in* or *self-loading* aluminum-alloy stretchers (**~70–95 pounds**) with special head wheels that reduce twisting and lifting. On smooth ground, one rescuer pushes at the head while one guides at the foot; four rescuers (one per corner) stabilize over rough ground; two rescuers facing each other at opposite ends can carry in narrow spaces with care against unbalancing.
  - *Loading Roll-In Manual Stretcher:* Place head-end wheels securely on the ambulance floor, release the wheel lever (with a slight lift if needed) while guiding the collapsing carriage, and lock the stretcher into front and rear securing devices.
  - *Power Stretchers:* Use pneumatic, hydraulic, battery, or combined mechanisms (some push-button) to raise/lower the carriage, reducing repetitive spinal loading—though often weighing up to **140 pounds**; *"no-lift-at-all"* power stretchers retract horizontally into the cabin via power controls and confirm engagement (such as a green indicator light).
  - *Safety & Loading Rules:* Never leave a patient on a wheeled stretcher unattended (especially when raised, even when strapped); verify adequate lifting power before loading; load hanging stretchers before wheeled stretchers; secure both stretcher and patient before driving.
- **Bariatric Stretchers and Devices:**
  - Heavy-duty wide-deck stretchers designed for larger/overweight patients—some rated up to **850–1,600 pounds** in a wheels-down position—featuring larger stability wheels, wide wheelbases, extended handles, and optional ambulance ramp and winch loading systems.
- **Portable Stretchers:**
  - Standard tubular metal frame with canvas or coated fabric bottom and securing straps (*soft stretcher*), plus *pole stretchers* or *canvas litters* (two poles with fabric and crosspieces).
  - *Use & Limitations:* Ideal for narrow/confined spaces where a wheeled stretcher cannot fit, or as an auxiliary stretcher (squad bench or hanging hardware) for multiple patients; folds compactly and vinyl-coated models clean easily. Basic models hold up to **350 pounds** (not recommended at maximum weight); must be carried; metal frames can interfere with some X-rays; ensure pole crosspieces are locked; pad the head and avoid rocky ground; do not use for spinal immobilization unless paired with a long backboard; four or more rescuers are preferred when lifting a pole stretcher.
- **Stair Chairs:**
  - Useful for narrow corridors/doorways, small elevators, and stairways where a wheeled stretcher cannot pass; some convert into portable stretchers.
  - *Contraindications:* Do **not** use for patients with altered mental status, suspected spinal injury, or lower-extremity injuries.
  - *Standard Stair Carry Steps:*
    1. Explain the plan to the patient and verify all straps are secure; position one rescuer behind at the head, one at the foot facing the patient, and a third rescuer ("spotter") guiding/counting steps (spotter above when going up, spotter below when going down); tell the patient not to reach out.
    2. Rescuer at the head tilts the chair back while the rescuer at the foot grasps the chair legs.
    3. Lift and begin carrying simultaneously, keeping wheels off the steps.
    4. Spotter counts out steps and calls upcoming conditions during ascent or descent.
  - *Tracked Stair Chairs:* Feature a track lowered behind the seat that glides down stairs with minimal EMT lifting effort (still requiring head and foot rescuers for stabilization), including powered models rated up to **500 pounds**.
- **Backboards & Full-Body Vacuum Mattresses:**
  - *Long & Short Backboards:* Lightweight plastic or composite boards with molded handholds (e.g., *Farrington* with rounded corners; *Ohio* with mitered corners/tapering sides that fit most basket stretchers and maneuver well in cars).
    - *Advantages:* Good lifting/moving tool, floats, lightweight, compact, CPR surface, X-ray translucent, carriable/loadable from ends or sides, and integrates with other gear.
    - *Disadvantages:* Must be carried, often stays with the patient, unstable on inclines, uncomfortable, can reduce breathing efficiency, risk hypothermia in cold settings, cause pressure sores or pain, and may be used primarily for extrication/movement before transferring to a stretcher mattress per local protocol.
  - *Full-Body Vacuum Mattress:* Conforms to the patient's shape once air is withdrawn to become rigid with multiple hand grips; eliminates void padding, fits inside a basket stretcher, and supports spine motion restriction (shape around patient and withdraw air, then secure patient).
- **Scoop Stretchers:**
  - Assembled and disassembled around the patient (typically rated up to **300 pounds**); useful in confined spaces and for pelvic or bilateral femur fractures.
  - *Limitations & Cautions:* Not recommended for suspected spinal injury; takes up considerable space; all-metal models adopt ambient temperature (prewarm in cold weather) and require padding of the head and bony prominences.
  - *Application Steps:* (1) Adjust length to fit patient → (2) separate halves and place beside patient; keeping spine in-line, gently roll patient onto one side and slide half underneath → (3) inspect back if not yet examined, then return supine → (4) assemble head end → (5) roll patient to opposite side, swing remaining half closed, and latch foot end → (6) pad head and bony prominences with pillow/folded sheet → (7) secure with at least three body straps.
- **Basket Stretchers (Stokes Basket):**
  - Long basket-shaped stretcher in welded metal with contoured chicken-wire web or tubular aluminum with molded polyethylene shell; accepts a scoop stretcher or Ohio-type backboard and secures onto a wheeled stretcher.
  - Enables full immobilization of a back-boarded patient across rough terrain; polyethylene shells slide smoothly over snow/rough ground while shielding against branches/twigs. Bulky, costly, often left with patient, metal can interfere with X-rays, and rope/ladder rescues require specialized training. Place a wheeled-stretcher mattress inside for comfort and cold insulation, pad body edges with rolled blankets, and strap securely with nylon webbing.
- **Flexible Stretchers:**
  - Canvas or synthetic transfer device (such as a *Reeves* flexible stretcher) with six carrying handles (three per side), ideal for narrow hallways (e.g., mobile homes), rapid spine motion restriction in tight spaces, or housing a back-boarded patient over stairs or rough terrain.

### Carrying Guidelines on Flat Terrain & Stairs
- **General Carrying Rules:**
  - Secure the patient to the device with a **minimum of three straps** and secure the patient's hands within the device so they cannot grab rescuers, railings, or objects.
  - **Feet first** when going **down steps** or **downhill**; **head first** when going **up steps** or **uphill**.
  - Account for rescuer weight/physical limits, keep weight close to your body, communicate continuously, and call specialized rescue teams for complex/high-angle/unstable terrain.
- **Two-Person Carry:**
  - One EMT at the **head** and one at the **foot**; place the **stronger rescuer at the head** because most body weight is there.
  - Foot EMT faces head EMT and walks **backward** (high trip risk and easy side-to-side unbalancing; rapid fatigue). Place a third person behind the foot EMT as a **spotter** (often grasping the foot EMT's belt for balance and calling out obstacles) when available.
- **Four-Person Carry:**
  - *Diamond Configuration:* One rescuer at the **head**, one at the **foot** facing forward with hands behind body grasping the device, and two rescuers on **opposite sides** facing forward grasping with the inner hand.
  - *Rectangular Configuration:* Two rescuers on opposite sides at the **head end** and two on opposite sides at the **foot end**, all facing forward and grasping with the inner hand.
- **Carrying a Supine Patient on Stairs:**
  - Use a stair chair when mental status and condition allow; if the patient must move supine, strap against longitudinal sliding and secure hands inside the device.
  - *Going Downstairs (Narrow Stairwells):* One EMT at the **head** facing the patient and one EMT at the **foot** facing the patient with their **back to the stairs**, going down **feet first**; place a **spotter** behind the foot EMT to guide, stabilize, and count steps.
  - *Going Upstairs:* Same two-EMT arrangement moving **head first**, so the **head** EMT moves **backward** up the steps; place a **spotter** ahead of the head EMT to guide and count steps.

### Patient Positioning, Special Populations & Neonatal Isolette
- **Clinical Positioning Rules:**
  - *Unresponsive without suspected head, neck, or spinal injury:* Left lateral recumbent (coma/recovery) position facing the rescuer in the ambulance to drain fluids/vomitus and prevent aspiration.
  - *Chest pain/discomfort or breathing difficulty:* Position of comfort—typically sitting up—if hypotension is absent.
  - *Suspected spinal injury:* Immobilize on a long backboard, vacuum mattress, or stretcher mattress per local protocol (or remove from backboard onto stretcher after extrication if permitted).
  - *Shock (hypoperfusion):* Supine unless protocol directs otherwise.
  - *Alert with nausea or vomiting:* Sitting or recovery position allowing airway management.
  - *Pregnancy past 20 weeks' gestation:* Left side (preferred) or supine with right hip elevated to lift the fetus off the inferior vena cava and prevent **supine hypotensive syndrome** (dizziness, hypotension, decreased cardiac output); if a **prolapsed umbilical cord** is suspected, place supine with hips elevated on a pillow per local protocol.
- **Special Populations:**
  - *Infants and toddlers:* Transport non-critically injured children in an undamaged car seat (ideally their own to ease fear), packing spaces around the child with rolled towels or taped folded sheets for immobilization.
  - *Older adults:* Watch for osteoporosis (brittle bones prone to fracture), handle gently, and ensure the patient understands what is happening and where they are going.
  - *Physical disabilities:* Adapt to fused joints or twisted limbs, pad areas needing support with rolled towels/padding, take extra care with straps, and ask the patient which positions are possible and comfortable.
- **Neonatal Isolette:**
  - Used for interfacility transport of a neonate to maintain a warm environment and prevent **hypothermia**.
  - Wheeled devices designed to engage standard ambulance stretcher mounts; verify secure ambulance mounting and either lift in or roll up a ramp.

### Packaging Patients for Air Transport (13 Rules)
1. Ensure any patient contaminated by hazardous materials is thoroughly decontaminated first (fumes in a confined cabin can incapacitate the crew, and many programs refuse hazmat-exposed patients because permanent aircraft removal can occur if decontamination fails).
2. Manage the airway with an endotracheal tube before aircraft arrival if possible and appropriate.
3. Keep the chest accessible on intubated patients so air crew can assess breath sounds prior to transport.
4. Confirm any backboard used for helicopter transport fits the responding helicopter model.
5. Secure the patient well to the backboard to prevent jostling during movement or flight.
6. Secure loose equipment, blankets, and sheets with tape or straps so nothing blows into rotors or engines, and secure loose scene gear.
7. Inform the patient of actions taken and prepare them for incoming helicopter noise and rotor wash.
8. Cover the patient's eyes, ears, and exposed wounds to protect against noise, dust, and debris.
9. Consider having an engine company wet the landing zone to reduce blowing dust and debris.
10. Have rescuers remove loose clothing or hats that could be drawn into rotors or engines.
11. Wait for pilot or crew instruction before approaching with the patient; let the crew assist loading unless instructed otherwise, stay well clear of the tail rotor, observe all placards, and know airframe-specific approach rules beforehand.
12. Lay an IV bag on the patient's chest during movement to the helicopter rather than having a rescuer hold it up.
13. Minimize personnel under the rotors at all times during loading, following local protocols and air medical guidelines.

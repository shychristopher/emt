# Chapter 6: Lifting and Moving Patients — TL;DR

## Principles of Safe Patient Movement

Patient movement is part of treatment: choose the easiest safe method that preserves airway access, ventilation, perfusion, comfort and ongoing care. The route, patient condition, equipment and available personnel jointly determine the method.

- Let equipment do the work; roll rather than carry when possible. Use purpose-designed devices and request adequate help before lifting.
- Never sacrifice rescuer safety for convenience. Assess hazards, patient weight, combined equipment weight, device capacity and crew capability before committing.
- Coordinate the entire move through one leader, clear commands and an agreed stop signal. Explain the plan to the patient; unexpected weight shifts can injure both patient and rescuers.

> [!NOTE]
> **ALS connection:** Assign clinical and carrying roles separately when necessary. An EMT provides foundational assessment, airway support, ventilation, hemorrhage control and reassessment; AEMTs and paramedics may provide additional authorized interventions. ALS presence does not expand EMT scope. Actual authority depends on licensure, credentialing, state law, protocols and medical direction. [National scope model][scope]; [Model clinical guidelines][model].

## Body Mechanics and Injury Prevention

**Body mechanics** uses mechanical advantage to reduce strain and injury during lifting, carrying, reaching, pushing and pulling.

1. Keep weight **close to the body**: a longer reach increases stress on the back even with lighter loads.
2. Generate force with the **legs, hips and gluteal muscles**, contracting abdominal muscles to stabilize the trunk rather than lifting with the back.
3. **Stack the body:** align shoulders over hips over feet; maintain the normal lumbar curve, neutral head and normal joint alignment. Lift first, then turn the entire body; never lift and twist simultaneously.
4. Reduce lifting height and distance by repositioning, rolling or lifting in stages.

Excessive **lordosis** (swayback) stresses the lumbar region; excessive **kyphosis** (slouching) fatigues the back and increases spinal pressure. Stand or sit with ears, shoulders and hips aligned and pelvis slightly tucked. Stand with knees slightly bent; sit with weight on both ischia, feet supported and lumbar support when possible. Flexibility, cardiovascular conditioning, strength training and nutrition complement safe technique.

Use an even number of appropriately matched rescuers when possible. A mismatch in strength or height can overload either partner. The textbook recommends limiting heavy two-person carries to **1 minute or less** because fatigue increases injury risk; this is not a guaranteed safe threshold. Include oxygen, monitor, stretcher and accessories in total load calculations. [NIOSH worker safety][niosh].

### Practical lifting and handling techniques

| Technique | Essential mechanics |
| --- | --- |
| **Power lift** | Feet approximately shoulder-width apart, slightly outward and flat; squat near the load, lock the back in alignment, tighten abdominal muscles and drive through heels/arches. Upper body rises before hips. |
| **Power grip** | Full palm and finger contact, fingers wrapped around the underside and thumb around the top of the handle. Hands generally at least **10 inches** apart for balance. |
| **Squat lift** | Stagger stance, weaker leg slightly forward and flat, drive upward with stronger leg while maintaining alignment. Textbook adaptation for unilateral leg/ankle weakness; not proof an impaired rescuer can lift safely. |
| **One-handed carry** | Stagger feet, bend at hips rather than waist, limit forward trunk lean to **45°**, keep back locked and avoid compensatory sideways leaning. |
| **Reaching** | Move closer rather than reaching beyond **15–20 inches**; support upper body with free arm when possible. Avoid twisting or overhead hyperextension. For log rolls, lean from hips and use shoulder muscles. |
| **Pushing/pulling** | Prefer pushing. Keep force between hips/waist and shoulders, close to body; kneel for low pushing tasks. Avoid overhead pushing or pulling. |

Textbook fatigue estimates: **100% effort for 6 seconds; 50% effort for 1 minute**. These are instructional estimates, not universal safe-work limits. Avoid prolonged strenuous exertion and obtain help before fatigue compromises control.

## Choosing the Urgency and Method of Movement

| Move | Indication and priority |
| --- | --- |
| **Emergency** | Immediate environmental danger (fire, hazardous materials, unstable surroundings) or inability to deliver essential lifesaving care in the current location. Move to safety or gain access, then reassess. |
| **Urgent** | Immediate life threat requiring expedited movement for treatment or transport. Provide immediately achievable care and coordinate ongoing resuscitation. |
| **Nonurgent** | No immediate threat to life; deliberately plan the device, positioning, staffing and route. |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Normally address ABCs and immediate life threats before moving. A hazardous scene or inaccessible lifesaving intervention can reverse that priority. Never become another victim. Movement urgency is not synonymous with ALS need or automatic rapid transport.

### Emergency moves

The principal injury risk is aggravation of spinal column or cord injury. When feasible, maintain head and neck alignment and pull along the body's long axis; never force neutral alignment against pain, resistance or neurological deterioration.

- **Armpit-forearm drag:** support the patient's crossed forearms from behind and drag along the long axis.
- **Shirt drag:** grasp clothing at the shoulders, supporting the head on the fists; force must transfer through the armpits, not the neck. Textbook excludes a T-shirt alone.
- **Blanket drag:** a single rescuer can roll the patient onto a blanket or coat and drag from the head end while keeping the face exposed.

**ALS connection:** Escape danger rather than delaying for ECG or vascular access. Once safe, reassess consciousness, breathing, perfusion, bleeding and neurological changes. [SMR consensus][smr]; [Trauma airway guidance][traumaair].

### Urgent moves and extrication

**Rapid extrication** may be indicated by inadequate airway/breathing/oxygenation/circulation, altered mental status, signs of shock, major hemorrhage, significant head/neck/torso injuries or bilateral femur fractures. The textbook board-assisted technique coordinates manual head stabilization, thorax support, freeing the legs, controlled rotation and transfer onto a long backboard. The board may serve only as an extrication device before transfer to the cot or vacuum mattress under local SMR protocol.

**Self-extrication:** the textbook describes coached, assisted exit by a noncritically injured patient who can move and is blocking access to another patient, with head/neck alignment and a collar. A 2025 UK expert consensus supports broader patient-led self-extrication when appropriate, emphasizing reduced entrapment time rather than absolute immobility. It does not replace US local protocols. Select methods with rescue personnel according to hazards, injuries, consciousness, mobility and SMR needs. [Extrication consensus][extrication]; [SMR consensus][smr].

> [!NOTE]
> **ALS connection — critical trauma:** Maintain effective BLS ventilation and hemorrhage control; do not delay achievable BVM support for advanced airway placement. Hemorrhage reduces circulating volume and oxygen delivery; report mental status, skin signs, serial pulse/BP, bleeding and neurological findings. Authorized advanced airway, vascular access, fluid or blood therapy depends on physiology, scope and protocol; none replaces definitive hemorrhage control. [Trauma airway guidance][traumaair]; [Trauma fluids][fluid]; [Trauma blood products][blood].

**Cardiac arrest:** moving from a soft surface to a suitable firm surface can facilitate compressions, but do not postpone starting CPR. Minimize interruption, resume compressions immediately and maintain ventilation/defibrillation. The 2025 AHA guidance favors on-scene resuscitation for most out-of-hospital arrests rather than routine transport during active CPR; selected exceptions are protocol-dependent. Mechanical CPR is not routine. [2025 BLS][bls]; [2025 systems of care][system]; [2025 ALS][als].

### Nonurgent movement and transfers

Choose a safe method, move the patient as a unit and take spinal precautions if indicated. **Do not walk** a patient with chest pain, respiratory problems, lower-extremity injury, suspected spinal injury, or lightheadedness/sweating on standing. Walking is not a diagnostic stress test; support and reassess symptomatic patients.

| Transfer | Purpose and key limitation |
| --- | --- |
| **Direct ground lift** | Two or three rescuers on the same side support the body, lift onto their knees, curl patient toward their chests, then stand together. Not recommended for heavier patients. |
| **Extremity lift** | Two rescuers support the patient under the arms (grasping wrists) and knees. **Contraindicated in suspected spinal or extremity injury.** |
| **Direct carry** | Bed-to-cot transfer with cot perpendicular to bed, head end at foot of bed; rescuers cradle, lift close to chest, rotate together and lower. |
| **Draw sheet** | Cot alongside bed; use the sheet to distribute support and slide patient across, bracing trunk and avoiding excessive reach. |

These are technique-selection principles, not complete skill instructions. Use taught procedures and adequate personnel. Before transfers, evaluate injury location, pain and positional tolerance; protect attached equipment. ALS analgesia may improve tolerance, but opioids such as fentanyl can depress ventilation, cause sedation or hypotension. Reassess pain, breathing, alertness and perfusion. [Prehospital pain guidelines][pain].

## Packaging, Monitoring and Reassessment

**Packaging** means selecting/preparing the carrying device, transferring and securing the patient, then moving to and loading the ambulance. Check device locks, use appropriate straps, tuck loose ends, maintain warmth and secure both patient and device before vehicle movement. Never leave a patient unattended on a wheeled stretcher. Recheck securement after each transfer and apply indicated spinal motion restriction (SMR).

**Before movement:** assess airway, respiratory effort, SpO₂, pulse, BP, mental status and relevant pain/neurological findings. Confirm oxygen and battery supply, approved equipment mounts, safe routing and slack of tubing/leads, and assigned clinical roles.

**During/after movement:** maintain treatment, announce deterioration, coordinate a safe stop if necessary, repeat assessment and confirm that equipment, airway support, lines and restraints remain functional and secure.

### ALS connection: Interpreting monitoring during movement

| Equipment | Information and limitations |
| --- | --- |
| Pulse oximeter | Estimates oxygen saturation, not ventilation; motion, cold and poor perfusion impair reliability. Supplemental oxygen can mask hypoventilation. |
| ECG | Electrical activity, not proof of a pulse; movement may create artifact. |
| Waveform capnography | Exhaled CO₂ trends reflect ventilation, circulation and metabolism; can help identify airway displacement or ventilation loss. |
| IV/IO and infusion equipment | Delivers authorized fluids/medications; dislodgement, kinking or interruption can compromise treatment. |

Compare readings with the actual patient. Report changes and assist within scope; do not independently alter advanced airways, ventilator settings or infusions without authorization. [Airway physiology][airway]; [Mechanical ventilation][vent]; [Model clinical guidelines][model].

## Patient Positioning and Physiological Tolerance

Position affects venous return, breathing mechanics, aspiration risk and injury tolerance.

| Condition | Position or consideration |
| --- | --- |
| General | Supine or sitting according to condition. |
| Unresponsive, breathing adequately, no suspected spinal injury | Left lateral recovery position, facing rescuer; facilitates drainage but does not guarantee airway protection. |
| Chest pain or dyspnea | Position of comfort, commonly upright, provided hypotension is absent. |
| Shock/hypoperfusion | Supine unless protocol or clinical circumstances dictate otherwise. |
| Suspected spinal injury | Appropriate SMR on cot, vacuum mattress or board as indicated; board may be for extrication only. |
| Alert and vomiting/nauseated | Sitting or recovery position allowing airway management. |
| Pregnancy after 20 weeks | Left side or elevate right hip when supine to relieve vena cava compression; follow obstetric protocol for suspected cord prolapse (textbook: supine with hips elevated). |

> [!WARNING]
> Recovery positioning is for appropriately breathing patients; absent or inadequate breathing requires resuscitation or assisted ventilation. Suspected spinal injury does not override airway safety. [2024 first aid][firstaid]; [2025 BLS][bls].

**ALS connection:** Upright posture can improve respiratory mechanics; pulmonary edema may worsen when supine. CPAP can improve gas exchange and reduce work of breathing but may reduce venous return; hypotension, vomiting, exhaustion or inability to protect the airway can make it unsuitable. CPAP may be BLS-authorized. Orthopnea suggests a positional respiratory problem, not a specific diagnosis. Monitor effort, alertness, perfusion and saturation. [Noninvasive ventilation][niv].

### Special populations

- **Pregnancy:** uterine compression of the inferior vena cava reduces venous return, cardiac output and BP (supine hypotensive syndrome). In cardiac arrest with fundus at/above umbilicus, the 2025 AHA guidance calls for manual leftward uterine displacement alongside CPR, rather than compromising compressions with side-lying. [2025 special circumstances][special].
- **Infants and toddlers:** use approved size/weight-appropriate ambulance restraints and permitted mounting configurations. The textbook's personal car-seat and rolled-padding technique is not blanket permission to improvise. Never transport unsecured or in an adult's arms. [2025 pediatric transport guidance][pediatric].
- **Older adults and disabilities:** osteoporosis increases fracture risk; establish baseline function, explain movement, pad/support deformities and respect comfortable achievable positioning. Do not force fixed joints into textbook alignment.

## Patient-Carrying Devices and Equipment Selection

Select by route, patient condition, load, staffing and ability to maintain treatment. The following capacities are **textbook examples**, not universal specifications; verify the actual model's rating, configuration, locks and approved operating procedures.

| Device | Principal use and limitations |
| --- | --- |
| **Wheeled stretcher** | Preferred for routine transport; adjustable positioning and rolling reduce carrying. Many accommodate up to **650 lb**. Manual roll-in models weigh **70–95 lb**; powered models up to **140 lb**, reducing repetitive manual spinal loading but increasing device weight. Uneven ground and stairs may require extra personnel or another device. |
| **Bariatric stretcher** | Wider, reinforced platform and larger wheels; some support **1,600 lb** wheels-down. Ramp/winch or dedicated vehicles may be needed. Confirm configuration-specific combined load. |
| **Portable stretcher / pole litter** | Lightweight, compact alternative for confined spaces or multiple patients; must generally be carried. Basic portable model rated up to **350 lb** in textbook (carrying that load discouraged). A pole litter requires locked crosspieces, preferably four or more rescuers and a board if spinal immobilization is needed. |
| **Stair chair** | Narrow corridors, elevators and stairs when seated positioning is safe. Tracked models reduce lifting during descent; still require two stabilizing rescuers. Some powered models rated **500 lb**. |
| **Long backboard** | Rigid lifting/extrication surface; lightweight, useful on rough terrain and for transfers. Prolonged use can cause pain, pressure injury, impaired breathing and heat loss. |
| **Vacuum mattress** | Air is removed to mold a rigid support around the patient, filling voids and providing SMR; can fit in a basket stretcher. |
| **Scoop stretcher** | Separates into halves assembled around the patient; useful in confined spaces and pelvic or bilateral femur fractures. Requires access from both sides, at least two rescuers, secure latches and padding. Example capacity **300 lb**. |
| **Basket (Stokes) stretcher** | Durable enclosure for rough terrain or specialized rescue; can accommodate other devices and flotation equipment. Bulky, requires carrying and padding/insulation. Rope or ladder rescue requires specific training. |
| **Flexible (Reeves) stretcher** | Flexible fabric, reinforcing structure, straps and multiple handles for restricted spaces; can accommodate a patient on a board. Must be carried. |

> [!CAUTION]
> **Spinal motion restriction is not absolute immobilization.** The textbook discourages scoop stretchers for suspected spinal injury, but the 2018 joint SMR consensus recognizes them as a transfer option when minimizing motion. This does not endorse every model or prolonged scoop transport. Use local criteria, device instructions and patient tolerance. Reassess neurological status and breathing after transfers. [SMR consensus][smr].

### Wheeled stretcher handling

On smooth ground the head-end rescuer pushes and the foot-end rescuer guides; four rescuers stabilize the stretcher on rough terrain. Manual loading requires securely supporting the head-end loading wheels before releasing/collapsing the carriage; verify front and rear locks. Powered loading reduces manual lifting but does not eliminate tip, pinch, battery or locking hazards. Load hanging stretchers before wheeled stretchers where applicable. Follow actual model instructions rather than assuming automatic engagement or a universal indicator. [Powered stretcher study][power]; [NIOSH worker safety][niosh].

### Stair chair suitability

> [!WARNING]
> Do not use the textbook stair-chair technique for altered mental status, suspected spinal injury or lower-extremity injury. Severe respiratory fatigue, vomiting, poor perfusion or a need for continuous BVM ventilation may also make seated movement unsafe.

A cooperative, alert patient with orthopnea may tolerate a seated move better than lying flat. Reassess before entering stairs; ensure oxygen, CPAP or other necessary respiratory support can be maintained safely, or select another method. Explain the tilt, verify straps, keep patient hands inside, coordinate head- and foot-end rescuers and use a spotter when available. Manual chairs are carried without letting wheels strike steps; tracked chairs require model-specific operation. [Noninvasive ventilation][niv].

**ALS connection:** Larger patients may have reduced respiratory reserve, especially supine. Plan staffing, equipment and positioning early, assessing respiratory effort, consciousness and perfusion rather than assuming tolerance based on size. [2024 first aid][firstaid]; [2025 BLS][bls].

## Confined Spaces, Stairs and Carrying Configurations

Use a rolling device whenever possible; otherwise choose a portable, flexible or rigid carrying device compatible with the route and condition.

- For the textbook supine carry, secure with at least **three straps**, preventing sliding in either direction; keep hands safely inside the device. Follow actual restraint/device instructions; this is not authorization for improvised behavioral restraint.
- **Downhill/downstairs: feet first. Uphill/upstairs: head first.** Follow approved carry orientation for the device. Use spotters to guide backward-moving rescuers, count steps and identify obstacles.
- **Two-person carry:** stronger rescuer at head (greater load), partner at foot; backward walking, lateral instability and fatigue increase risk.
- **Four-person carry:** diamond configuration (head, foot and one on each side) or rectangular configuration (one at each corner) distributes load and improves stability.
- Maintain load close to body, continuous communication and coordinated pace. Clear obstacles, snow and ice before carrying; seek trained assistance for unstable, high-angle or other specialized conditions.

**ALS connection:** Ensure the route permits essential clinical care, including assisted ventilation. If necessary, use another device or staged movement with safe reassessment points. [NIOSH worker safety][niosh]; [Model clinical guidelines][model].

**Routine dialysis transfer:** stable baseline patients may need only BLS support, warmth, safe positioning and reassessment. Missed dialysis plus new dyspnea, weakness, palpitations or near-syncope warrants reassessment; fluid overload or hyperkalemia may be involved, but symptoms are nonspecific and a normal ECG does not exclude hyperkalemia. Report last dialysis, baseline function, symptom changes and vital trends; protect dialysis access from compression/trauma and follow access-arm BP restrictions. [2025 special circumstances][special].

## Air Medical Transport

Air transport selection depends on expected clinical benefit, time to appropriate care, risks and resource capabilities, not distance or severity alone. Coordinate early with flight personnel. [Air medical utilization consensus][airmedical].

- Decontaminate hazardous-material exposure according to flight-team requirements; confined aircraft cabins amplify contamination hazards.
- Confirm patient restraint and board/cot compatibility, secure equipment and loose clothing, explain noise and rotor wash, protect eyes, ears and exposed wounds, and coordinate landing-zone preparation with the crew.
- Approach only when instructed by pilot/crew; stay clear of the tail rotor, obey aircraft-specific procedures and minimize personnel near rotors. Let flight personnel direct loading.
- Maintain ventilation and airway access; ensure the chest remains accessible for assessment when intubated. Communicate treatments, oxygen needs, injuries, response and recent changes. Secure lines and infusions according to flight-team instructions.

> [!CAUTION]
> **ALS connection:** Endotracheal intubation is not a universal prerequisite for flight. Effective BLS ventilation or an alternative airway may be preferable if intubation would delay care or destabilize physiology. The textbook suggestion to lay IV bags on the patient is not universally safe for gravity- or pump-dependent infusions. Follow authorized clinical and flight-team direction. [Trauma airway guidance][traumaair]; [Mechanical ventilation][vent].

## Neonatal Transport

A **neonatal isolette** provides a warm controlled environment and an approved equipment platform for interfacility transport. Newborns have high surface area relative to body mass, and preterm infants have limited thermal reserves. Cold stress increases metabolic demand and vulnerability to hypoglycemia and inadequate oxygen delivery. Prevent both cooling and overheating; secure the isolette using approved ambulance mounts or loading equipment.

**ALS connection:** Specialty teams may manage temperature, oxygen, ventilation, glucose and IV infusions. These tasks are not automatically authorized even for field paramedics. EMTs assist with route preparation, securement, supplies and reporting changes; do not alter isolette settings or disconnect/adjust clinical equipment without authorization and training. The 2025 AHA/AAP neonatal guideline addresses stabilization/resuscitation after birth, not a complete isolette transfer protocol. [2025 neonatal resuscitation][neonatal].

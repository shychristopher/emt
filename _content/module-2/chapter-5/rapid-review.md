# Chapter 5: Communication — TL;DR

## Communication in the EMS System

Communication connects public access, dispatch, field response, patient assessment, medical direction, receiving-facility preparation and transfer of care. System communication coordinates resources and clinical decisions; therapeutic communication builds trust, improves the accuracy of assessment and supports patient cooperation.

The **EMS Act of 1973 (Public Law 93-154)** recognized communications as one of 15 essential EMS system components, supporting public access, immediate dispatch and medical oversight. Modern systems supplement narrowband voice radio with electronic patient records, ECG transmission and broadband services. **FirstNet**, established by legislation in 2012, provides a nationwide public safety broadband network. Mobile integrated health care (MIH) and community paramedicine also require communication systems capable of supporting scheduled and unscheduled community care.

## Communication Infrastructure

| Equipment | Function and limitations |
| --- | --- |
| Base station | Dispatch and coordination hub; typically 80–150 W. Elevated antennas improve coverage. |
| Mobile radio | Vehicle-mounted; typically 20–50 W, with an average range of 10–15 miles on average terrain. |
| Portable radio | Handheld; typically 1–5 W, with limited range. Allows communication away from the ambulance. |
| Repeater | Receives a signal and rebroadcasts it at a different frequency and higher power to extend coverage. |
| Digital equipment | Encoders convert signals into digital codes; decoders recognize the intended codes. Supports efficient use of crowded frequencies. |
| Mobile data terminal (MDT) | Receives dispatch details and transmits unit status; supplements rather than replaces voice communication. |
| Cellular phone | Supports voice, images and data; useful as backup but vulnerable to dead spots and network congestion during disasters. |
| Satellite communication | Alternative for remote regions with inadequate terrestrial radio infrastructure. |

Land mobile radio systems commonly use VHF, UHF or 700–800 MHz frequencies to connect dispatch, vehicles and medical facilities. Actual radio coverage depends on terrain, buildings, antenna placement and transmitter power.

**Telemetry** is automated transmission of patient data, including vital signs and ECGs. **Telemedicine** connects patients and EMS providers with remote clinicians to support assessment, triage and, in eligible programs, treatment without conventional hospital transport.

> [!CAUTION]
> The historical federal Emergency Triage, Treat and Transport (ET3) model operated from January 1, 2021, through December 31, 2023. It is not an ongoing nationwide Medicare entitlement for treatment in place. Local programs may have independent eligibility, consent, documentation and escalation requirements. [CMS][5]

### ALS Connection: Clinical Data Transmission

A transmitted 12-lead ECG can support early recognition of myocardial ischemia and cardiac-team preparation. A nondiagnostic initial tracing does not exclude acute coronary syndrome (ACS); persistent or worsening symptoms may warrant repeat tracings. Authorized EMTs may acquire and transmit ECGs, while independent advanced interpretation and treatment selection are distinct responsibilities. [1][1] [3][3]

| Monitoring tool | What to communicate |
| --- | --- |
| 12-lead ECG | Symptoms, onset, tracing time and quality, and changes. |
| Continuous ECG | Electrical rate/rhythm and discrepancies with the palpable pulse. |
| Waveform capnography | Exhaled CO₂, ventilation-related changes and waveform quality. |
| Pulse oximetry | Estimated oxygen saturation, signal quality and breathing effort. |
| Noninvasive BP | Pressure trends, measurement method and inconsistent readings. |

Electrical activity does not establish a perfusing pulse. EtCO₂ reflects ventilation, pulmonary blood flow and metabolism; low values alone do not identify the cause. Motion, cold extremities and poor circulation may impair readings. Assess the patient first; communicate measurements, times, clinical context, trends and uncertainty rather than inventing values. [1][1] [2][2] [4][4]

## Regulation and Communication Readiness

The **Federal Communications Commission (FCC)** regulates radio licensing, call signs, frequencies, approved equipment, transmission power and interference; obscenity and profanity are prohibited on broadcasts.

Protect and clean equipment, periodically check frequency accuracy, inspect battery-powered devices daily, maintain fully charged spares and plan for equipment or power failure. Backup generators, alternate approved channels and cellular phones can maintain system communication.

> [!NOTE]
> Mixed-level crews should assign responsibility for dispatch contact, ECG transmission, medical orders and event times before the call. If communication fails, continue patient care under authorized standing protocols and use approved alternatives. Silence is not approval for an intervention requiring an order.

## Radio, Telephone and Timekeeping Practices

### Radio Technique

1. Select the correct frequency, reduce background noise and listen before transmitting.
2. Press the push-to-talk (PTT) button and wait approximately **1 second** before speaking; hold the microphone about **2–3 inches** from the mouth.
3. Identify the called unit, then your own unit; wait for permission to proceed.
4. Speak clearly, calmly and concisely in plain language. Pause during transmissions longer than **30 seconds** to allow emergency traffic.
5. Clarify potentially confused numbers (e.g., “thirteen, one-three”). Report observable facts and clearly identified clinical impressions rather than unconfirmed diagnoses.
6. Record important information and repeat received orders word for word. Use “affirmative” and “negative” when appropriate; end with “over” and await acknowledgment.

Common terms: **copy** (received and understood), **stand by** (wait), **go ahead** (proceed), **break** (pause), **over** (awaiting reply), **clear** (end of transmission), **ETA** (estimated time of arrival), and **say again** (repeat). Codes such as 10-4 shorten airtime but can cause misunderstanding when agencies use different systems; plain English is generally more interoperable. Codes are not a confidentiality safeguard.

Phone reports follow the same principles. Know important contact numbers, coverage limitations and the backup plan if a call fails.

### Closed-Loop Communication, Privacy and Time

**Closed-loop communication:** sender gives an instruction → receiver repeats or confirms it → sender verifies or corrects it → receiver reports completion and results. Use this both over the radio and within the crew. [6][6]

Avoid patient names over open radio and unnecessary identifiable details. Use approved channels and reasonable safeguards. HIPAA permits treatment-relevant disclosure to receiving clinicians without separate authorization, not indiscriminate disclosure to unrelated parties. Cellular and radio security varies by system. [7][7]

Use accurate, synchronized clocks and 24-hour time: 1427 = 2:27 PM; 0030 = 12:30 AM. Midnight may be recorded as 0000 at the start of a date or 2400 at the preceding date's end where accepted. Distinguish symptom onset, last known well, contact, interventions, reassessment and transfer times. Label unknown or estimated times accurately. [2][2]

## Team Coordination, Dispatch and Scene Communication

### Scene Leadership and Scope

Coordinate respectfully with fire, rescue, police and other clinicians. For an alert patient without immediate threats, a brief report from first responders (generally no more than one minute) may precede direct contact. If the patient is unresponsive or has immediate life threats, proceed directly to the patient when safe and collect history while providing care. Avoid authority disputes that interfere with patient care.

- **EMT:** Foundational assessment, noninvasive resuscitation and authorized medications.
- **AEMT:** Adds selected vascular access, fluids and medications.
- **Paramedic:** Broader advanced assessment, ECG interpretation, airway, medication and electrical therapy capabilities.

The national scope model is a consensus framework, not a license; state law, credentialing, training, agency policy and medical direction determine actual authorization. Decline unauthorized tasks and offer an authorized alternative. [1][1]

**Crew resource management** requires shared priorities, explicit task assignments, continued BLS during advanced care, reassessment and timely communication of changes. Use CUS to escalate concerns: **Concerned → Uncomfortable → Safety issue**. Repeat an overlooked safety concern and escalate appropriately. [10][10]

### Dispatch Communication

Emergency medical dispatchers (EMDs) obtain information, direct resources and provide caller instructions. Dispatch communications and timestamps may become part of the legal record.

The usual dispatch sequence is:

1. Acknowledge receipt of the assignment.
2. Report en route status.
3. Give scene ETA and report delays or unusual road conditions.
4. Report arrival; request resources and update dispatch during prolonged scene times.
5. Report departure, destination, hospital ETA and multiple patients when applicable.
6. Report arrival at the hospital or rendezvous point.
7. Report clear/available after transfer.
8. Report departure from the hospital for the station.
9. Report arrival back at the station.

Advanced Automatic Collision Notification (AACN) may supply crash location, force direction, change in velocity, rollover and related data. Such information helps anticipate severity and resources but does not replace patient assessment or locally adopted trauma destination criteria. The 2021 national field-triage guideline, published in 2022, supersedes the older CDC-era guidance. [8][8]

**ALS connection:** Request resources early using the patient's problem, severity and practical needs. An ALS intercept should consider expected benefit, transport time and access to definitive care; do not hold a deteriorating patient solely to obtain ALS. Dispatch information is a starting point, not a diagnosis. [2][2]

## Therapeutic Communication and Patient Interviewing

### Communication Process

The sender **encodes** thoughts into verbal or nonverbal messages; the receiver **decodes** them through personal experiences, perceptions and cultural context. Feedback helps both parties verify whether the intended meaning was understood.

Build rapport through the **three Cs: competence, confidence and compassion**. Introduce yourself and the team, address the patient respectfully, obtain consent, explain care before performing it, offer choices when possible and maintain privacy. Use calm, plain language, active listening and sufficient time for answers. Reduce environmental distractions and maintain scene awareness and a safe exit.

| Technique | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Facilitation | Encourage the patient to continue speaking. |
| Clarification | Resolve unclear or ambiguous information. |
| Summary | Restate the interpreted message and confirm accuracy. |
| Explanation | Describe care and expectations in understandable language. |
| Silence | Allow time to think and respond; adapt to emotional needs. |
| Reflection | Return the patient's expressed feelings to demonstrate understanding. |
| Empathy | Recognize and respect the patient's emotional experience. |
| Confrontation | Address discrepancies assertively and respectfully, without aggression. |

> [!WARNING]
> Historical **facilitated communication**, involving physical guidance of letter selection, is discredited because the facilitator may author the message. Do not use it to establish symptoms, consent, refusal or preferences. Legitimate augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) enables the patient's independent responses. [ASHA, 2018][11]

### Verbal and Nonverbal Assessment

Open posture, a relaxed expression and appropriate eye-level positioning can establish trust. Eye contact, gestures, touch and interpersonal distance vary by culture and individual preference. Ask before entering intimate space or using touch when reluctance is present.

Approximate spatial zones described in the source: intimate (under 1.5 ft), personal (1.5–4 ft), social (4–12 ft) and public (12 ft or more). Adapt to patient comfort and scene safety.

Nonverbal behavior provides clues, not proof of deception, incapacity or aggression. Pain, neurologic changes, fear, disability and cultural differences affect expression. Use nonthreatening posture, distance and a clear exit with agitated patients; new difficulty speaking or following instructions may indicate clinical deterioration. [2][2]

### Interviewing and Clinical History

- Begin with **open-ended questions** to obtain the patient's account; use **closed-ended questions** to clarify details, gather facts quickly or focus an overly lengthy response.
- Ask one question at a time; avoid interruptions, leading questions, blame, jargon, false reassurance and unsupported diagnoses.
- Use SAMPLE and OPQRST to organize history. Communicate medication amounts/times, allergies, symptom onset, last known well, relevant bleeding and differences from baseline when indicated. Distinguish patient-reported information from verified observations.
- A capable adult makes their own informed decisions despite family preferences. Follow applicable consent, capacity and refusal procedures for children or patients lacking capacity; involve medical direction as indicated.
- If hostility makes the scene unsafe, do not approach; obtain appropriate assistance and follow agency restraint procedures. Once safe, communicate calmly and establish acceptable conduct.

> [!TIP]
> In high-acuity encounters, gather essential history while another crew member addresses immediate threats. Do not postpone lifesaving care or necessary transport to complete the interview. Use teach-back when appropriate: ask patients to explain what they understood in their own words. [6][6]

## Communication Adaptations and Family Involvement

Avoid **ethnocentrism** (treating one's own cultural practices as superior) and cultural imposition. Ask about patient preferences rather than assuming them.

- **Language or sensory barriers:** Speak directly to the patient; use qualified interpreters, approved language services or suitable communication aids. Family/bystander interpretation is not the default; applicable federal rules restrict it, with limited emergency exceptions. Writing may be insufficient for complex consent. Document the communication method and limitations; do not delay immediate lifesaving care. [12][12] [13][13]
- **Older adults:** Do not assume hearing loss or cognitive impairment. Provide glasses or hearing aids when available, keep lips visible, adjust speech to need and allow response time without infantilizing the patient.
- **Children:** Approach at eye level, use simple truthful language and involve calm caregivers. Family-centered care incorporates caregivers' knowledge of usual development, behavior, conditions, devices and individualized care plans. Include the patient as much as possible and distinguish caregiver reports from your observations. [2][2] [6][6]

## Clinical Reporting, Medical Direction and Transfer of Care

Medical direction provides patient-specific authorization, procedural guidance and triage decisions when needed. Prearrival notification enables the hospital to prepare personnel, equipment and specialty teams.

### Shared Patient Report

Include unit identification and service level (BLS/ALS); age and sex if known; chief complaint; needed specialty services; pertinent history, scene and mechanism; major past illnesses; mental status; baseline vital signs and exam findings; care given and response; current condition; further orders requested when indicated; and ETA. Update the receiving facility when the patient's condition changes.

**SBAR** provides a predictable structure for orders, notifications and handoffs:

| Component | Information |
| --- | --- |
| Situation | Immediate problem and reason for communication. |
| Background | Relevant history, onset and care already provided. |
| Assessment | Subjective/objective findings, severity and trends. |
| Recommendation | Requested action, resources or further orders. |

For stroke alerts, communicate onset/last known well, neurologic deficits and relevant glucose; for cardiac alerts, symptoms, available ECG and hemodynamic status; for trauma alerts, mechanism, injuries, physiologic trends and interventions. Follow local activation criteria. [2][2] [3][3] [8][8]

### Medical Orders and ALS Medication Safety

Provide relevant history, allergies, prior medications, findings and trends. Record orders and repeat them **word for word**, including medication, dose/units, route, timing and restrictions; obtain confirmation. Clarify unclear or potentially inappropriate instructions, including orders to withhold treatment. Document actual intervention, time and response, and promptly report adverse changes.

Standing protocols authorize specified actions without online consultation; online direction supplies patient-specific guidance. Neither overrides legal scope or individual authorization. [1][1]

> [!NOTE]
> **ALS connection — Suspected ACS:** Aspirin reduces platelet aggregation; nitroglycerin dilates vessels and reduces cardiac workload but may affect BP and interact with certain medications. Report doses, times, allergies, bleeding history and relevant interacting medications. Pain relief does not establish a diagnosis. Oxygen addresses inadequate oxygenation, not the coronary blockage itself; the 2025 ACS guideline does not support routine oxygen for adequately oxygenated patients. Historical SBAR medication examples are not standing orders. Follow authorized protocols. [3][3]

### Recognizing and Reporting Deterioration

In the source's abdominal-pain scenario, BP falls from **88/64 to 82/62 mmHg**, HR rises from **128 to 134/min**, and RR rises from **24 to 26/min**. The patient becomes less responsive, paler and more weakly perfused. Initial room-air SpO₂ is 96%; later the device returns an error.

These combined trends suggest worsening circulatory shock, not a confirmed cause. Oxygen delivery depends on blood flow and hemoglobin as well as oxygen saturation. Poor perfusion may impair oximeter readings; an error is not a measured saturation or proof of hypoxemia. Continue primary assessment, indicated authorized BLS support, heat-loss prevention and frequent reassessment; expedite transport and immediately update the receiving facility. Coordinate ALS without delaying definitive care. Vascular access or fluids may be considered by authorized advanced clinicians when appropriate; fluids do not control hemorrhage and are not appropriate for every shock state. [1][1] [2][2] [4][4]

### Handoff and Continuity of Care

Transfer responsibility and information to an identified, available receiving provider who can ask questions. Give an oral report with current condition, initial and repeat findings, history, medications and allergies, interventions with times, responses and ongoing support or outstanding concerns. Distinguish personal observations from witness reports. Provide the PCR to the receiving facility or field provider as applicable. A structured verbal report does not replace documentation. [6][6] [9][9]

**Handoff memory aid:** problem → baseline → care → trend → outstanding concern.

Continue assigned authorized care until transfer is accepted; do not interrupt advanced therapy unless appropriately directed and continuity is maintained. Stable reassessments and controlled bleeding, as in the source's hand-injury example, may support BLS management; preserved distal movement and sensation do not rule out deeper injury. Report mechanism, distal findings and any worsening perfusion or recurrent bleeding.

> [!CAUTION]
> The historical hand-injury scenario describes transport on a crew jump seat. This is not authorization to use a crew seat for patient transport. Use an approved patient transport position and restraint system; handle cot refusal through applicable consent/refusal procedures.

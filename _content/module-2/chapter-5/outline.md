## 1. Evolution, Components & Regulation of EMS Communications

### Historical Evolution & Modern EMS Communications
- **Early Era vs. EMS Act of 1973:**
  - Early ambulance crews only transported patients without providing emergency care or needing advanced communications.
  - The **EMS Act of 1973 (Public Law 93-154)** designated communications as one of the **15 essential components** of an EMS system—alongside public access, immediate dispatch response, and medical oversight—and provided grant funding for Very High Frequency (VHF) and Ultra High Frequency (UHF) radios.
  - Narrowband voice radio remains widely used in many EMS systems today.
- **Modern Technological Advancements:**
  - Field-to-hospital and field-to-medical-direction data transmission.
  - Longitudinal electronic medical records (EMRs) transmitting data to the field via National EMS Information System (NEMSIS) electronic patient care report (PCR) systems.
  - Transmission of 12-lead electrocardiograms (ECGs) to medical facilities or medical direction.
  - Expanded communications supporting mobile integrated health care (MIH) and community paramedicine across emergency, unscheduled, and scheduled community health services.
- **FirstNet Broadband Network:**
  - Created under the **First Responder Network Authority (FirstNet)** via the **Middle Class Tax Relief and Job Creation Act (Public Law 112-96)**, signed in **2012**.
  - Nationwide public safety broadband network comparable to major cellular networks, reserved specifically for public safety including EMS.
- **Operational & Clinical Value:**
  - Enables faster patient access, hospital preparation, resource requests, direct medical direction links, and 12-lead ECG transmission to support lifesaving prehospital care.
  - Effective system communication must be paired with **therapeutic communication** to build positive patient relationships and adapt to patients with special needs.

### Components of an Emergency Communications System
- **Base Station:**
  - Serves as the central dispatch and coordination area and ideally maintains contact with all other elements of the EMS system.
  - Best located on suitable terrain—preferably a hill—and near the hospital serving as a medical command center.
  - Typically uses relatively high-power output (**80–150 watts**) paired with a suitable antenna within a short distance.
  - Antenna quality is critical to transmission and reception efficiency; Federal Communications Commission (FCC) rules limit transmission power output, while man-made noise limits minimum usable reception signal levels (a good antenna system helps offset these limits).
  - Newer systems also incorporate cellular and Internet-based communication systems.
- **Land Mobile Radio Systems (LMRS):**
  - Usually UHF, VHF, or **700- to 800-MHz** systems.
  - Enable dispatch base-to-vehicle (mobile and portable radios), vehicle-to-vehicle, vehicle-to-medical facility, and medical facility-to-medical facility communications.
- **Mobile Radios (Transmitter/Receivers):**
  - Vehicle-mounted devices used to communicate within the EMS system.
  - Usually transmit effectively at lower power than base stations—typically **20–50 watts**.
  - Most transmit over a **10- to 15-mile** range on average terrain, though distance depends on terrain and decreases in mountainous areas or areas with many tall buildings.
  - Higher-output mobile transmitters yield proportionally greater transmission ranges than lower-output units.
- **Portable Radios (Transmitter/Receivers):**
  - Handheld transmitter/receivers useful when out of the vehicle or in the patient compartment to stay in contact with base, crewmates, and medical direction.
  - Can also be used by medical direction when stationed at a hospital without a dedicated radio.
  - Typically have lower power outputs (**1 to 5 watts**) and limited range, though signals can be boosted by rebroadcast through a repeater.
- **Repeaters:**
  - Receive transmissions from lower-powered sources (such as mobile or portable radios) and rebroadcast them at another frequency and higher power.
  - Enable communications across wide coverage areas or difficult terrain where direct transmission/reception is challenging.
  - May be located in emergency vehicles or at fixed sites across the EMS service area.
- **Digital Equipment:**
  - **Encoder** breaks down sound waves into unique digital codes, while a **decoder** recognizes and responds only to those codes—allowing different mobile and base stations to share the same broadcast frequency and increasing message capacity on crowded channels.
  - **Mobile Data Terminal (MDT)** mounted in the ambulance cab receives digital radio signals to display (and sometimes print) dispatch information instead of voice dispatch.
  - Allows EMTs to acknowledge or update status—en route, on scene, en route to hospital, at hospital, or back in service—by pressing a designated button or using voice, facilitating rather than replacing voice communication.
- **Cellular (Wireless) Phones:**
  - Transmit and receive wirelessly across a service area divided into slightly overlapping geographical cells (**2 to 40 square miles** each) acting like radio base stations, automatically handing off calls between cells.
  - *Advantages:* Excellent sound quality, channel availability, easy maintenance, often greater privacy, and smartphone capabilities (sending scene images, accessing/sending data files, searching the Internet); useful as a radio backup or where radio infrastructure is cost-prohibitive.
  - *Disadvantage:* Relies on the public telephone system, which can easily become overwhelmed during multiple-casualty disasters.
- **Telemetry:**
  - Transmits data via an automated communications process—most often wireless data transfer.
  - Historically focused on ECG transmission; now also transmits vital signs, 12-lead ECGs, and more advanced patient data.
- **Telemedicine (Telehealth):**
  - Commonly used in mobile integrated health care (MIH) for non-urgent or non-emergency prehospital care, connecting EMS providers and patients remotely with health care practitioners for advanced assessment and care.
  - Supports MIH triage and remote decisions on treating without transport—helping ease emergency department overcrowding (expanded notably during the COVID-19 pandemic).
  - Under the Emergency Triage, Treat and Transport (**ET3**) model announced in early 2019 by HHS, participating EMS agencies can partner with qualified health care providers for on-scene or telehealth treatment and transport to alternative destinations (such as primary care offices or urgent-care clinics).
- **Land Mobile Satellite Communications:**
  - Alternative to radio communications in sparsely populated, remote rural areas where EMS radio is nonexistent or prohibitively expensive.

### Broadcast Regulations (FCC) & System Maintenance
- **Federal Communications Commission (FCC) Jurisdiction:**
  - Licenses individual base station operations and assigns radio call signs.
  - Approves equipment for use, sets transmitter power output limits, assigns radio frequencies, and monitors field operations.
  - Enforces rules limiting interference with emergency radio broadcasts and prohibiting obscenity and profanity on the air.
- **System Maintenance & Backups:**
  - Protect equipment from mishandling or unnecessary exposure to harsh environmental conditions; clean regularly with a damp cloth and mild detergent.
  - Check equipment periodically to verify it has not drifted from its assigned frequency.
  - Inspect battery-powered mobile radios, portable radios, and phones daily—inserting freshly charged rechargeable batteries each day, placing used batteries in the recharger, and keeping a backup set of fully charged batteries on hand.
  - Maintain system backups—such as emergency generators at base and repeater stations during power outages or cellular phones for radio failures—and stay open to evolving communication technologies.

## 2. Communicating Within the EMS System

### Standard Progression of Radio Transmissions
1. EMS dispatch transmits the call to the EMS unit.
2. EMS unit acknowledges the call was received.
3. EMS unit notifies dispatch that they are en route.
4. EMS unit notifies other agencies if necessary (such as medical control or local hospital).
5. EMS unit notifies dispatch that they are at the scene.
6. EMS unit contacts medical direction to report the patient's condition and seek orders.
7. EMS unit notifies dispatch that they are leaving the scene.
8. EMS unit contacts the receiving hospital and gives the patient report.
9. EMS unit notifies dispatch of arrival at the hospital.
10. EMS unit notifies dispatch upon leaving the hospital.
11. EMS unit notifies dispatch upon arrival back at the station.

### Ground Rules for Radio Communication (16 Rules)
1. Turn on the radio, select the correct frequency, use EMS frequencies only for EMS communication, and reduce background noise by closing vehicle windows.
2. Listen before transmitting to ensure the channel is clear and avoid interrupting another transmission; adjust the radio to a proper volume.
3. Push the "press to talk" (PTT) button and wait **1 second** before speaking so system repeaters have time to operate and the start of your message is not cut off.
4. Hold the microphone about **2 to 3 inches** from your lips and speak clearly, calmly, and slowly in a monotone voice so you can be completely understood.
5. Address the unit being called by its name and number, then identify your unit by name.
6. Wait for the called unit to signal you to begin (such as saying "go ahead"); if they respond with "stand by," wait to transmit until further notified.
7. Keep transmissions brief; if a transmission takes more than **30 seconds**, pause for a few seconds to let other units make emergency transmissions.
8. Keep transmissions organized and to the point using plain English and clear text; avoid slang, meaningless phrases (such as "Be advised"), excessive politeness ("Please," "Thank you," or "You're welcome" since courtesy is assumed), and codes or agency-specific terms unless accepted in your system's communications.
9. When transmitting a number that could be confused with another (such as `13` vs. `30`), say the number ("thirteen") followed by its individual digits ("one-three").
10. Avoid offering a diagnosis; remain impartial and state only objective information (observable, verifiable facts such as vital signs, chief complaint, and medications the patient has) and relevant subjective information (opinions or judgments such as "The patient appears to be in extreme pain") gathered during assessment.
11. When receiving orders or information from dispatch, medical direction, or other medical personnel, use the "echo" method or closed-loop communication by immediately repeating the order word for word to confirm accuracy and understanding.
12. Always write down important information received from other parts of the EMS system, such as addresses or medication-assistance orders.
13. Because airwaves are public and scanners can pick up radio and cellular messages, protect patient privacy by not using the patient's name; use objective, impartial language; avoid personalized comments about the patient or condition (which could be grounds for slander); and never use profanity on the air.
14. Use "we" instead of "I," since an EMT rarely acts alone.
15. Use "affirmative" and "negative" instead of "yes" and "no," which can be difficult to hear.
16. Say "Over" when finished and wait for confirmation that the other unit received your message and does not need anything repeated.

### Phone / Cellular Phone Communication
- Treat the content and format of phone or cellular reports similarly to radio communications.
- Know the cellular technology in use and local cellular dead spots, and maintain a backup plan if a cellular transmission fails during a report or agency contact.
- Keep key phone numbers readily accessible, including medical direction, local hospital emergency departments, and dispatch centers.

### Communicating with Dispatch
- **Role of Dispatch & AACN:**
  - Often the first contact on a run (sometimes a certified Emergency Medical Dispatcher [EMD]) and the public's point of contact via 911; gathers information, directs appropriate emergency services to the scene, and instructs callers until help arrives.
  - **Advanced Automatic Collision Notification (AACN):** Standard in many newer vehicles; indicates a crash occurred, provides exact vehicle location, and reports velocity change, direction of force, airbag deployment, rollover, and multiple collisions. Per the 2009 CDC *Guidelines for Field Triage of Injured Patients*, this data helps predict injury severity, air vs. ground transport, and trauma center level prior to EMS arrival.
  - **Legal & Timekeeping Value:** Dispatch often records conversations with callers, police, fire, EMS units, and receiving facilities (which can become part of a legal record in court) and usually provides the time after most transmissions, helping ensure accurate written run times if a lawsuit arises.
- **Nine Key Points to Communicate with Dispatch:**
  1. Acknowledge receipt of dispatched call information.
  2. Advise dispatch when en route to the call.
  3. Estimate scene ETA while en route and report special road conditions or unusual delays.
  4. Announce arrival on scene, request needed additional resources, and help coordinate response (some systems also require reporting when reaching the patient, and dispatch should be contacted during prolonged on-scene time without communication).
  5. Announce departure from the scene along with destination hospital, number of patients transported (if more than one), and ETA at the hospital.
  6. Announce arrival at the receiving hospital/facility or at a rendezvous point.
  7. Announce being "clear" and available for another assignment after transferring the patient.
  8. Announce leaving the hospital and returning to the station.
  9. Announce arrival back at the station.

### Communicating with Health Care Professionals
- **Communicating with Medical Direction:**
  - Consulted for permission to assist with a patient's prescribed medication, orders to administer ambulance-stock medications, procedure guidance, triage support, or directives not to perform certain procedures.
  - *Standard Radio Report Elements to Medical Direction:*
    - Unit identification and service level (BLS or ALS), plus provider identification if desired
    - Patient's age and sex, if known
    - Patient's chief complaint
    - Need for special services (such as trauma team, stroke team, STEMI alert, or specialized center transport)
    - Brief, pertinent history of the present illness, including scene assessment and mechanism of injury
    - Major past illnesses
    - Patient's mental status
    - Patient's baseline vital signs
    - Pertinent physical examination findings
    - Emergency medical care given by you (and others)
    - Patient's response to emergency medical care
    - Patient's current condition
    - Request for further actions/interventions at the receiving facility
    - Estimated time of arrival
  - *Order Verification & Safety Rules:*
    - Repeat orders from medical direction word for word after receiving them—including orders not to do something.
    - If an order is unclear, ask for it to be repeated and then repeat it back word for word.
    - If an order appears inappropriate, question it in case medical direction misunderstood your report or misspoke, helping prevent harmful medications or inappropriate procedures.
  - **SBAR Standardized Format:**
    - **S — Situation:** Problem or reason for calling and the patient's chief complaint.
    - **B — Background:** Concise past medical history and response to treatment so far.
    - **A — Assessment:** Pertinent subjective and objective findings (mental status, vital signs, neurologic findings, blood glucose level, Glasgow Coma Score).
    - **R — Recommendation:** Specific request for the patient (such as an order for another nitroglycerin spray).
    - Standardizes expectations across medical professionals to reduce miscommunication, missed communication, or misunderstanding.
- **Communicating with the Receiving Facility:**
  - Helps doctors, nurses, and staff prepare rooms and equipment, request extra personnel (especially for trauma, stroke, or heart attack patients), and ensure continuity of care.
  - *Pre-Transport Radio Report Elements (When Leaving the Scene):* Includes unit ID/service level, patient age/sex, chief complaint, need for special services (trauma, stroke, STEMI alert), pertinent HPI/scene/MOI, major past illnesses, mental status, baseline vitals, pertinent exam findings, emergency care given (including by EMRs), patient response, current condition, and ETA.
  - *En-Route Updates & Arrival:* Continue reassessing and recording findings during transport; report deterioration or improvement per local protocol, and notify the receiving hospital upon arrival if required by your system.
- **The Oral Report (At Facility Transfer of Care):**
  - Delivered in person when arriving at the facility and transferring the patient to staff, summarizing the earlier broadcast report plus reassessment updates:
    - Patient's chief complaint
    - Vital signs taken en route
    - Treatment given en route and the patient's response
    - Pertinent history not included in the earlier report to the facility
  - Ask if receiving facility personnel need additional information before leaving, provide a copy of the written PCR, and gather any details needed to finish documentation.
- **Transferring Patient Care to Another EMS Provider:**
  - Follow agency policies and protocols when transferring care in the field to another EMT or paramedic.
  - *Verbal Handoff Report Elements:* Current condition, age/sex, chief complaint, brief pertinent history, how the patient was found, major past illnesses, vital signs obtained, pertinent physical exam findings, emergency care given, and patient response.
  - Provide a copy of documentation if requested before leaving the scene; **SBAR** is especially useful during handoffs from EMRs, when requesting orders from medical direction, and during hospital arrival transfer reports.

## 3. Team Communication, Radio Codes & Terminology

### Taking Charge & Scene Dynamics
- **Presence & Authority:** Project confidence from arrival through departure using personal appearance and a professional manner so bystanders, family members, and the patient recognize you are in charge.
- **Alert Patient, No Immediate Life Threats:** If the patient is alert with no apparent life-threatening conditions, it may be appropriate to receive a brief initial update—typically a minute or less—from an Emergency Medical Responder (such as a police officer, relative, or first-aid provider) before making direct patient contact.
- **Unresponsive Patient with Life-Threatening Injuries:** Go directly to the patient and question bystanders while rendering emergency care.
- **Transfer of Authority & Advocacy:**
  - Achieve an orderly transfer of authority when a physician, fire-rescue crew, or police are on scene to avoid disputes during patient care.
  - With rescue or police personnel, state clearly: *"We’re Emergency Medical Technicians. Is there anything we need to know before we provide emergency care?"* to obtain a quick briefing and assume care.
  - With a physician present, state: *"We’re certified EMTs, Doctor. How can we help?"*
  - Always advocate for good, proper patient care and prevent secondary issues from interfering with it.

### Radio Codes & Plain English Shift
- **Potential Benefits:** Can shorten airtime, deliver concise information, and transmit messages not readily understood by patients, family, or bystanders.
- **Key Limitations:** Ineffective unless understood by everyone in the system; medical information is often too complex for codes; infrequently used codes waste time looking up meanings.
- **Ten-Code System & Modern Practice:** Some services still use Ten-Codes (published by the Associated Public Safety Communications Officers [APCO], such as `10-4` meaning *"received and understood"*), primarily in dispatch, while many EMS systems have moved away from codes in favor of standard plain English.

### Military Time & Frequently Used Radio Terms
- **Military Time:** U.S. DOT guidelines require accurate, synchronous clocks, and most systems use 24-hour military time rather than A.M./P.M. in radio and written reports:
  - `1:00 A.M.` to `12 Noon` = `0100` to `1200` hours
  - `1:00 P.M.` to `Midnight` = `1300` to `2400` hours
  - *Examples:* `1427 hours` = `2:27 P.M.`; `0030 hours` = `30 minutes after midnight`
- **Frequently Used Radio Terms:**

| Radio Term | Meaning | Radio Term | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Break** | Afford a "pause" so the hospital can respond or interrupt if needed | **Landline** | Refers to telephone communications |
| **Clear** | End of transmission | **Over** | End of message; awaiting reply |
| **Come in** | Requesting acknowledgment of transmission | **Repeat / say again** | Did not understand message |
| **Copy** | Message received and understood | **Spell out** | Asking sender to spell out phonetically words that are unclear |
| **ETA** | Estimated time of arrival | **Stand by** | Please wait |
| **Go ahead** | Proceed with your message | **10-4** | Acknowledging that message is received and understood |

## 4. Therapeutic Communication & Special Interviewing Situations

### Principles & Process of Communication
- **Definition & Scope:** Communication is a dynamic process combining verbal and nonverbal expressions into meaningful messages sent by one person and interpreted by another—shaped by gender, culture, age, environment, and personal experience.
- **The Communication Loop:**
  1. **Sender & Encoding:** A *sender* creates a message using words and symbols (*encoding* = converting information into a message).
  2. **Receiver & Decoding:** The *receiver* translates and interprets the message (*decoding*) through their own perceptions, values, and experiences; closer alignment between intended and decoded meaning yields more effective communication.
  3. **Feedback:** Any verbal or nonverbal, positive or negative information an individual receives about their behavior. The receiver encodes feedback and sends it back to the original sender in an ongoing loop.

### Nine Therapeutic Communication Responses
1. **Facilitation:** Encourages the patient to share more information (e.g., *"please continue,"* *"go on,"* or *"uh, huh"*).
2. **Clarification:** Asking more questions or asking a patient—such as one too upset to speak clearly—to repeat or clarify a message so communication is not impaired.
3. **Summary:** Rephrasing and repeating back what you interpreted from the patient's message and checking whether it accurately captures their feelings or ideas (asking for clarification if not).
4. **Explanation:** Presenting information in a way the patient can understand—accounting for age and language barriers—explaining what to expect before it happens to build trust and rapport, and watching verbal and nonverbal cues for understanding.
5. **Silence:** Allows time to think, gain insight, or formulate a response (especially when language or other barriers slow answers); therapeutic for some (such as outgoing individuals), though potentially less beneficial for someone coping with depression depending on their needs.
6. **Reflection:** Shows you understand what the patient said or feels by redirecting their feelings and statements back to them in a way that fosters empathy, respect, and understanding.
7. **Empathy:** Recognizing and understanding another person's state of mind or feelings ("putting yourself in the patient's shoes"), even without experiencing the event yourself—especially helpful with an agitated patient.
8. **Confrontation:** Assertively—never angrily or aggressively—addressing discrepancies in a patient's feelings, attitudes, beliefs, or behaviors to help them overcome a crisis and understand themselves, while maintaining respect and acting in their best interest.
9. **Facilitated Communication:** Supporting a patient's hand, wrist, arm, shoulder, or elbow so they can select letters on a letterboard, electronic keyboard, or other communication device (such as for certain developmental or sensory disabilities), without influencing what is communicated.

### Communicating with the Patient & Patient Contact Guidelines
- **High-Emotion Scenes & The Three Cs:** Scenes often involve injured, frightened, anxious, angry, or shocked people; convey **competence**, **confidence**, and **compassion** to improve cooperation and reduce hostile or irrational reactions while staying vigilant with an egress plan and building rapport.
- **Patient Contact Best Practices:**
  - Dress professionally and approach with compassion and confidence.
  - Introduce yourself by the name you want to be called, ask the patient's name and preferred address—erring on the side of formality (e.g., *"Mrs. Lubeck"* or *"Mr. Perez"*) with older adults—and use their name throughout care; introduce your partner and close helpers by name.
  - Ask explicit permission before helping (e.g., *"I’m going to help you. Is that all right?"*) to support consent.
  - Expect initial refusal (*"No!"* or *"I’m okay!"*) from fear, confusion, or **defense mechanisms** (psychological coping strategies protecting against unwanted feelings/thoughts that can block communication), and respond calmly using focus shifts or distraction (e.g., asking about shoulder pain or a worried spouse).
  - Speak clearly, calmly, and slowly in plain everyday language (avoiding medical jargon, codes, and abbreviations), and give orders quietly under stress or medical shock.
  - Use a compassionate, professional tone—raising your voice only for hearing impairment—while avoiding sarcastic, flippant, angry, or unprofessional tones.
  - Protect privacy by limiting bystanders, avoiding broadcasting details to a crowd, and interviewing the patient alone when possible rather than in front of friends or family.
  - Limit interruptions to support active listening, allowing them only when needed for care.
  - Control the physical environment: ensure adequate lighting, reduce outside noise/interference, turn off unneeded noisy equipment, and ask permission before turning off a TV or loud device.
  - Be courteous—explaining actions, offering choices when possible, being honest, and apologizing for necessary discomfort—to give emotionally unstable patients and bystanders a sense of control; give ample time to answer before asking the next question.

### Nonverbal Communication
- Nonverbal communication (body language) operates from the first moment and can convey meaning as strongly as—or stronger than—spoken words; when verbal and nonverbal cues conflict, meaning is drawn primarily from nonverbal expression.
- **Posture:**
  - Approach with open arms, open hands, and relaxed shoulders to signal care, confidence, and safety; avoid crossed arms, pointing fingers, clenched fists, or negative head shakes that signal hostility, anger, disgust, or discomfort.
  - Match the patient's eye level to signal equality; stand above only when asserting authority with a hostile/aggressive patient; position below eye level for children or older adults to increase comfort and security.
- **Distance Zones (American Cultural Norms):**
  - **Intimate Zone (`<1½ feet`):** Ask and explain before entering personal or intimate space for physical assessment, stepping back if met with resistance.
  - **Personal Distance (`1½ to 4 feet`):** Standard distance for verbal patient communication.
  - **Social Distance (`4 to 12 feet`):** Conversational distance without shouting.
  - **Public Distance (`>=12 feet`):** Keep 12 feet or more in public when feasible, adapting to cultural spatial norms and patient reactions.
- **Gestures:** Body movements and facial expressions (winking, smiling, eye rolling, furrowing brows) conveying fear, anxiety, pain, sorrow, relief, or dismay; watch and acknowledge them and offer a warm smile.
- **Eye Contact:** Maintain eye contact to show interest and concern, but adjust if a patient is reluctant because some cultures view direct eye contact as impolite or aggressive.
- **Haptics (Study of Touch):** A sincere hand-hold, shoulder pat, hug, or forearm touch can calm and comfort powerfully, though perception varies by age, gender, culture, and experience—ask permission if you sense reluctance and read eye contact to avoid feeling intrusive.

### Question Types & Interviewing Pitfalls
- **Question Types:** Ask one question at a time in clear, non-jargon language tailored to age and development, allow ample time to answer, and take notes.
  - **Open-Ended Questions:** Invite detailed answers in the patient's own words (e.g., *"What seems to be the problem?"* or *"How are you feeling?"*) to gather rich information whenever possible.
  - **Closed-Ended Questions:** Direct questions seeking specific facts quickly or filling in details (e.g., *"What medications do you take?"* or *"When did the pain begin?"*) to focus and guide the interview.
- **Interviewing Pitfalls to Avoid:**
  - Avoid **leading questions** that nudge a specific answer (e.g., *"So you’re having chest pain. It really hurts doesn’t it?"*), as they distort accuracy and skew questioning.
  - Do not interrupt a speaking patient; look at them while listening and enter notes after they finish speaking.
  - Prevent excessive talking by either party: use more closed questions with overly talkative patients to keep answers concise, and avoid over-talking yourself so you can listen actively.
  - Never give false assurance or lie; stay honest and compassionate while addressing concerns genuinely.
  - Do not give inappropriate advice, diagnose, or discourage prompt hospital evaluation; use your authority responsibly without abusing trust.
  - Avoid blame-implying *"why"* questions (e.g., *"Why did you do that?"*); stay impartial and nonjudgmental.
- **Challenging Interview Situations:**
  - *Family preferences:* Adults with capacity make their own decisions despite family wishes (explain rights empathetically); consider family preferences for minors needing parental consent or incapacitated adults, and explain kindly when clinical needs require a non-preferred facility (such as a trauma center over a smaller local ED).
  - *Unmotivated patient:* Start normally with open-ended questions, confirm understanding, reassure them of your help, use a phone language line for language barriers, shift to closed questions if needed, give positive feedback, and address special needs and cultural factors.
  - *Hostile patient:* Never approach an unsafe scene—wait for law enforcement and follow restraint policies/backup needs if required—then build rapport with a calm, nonthreatening demeanor, explain how cooperation helps, set clear behavioral boundaries with safe alternatives, and proceed with interviewing.

### Special Circumstances & Family-Centered Care
- **Transcultural Considerations:**
  - **Culture** (thoughts, communications, actions, and values of a racial, ethnic, religious, or social group) shapes how people view illness, receive information, and prefer care, including folk medicine practices.
  - Overcome stereotypes and **ethnocentrism** (viewing one's own culture as right/superior and others as inferior) without imposing your beliefs or values; respect cultural differences in personal space, eye contact, and sick-role behavior, and use family/bystanders, dispatch/medical direction, or toll-free interpreter lines when a language barrier exists (noting who interpreted).
- **Elderly Patients:**
  - Allow extra time; watch for hearing or vision changes without assuming all older adults have them, speak slowly, distinctly, and slightly louder if needed, help get glasses or hearing aids, keep lips visible and consider ASL interpreters or written notes for hearing loss, offer help gathering info/medications to prevent fatigue, and treat older adults with full adult respect rather than talking down to them.
- **Children:**
  - Work patiently with parents—keeping parents calm and confident so they do not transfer fear or confusion—get near the child's eye level (crouching or sitting rather than looming), maintain eye contact, speak calmly in simple non-condescending language, and always tell the truth so trust is not broken.
- **Family-Centered Care:**
  - Originated in **1987** under former U.S. Surgeon General C. Everett Koop's initiative for children with special health care needs and championed in EMS by the EMSC program; values family knowledge of the child's condition and communication abilities, relies on understanding growth and development to anticipate physiologic and emotional needs, and promotes open family communication throughout assessment and care.

# Chapter 1: Emergency Medical Service Systems, Research, and Public Health — Rapid Review

## EMS systems and the continuum of care

Emergency medical services (EMS) reduce preventable death and disability through coordinated emergency care, not merely ambulance transportation. Modern EMS connects **recognition → dispatch → first response → BLS/ALS assessment and care → transport → definitive treatment → recovery or rehabilitation**. The effectiveness of prehospital interventions depends partly on access to the rest of this system.

### Historical development

- Korean and Vietnam wartime experience demonstrated the value of treatment before and during transport; Iraq and Afghanistan experiences continue to inform trauma care.
- **1966:** The *White Paper*, *Accidental Death and Disability: The Neglected Disease of Modern Society*, exposed prehospital care deficiencies. The Highway Safety Act required state highway safety programs and led to DOT/NHTSA leadership in EMS curricula.
- **1970s:** Public CPR/BLS education expanded; the 1973 EMS Systems Act funded system development and training.
- **1993–2006:** The NREMT education blueprint, 1996 *EMS Agenda for the Future*, 2000 education agenda, 2005 Core Content and 2006 Scope of Practice Model advanced integration and national consistency. The IOM advocated common scope, paramedic program accreditation and national certification before licensure/credentialing.
- **EMS Agenda 2050:** A people-centered vision for continued integration and improvement of EMS within health care ([NHTSA](https://www.ems.gov/resources/newsletters/may-2019/creating-a-people-centered-ems/)).

## Governance, resources and service models

State and territorial EMS agencies plan, coordinate and regulate their systems and license agencies and personnel. Federal frameworks guide consistency, but states control their system design and regulations.

NHTSA's ten recommended system assessment standards address:

| Standard | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Regulation and policy | Laws, rules and statewide leadership |
| Resource management | Equitable access to acceptable care |
| Human resources and training | Adequately trained ambulance personnel; EMT-level training in the source's recommended standard |
| Transportation | Safe, reliable ground and air transport |
| Facilities | Timely access to appropriate receiving facilities |
| Communications | Public access and coordination between dispatch, crews and hospitals |
| Public information and education | Prevention and appropriate use of EMS |
| Medical direction | Physician oversight and delegation |
| Trauma systems | Coordinated triage, trauma facilities and rehabilitation |
| Evaluation | Continuous measurement and improvement |

Effective systems balance clinical outcomes, service quality, economic efficiency, accountability, improvement and resilience. Services extend beyond 911 transport to prevention, triage, medical first response, transfers, disaster/event coverage, critical care and air transport, specialized response support and community paramedicine.

| Service model | Organizational distinction |
| --- | --- |
| Fire-based | Uses fire department infrastructure; may transport or provide first response only. |
| Municipal (third service) | Separate public EMS agency alongside police and fire. |
| Private | Company-operated; may provide emergency and nonemergency transportation under local rules. |
| Hospital-based | Operated by a hospital; may provide emergency response and interfacility transport. |
| Law enforcement | EMS function within a police organization; uncommon. |
| Public safety officer | Personnel cross-trained in fire, police and EMS duties. |

> [!NOTE]
> **ALS connection:** Administrative model does not establish clinical capability. Tiered systems may dispatch BLS and ALS separately or use mixed-level crews. Know actual credentials, equipment, upgrade/intercept criteria and available specialty resources; coordinate additional help without unnecessary transport delay. Specialty transfers may require expertise beyond ordinary paramedic practice ([ACEP, 2024](https://www.acep.org/amt/newsroom/june-2024/medical-direction-in-critical-care-transport-a-guide-to-an-emergency-physician-s-involvement)).

## EMS access, dispatch and response

911 simplifies public access, permits simultaneous police/fire/EMS dispatch and enables pre-arrival instructions. The **public safety answering point (PSAP)** collects and verifies information and alerts appropriate services; emergency medical dispatchers (EMDs) can guide lifesaving actions such as CPR or bleeding control.

- **Enhanced 911 (E-911):** ANI identifies the calling number; ALI identifies location. Wireless location may be imprecise, and calls near jurisdictional boundaries can reach the wrong PSAP.
- Historical wireless framework: Basic 911 routes calls regardless of subscription; Phase I supplies number and cell-site location; Phase II supplies coordinates with historically specified accuracy of 50–300 m, depending on technology. These are historical requirements, not verified current specifications.
- **VoIP:** The source describes requirements for 911 access, callback number and registered physical location; location information must be accurate.

Dispatch information is a starting point, **not a confirmed diagnosis**. Reassess on arrival and update dispatch regarding actual acuity, access, patient count and resource requirements.

## Provider levels and clinical authorization

The [2019 National EMS Scope of Practice Model, including 2021 changes](https://www.ems.gov/assets/National_EMS_Scope_of_Practice_Model_2019_Change_Notices_1_and-_2_August_2021.pdf) describes four provider levels:

| Level | Role and representative capabilities |
| --- | --- |
| EMR | Immediate lifesaving assistance: CPR/AED, basic ventilation, bleeding control, basic stabilization and emergency childbirth. |
| EMT | BLS assessment, care and transport: EMR skills plus ambulance equipment, oxygen/ventilation support, pulse oximetry, glucose measurement, CPAP and authorized medications. |
| AEMT | BLS plus limited advanced care: IV/IO access, selected parenteral medications, supraglottic airway and waveform capnography. |
| Paramedic | Broader advanced assessment, field impressions, interpretive ECG, manual defibrillation/pacing, endotracheal intubation and expanded pharmacology. |

These are illustrative national model capabilities, not a local permission list. The textbook lists glucose monitoring at AEMT level, but the national model includes it at EMT level. ECG acquisition/transmission may be included at EMT/AEMT level; interpretive 12-lead ECG is a separate paramedic-level skill in the model.

**Education** develops knowledge and skills; **certification** verifies competency; **licensure** grants legal permission; **credentialing** grants system-specific approval. The [National EMS Education Standards (2021; 2025 update)](https://www.ems.gov/projects/ems-education-standards.html) establish entry-level education expectations rather than practice authorization.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Knowledge, national certification, a state license or a higher-level partner's instruction alone does not authorize every procedure. Follow state law, individual credentialing, local protocols and medical direction. Confirm authorization and supervision requirements even when assisting with advanced care.

## Medical oversight and clinical governance

The physician medical director assumes clinical responsibility for the EMS system, establishes medical direction, oversees education and credentialing, and facilitates quality improvement. EMTs provide care as designated agents within applicable legal and system limits.

| Concept | Distinction |
| --- | --- |
| Indirect medical oversight | Routine clinical governance, education, protocols and quality review; constitutes most oversight. |
| Direct medical oversight | Real-time guidance affecting patient care, either **on-line** (radio, phone or video) or **on-scene** (physician physically present). |
| Protocol | Approved framework for triage, treatment, transport and destination, including conditions requiring consultation. |
| Off-line medical direction | Predetermined written guidance allowing authorized care without contacting a physician. |
| Standing order | Protocol component allowing an action without real-time input; some systems use the term specifically for loss of communication. |

The textbook's historical chest-pain example distinguishes standing orders (initial authorized treatment) from on-line orders (permission for additional nitroglycerin when initial treatment does not relieve pain). It is **not a current universal treatment protocol**.

> [!CAUTION]
> **Current-practice clarification:** The [2025 ACS guideline](https://doi.org/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001309) recommends oxygen for ACS with SpO₂ below 90%, not routine oxygen at or above 90%; this is not a universal oxygen target for other illnesses. Assess breathing, distress and reading reliability. Nitroglycerin requires consideration of hemodynamic stability, possible right ventricular infarction, recent PDE-5 inhibitor use and local orders; aspirin requires attention to serious allergy and active bleeding. Neither standing nor on-line orders automatically override scope.

**ALS connection:** In ACS, reduced coronary blood flow produces ischemia. Aspirin inhibits platelet aggregation; nitroglycerin may relieve ischemic discomfort through vasodilation but may lower BP. A 12-lead ECG can reveal ischemia or rhythm disturbances and support cardiac-center activation; neither a normal initial tracing nor symptom relief excludes ACS. EMTs contribute symptom history, vital trends, medications/allergies, authorized care and prompt communication of deterioration.

## Health care resources and destination selection

Prehospital care extends from the scene through transport; out-of-hospital care also includes encounters without transport and interfacility care. The ED provides stabilization, while specialty centers offer capabilities beyond a general ED:

- Trauma: surgical and specialized injury management; burn: complex burns and rehabilitation; spine: severe spinal injuries.
- Cardiac: advanced cardiac intervention; stroke: time-sensitive imaging and treatment.
- Pediatric and obstetric: age- or pregnancy-specific expertise; poison and hyperbaric: selected toxic exposures/diving emergencies; psychiatric: behavioral emergencies.

Select destinations according to acuity, stability, time, regional triage policy and facility capability. The nearest hospital is not always the most appropriate, but bypass is not automatically justified. Consult medical direction when indicated; prenotify the receiving team ([ACS field triage, 2021](https://www.facs.org/quality-programs/trauma/systems/field-triage-guidelines/)).

**Suspected stroke:** Focal deficits or inability to speak may suggest stroke but do not establish its diagnosis or type. Perform an authorized stroke screen and glucose measurement (glucose abnormalities can mimic stroke), identify **last known well**, assess airway protection and vital signs, and report findings. Speech impairment alone does not prove incapacity. ALS may refine severity assessment, but hospital imaging is critical; procedures should not needlessly delay transport. Regional systems determine whether direct thrombectomy-capable transport or a nearer stroke-capable facility is preferable ([AHA/ASA, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1161/STR.0000000000000513)).

## EMT responsibilities and professional practice

The EMT's responsibilities span readiness, scene safety, vehicle operation, assessment, emergency care, safe movement, transport, handoff, documentation, advocacy, emotional support, interagency coordination and professional development.

Professional competence combines knowledge, equipment familiarity, integrity, empathy, judgment, leadership, listening, adaptability, teamwork, time management and clear communication. Maintain hygiene and appropriate appearance, clean/disinfect equipment, understand local geography and traffic laws, and maintain certification/licensure through continuing education, competency verification and renewal requirements. Recognize stress reactions and seek appropriate support.

The ADA (1990) protects against disability-based employment discrimination and provides for reasonable accommodation. Essential job functions and accommodations vary. The source's 125-lb lifting example is neither a universal qualification nor a safe single-person lifting target; use sufficient help and mechanical aids.

## Scene safety, assessment and patient management

**Personal safety comes first.** An injured rescuer cannot provide effective care and creates an additional rescue burden. Identify hazards continuously, use appropriate PPE and lighting, secure a safe treatment area, and coordinate with police, fire and technical rescue personnel. Do not enter uncontrolled violent or hazardous scenes or technical rescue environments without appropriate clearance, training and authorization.

- **Primary assessment:** Identify and address immediately life-threatening airway, breathing, circulation and severe bleeding problems.
- **Secondary assessment:** Identify, stabilize and treat additional injuries or illnesses through examination and history.
- **Reassessment:** Monitor trends and response to care; reassess immediately after relevant interventions or condition changes and at intervals appropriate to acuity and protocol.

### BLS observations and supplemental ALS reasoning

| BLS finding | Potential ALS contribution and rationale |
| --- | --- |
| Ineffective breathing or declining alertness | Capnography/airway assessment: ventilation may be failing despite oxygen delivery. |
| Chest discomfort, syncope or abnormal pulse | ECG acquisition and interpretation may identify ischemia or rhythm disturbance. |
| Cool skin, weak pulses, hypotension or confusion | Shock assessment and possible vascular access: perfusion may be compromised. |
| Altered mental status | Glucose and neurologic evaluation help distinguish overlapping metabolic and neurologic causes. |

These are assessment connections, not automatic treatment pathways. No single monitor or finding establishes a diagnosis ([NASEMSO model guidelines, v3, 2022](https://www.nasemso.org/content.aspx?module_id=701974&page_id=22)).

**Oxygenation** is oxygen entering blood; pulse oximetry estimates saturation. **Ventilation** is air movement and CO₂ removal; waveform capnography displays exhaled CO₂ over the respiratory cycle. Normal SpO₂, especially with supplemental oxygen, does not establish adequate ventilation. Exhaled CO₂ also depends on circulation.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Effective BVM ventilation may be sufficient while advanced options are evaluated. Advanced airways have risks; capnography helps verify and monitor endotracheal tubes in adult arrest. High-quality compressions, timely defibrillation for shockable rhythms and effective ventilation remain fundamental despite availability of IV/IO access, medications or advanced airways. Avoid unnecessary CPR interruptions ([AHA BLS, 2025](https://cpr.heart.org/en/resuscitation-science/cpr-and-ecc-guidelines/adult-basic-life-support); [ALS, 2025](https://cpr.heart.org/en/resuscitation-science/cpr-and-ecc-guidelines/adult-advanced-life-support)).

### Safe movement and trauma reasoning

Select emergency, urgent or nonurgent moves according to safety and clinical need. Use proper mechanics, sufficient personnel and equipment. Assign responsibility for airway, tubing, monitors and lines; reassess the patient and device function after moving.

A substantial fall with thigh deformity and loss of distal sensation/movement illustrates why reassuring initial vital signs and palpable pulses do not exclude major injury or deterioration. Assess distal circulation, sensation and movement before and after stabilization. Control bleeding, protect from heat loss, reassess and coordinate trauma resources. Report neurologic deficits and changes explicitly.

> [!CAUTION]
> Traction splinting is not automatic for suspected femoral injury: consider associated pelvic, knee and lower-extremity injuries, uncertainty about fracture location and safe application. Comparative benefit is uncertain; static stabilization may be preferable ([NAEMSP, 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40334120/)). Use selective, protocol-directed **spinal motion restriction**, minimizing unnecessary time on rigid extrication boards ([joint statement, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1080/10903127.2018.1481476)).

**ALS connection:** Vascular access permits delivery of fluids/medications but does not stop internal hemorrhage. Authorized analgesia may improve comfort without resolving underlying injury; opioids such as fentanyl may depress respiration or consciousness and affect hemodynamics. Reassess pain, breathing, alertness and perfusion. Medication authorization varies, particularly at AEMT level ([prehospital pain guideline, 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34928760/)).

## Transport, handoff, documentation and advocacy

Choose the destination before departure, notify the receiving facility of patient count, severity and anticipated needs, continue assessment and authorized care en route, and communicate changes. Drive safely; lights and sirens are not appropriate for every transport. Restrain occupants whenever feasible; do not leave until care has been properly transferred.

**Handoff:** Communicate patient concern, mechanism/history and onset, relevant findings, initial and latest vitals, interventions with times and responses, medications/allergies and unresolved risks. MIST (mechanism/medical complaint, injuries/information, signs, treatments) is one possible structure. Confirm reception of critical information. Provide both oral and written reports ([joint transfer statement, 2014](https://doi.org/10.3109/10903127.2014.883001)).

The PCR becomes part of the patient's medical and agency records. Complete it promptly, documenting actual observations, times, treatment conditions, responses, communications and transfer of responsibility. Distinguish measured, reported, estimated and unavailable information; missing data are not normal findings.

Advocacy includes protecting dignity, privacy, rights and valuables; documenting property transfer; informing family of destination as appropriate; relaying patient preferences and concerns; explaining care within competence; and maintaining confidentiality. Speak up about safety concerns regardless of who leads clinical care.

## Quality improvement and patient safety

**Continuous quality improvement (CQI)** uses internal/external review, audits and feedback to identify system weaknesses, implement improvements and evaluate outcomes. Its primary purpose is system improvement rather than punishment; unsafe individual conduct may still require accountability.

EMTs contribute through accurate PCRs, feedback, review participation, preventive equipment maintenance, continuing education and skill practice. System review can identify dispatch delays, training needs, communication failures and treatment interruptions. In arrest, evaluate the entire chain from recognition and CPR through defibrillation and outcome, not just advanced procedures.

High-risk activities include handoff, patient movement, ambulance transport and inappropriate spinal motion restriction. Errors arise from inadequate knowledge, poor skill performance, failure to follow rules and communication breakdowns. Reduce risk with clear protocols, labeled medications, lighting, decision aids, fewer interruptions, assistance and questioning assumptions.

> [!TIP]
> **Closed-loop communication:** Assign the task to a specific person, have them repeat the instruction, confirm it, and acknowledge completion/results. Report discrepancies and possible medication errors immediately; do not attempt unauthorized corrective treatment.

## EMS research and evidence-based practice

Evidence-based medicine (EBM) uses valid research to inform clinical decisions rather than relying on tradition or intuition alone. Evidence-based guidelines (EBGs) translate research into recommendations; strength varies with evidence quality. Research helps improve patient outcomes, demonstrate EMS value, protect personnel and improve documentation.

**Scientific method:** Research question → literature review → hypothesis → study → data collection/analysis → support or reject hypothesis → identify limitations → report results.

**Evidence-based decision-making:** Formulate a clinical question, search applicable literature, critically appraise validity/reliability, then revise and implement protocols through authorized processes if evidence supports change. Consider study population, comparison, setting, outcomes and applicability to local practice.

Distinguish evidence (research findings), recommendations (expert judgments), guidelines (clinical frameworks), protocols (approved operational rules), scope/credentialing (limits on practice) and medical direction (clinical oversight). Evidence does not authorize independent protocol changes during a call.

The national prehospital EBG model is cyclical: system inputs → evidence accumulation/evaluation → priorities → guideline development → protocol development → dissemination → implementation → outcomes/QI evaluation → renewed evidence evaluation. The 2001 National EMS Research Agenda, 2014 FICEMS strategic goal and subsequent EBG initiatives promote data-driven EMS; a national pediatric pain project applied the model to guideline development.

- **[NEMSIS](https://nemsis.org/what-is-nemsis/):** Standardizes EMS data for research, benchmarking and system improvement.
- **[CARES](https://mycares.net/sitepages/aboutcares.jsp):** Links dispatch, responder, EMS and hospital data for out-of-hospital cardiac arrest outcomes and survival benchmarks. Registry associations do not alone prove causation.
- EMS crash data identify injury patterns and inform prevention and care strategies.

Every EMT contributes through accurate data collection, research participation and evidence-informed practice. Meaningful clinical outcomes (survival, neurologic function, pain relief and safety) matter more than the number or complexity of procedures performed.

## Public health: mission, functions and prevention

Public health protects and improves the health of **populations** through organized community efforts. Its mission includes preventing disease and injury, promoting physical and mental health, controlling epidemics and environmental hazards, responding to disasters and ensuring accessible, quality health services. Winslow's 1920 definition emphasizes prevention and organized effort; the 1988 IOM mission centers on assuring conditions in which people can be healthy.

| Public health | Individual medicine |
| --- | --- |
| Population health and prevention | Individual diagnosis and treatment |
| Considers behavioral, biological, social, economic, cultural, environmental and psychological influences | Traditionally focuses on problems within the body or mind |
| Surveillance, education, policy and community interventions | Clinical assessment and care |

Both rely on science, overlap in prevention and collaborate on chronic disease, epidemics and other health threats. Government at all levels participates, with much implementation occurring at state/local levels through surveillance, screening, education, vaccination, access to care and sanitation. Public, private and voluntary organizations and individuals form an intersectoral public health system.

### Three core functions and essential services

| Function | Historical essential services |
| --- | --- |
| Assessment | Monitor health; investigate hazards; inform, educate and empower. |
| Policy development | Mobilize partnerships; develop policies and plans. |
| Assurance | Enforce protective laws; link people to care; ensure workforce competency; evaluate access, quality and effectiveness. |
| All three | Research innovative solutions. |

Epidemiology and biostatistics support assessment; administration, policy, behavioral science, education and environmental health contribute across functions. The [CDC's 2020 revision](https://www.cdc.gov/public-health-gateway/php/about/) places **health equity** at the center of the ten essential services; the table reflects the historical framework taught in the source.

Major historical public health achievements include vaccination, safer vehicles/workplaces/food, infectious disease control, reduced coronary and stroke mortality, maternal/infant health, family planning, fluoridation and recognition of tobacco hazards. Historical vaccination achievements should not be interpreted as confirmation of worldwide polio eradication.

Historical U.S. mortality patterns illustrate the changing burden of disease: in 1900 infectious illnesses predominated and life expectancy at birth was 47.3 years; in 2018 heart disease, cancer and unintentional injuries led, with life expectancy 78.4 years. In 2020 life expectancy fell to 77.0 years. These are dated source figures, not current estimates.

### EMS contributions across prevention levels

| Level | Purpose | EMS examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary | Prevent disease or injury before it occurs. | Fall/fire prevention, seat belt/car seat/helmet education, vaccination and health promotion. |
| Secondary | Identify problems early and limit progression or complications. | Screening, surveillance/reporting, public AED access and emergency preparedness (as classified in the source). |
| Tertiary | Manage established illness or injury and reduce disability. | Acute treatment and support for disease management and rehabilitation. |

Public CPR education and AED access connect population prevention to individual resuscitation; AEDs treat shockable cardiac arrest rhythms, not uncomplicated heart attacks with a pulse. Screening/vaccination require program-specific authority, training and oversight ([AHA BLS, 2025](https://cpr.heart.org/en/resuscitation-science/cpr-and-ecc-guidelines/adult-basic-life-support)).

EMS identifies community risks, reports through authorized channels, educates, assists disaster/pandemic response and connects underserved or vulnerable people to appropriate services. Electronic health records and surveillance data can inform public health decisions.

**Social determinants of health (SDOH):** Conditions in which people live, learn, work and age influence health risks and disparities. Five domains are economic stability, education access/quality, health care access/quality, neighborhood/built environment, and social/community context. Medication affordability, transportation barriers and language difficulties may affect patient outcomes. Report objective barriers and use approved referrals; avoid assumptions about noncompliance and protect confidentiality.

## Mobile integrated healthcare and community paramedicine

**Mobile integrated healthcare (MIH)** integrates mobile out-of-hospital services from multiple health care entities with an EMS system. **Community paramedicine (CP)** specifically involves EMS agency personnel integrated with other health care entities.

MIH-CP broadens traditional emergency response through patient education, preventive visits, chronic disease management, post-discharge follow-up, access for underserved populations, support for frequent users or patients at risk of readmission, and program-authorized telephone advice or alternative care pathways.

> [!NOTE]
> Community paramedicine is not simply routine ALS. Specially trained clinicians collaborate with primary care, nursing, behavioral health and social services. EMTs may assist with authorized observations, education and referrals. Entry-level licensure does not automatically confer specialty credentials, and frequent EMS use or a scheduled visit does not exclude an acute emergency ([NAEMT MIH-CP](https://naemt.org/resources/mih-cp)).
